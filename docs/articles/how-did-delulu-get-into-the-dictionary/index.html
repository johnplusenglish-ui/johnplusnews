Core version

Imagine a teacher says, "That is not a real word." But you and your friends use that word every day. Is the teacher right?

In August 2025, the Cambridge Dictionary added some new words from the internet. Two of them are "delulu" and "skibidi". "Delulu" is short for "delusional". It is a playful, informal word that describes someone who believes things that are not realistic, often because they want them to be true. "Skibidi" is a slang word that can mean "cool" or "bad". It became popular because of a YouTube series called Skibidi Toilet.

How does a word get into a dictionary? The experts who write dictionaries are called lexicographers. They watch how words are used in books, in newspapers and online. They look for words that people keep using, not only for a week or a month. When they decide that a word has staying power, they can add it.

Slang is often short-lived. Many slang words are popular for a short time and then disappear, so editors must be careful. In the last twelve months, the Cambridge Dictionary added more than 6,000 words. Many other words did not get in. Words can also leave a dictionary when people stop using them.

A dictionary is a record of how people use words. It is not a list of the only real words. A writer for Cambridge University Press says that if a word is not in one dictionary, that does not make it wrong or fake.

Many dictionary entries show when a word is informal. Informal words are fine with friends. In an essay or a job interview, you might choose different words. So which word would you add to a learner's dictionary? What evidence would you collect first?

Intermediate version

Imagine a teacher tells you, "That is not a real word." The problem is that you and your friends use it every day, in messages, in games and in jokes. Who is right?

In August 2025, the Cambridge Dictionary added a handful of internet slang words to its pages. Two of them were "delulu", short for "delusional", and "skibidi", a slangy way of saying "cool" or "bad" that became popular through the YouTube series Skibidi Toilet. Both words are informal, and they are mostly used for fun.

A word does not get in just because it is trending. Dictionary editors, called lexicographers, closely monitor how words are used in books, newspapers and online. According to a Cambridge University Press article by the linguist Karen Stollznow, they decided that these words showed staying power, which means that people were likely to keep using them.

Slang makes that decision difficult, because it is often fleeting. Stollznow writes that slang frequently fails to survive the vetting process. The dictionary added more than 6,000 words in the previous twelve months, but many more candidates never made it in. Words can also be removed. An older example is "younker", a word that was once used for a young person, which was retired because it fell out of use.

Stollznow also stresses that a word's absence from a particular dictionary does not make it any less real or valid. A dictionary is a record of how a language is used, not a list of permissions. Definitions are reviewed too, because the meaning of a word can change over time.

A dictionary can also tell you when a word is informal. "Delulu" might be perfect in a message to a friend, but you might choose a different word in a school presentation or a message to a relative. Choosing words for your audience is part of knowing a language, and a dictionary entry is not a recommendation to use slang everywhere.

So imagine you were an editor for a learner's dictionary. Which word would you add, and what evidence would you collect first? How many people use it, for how long, and in what kinds of text? Those are exactly the questions that lexicographers ask.

Advanced version

There is a moment, familiar to anyone who has used slang, when a word stops belonging only to you. A teacher says it, a parent says it, or it appears in print. In August 2025, that moment arrived on a large scale when the Cambridge Dictionary added internet slang, including "delulu" and "skibidi", to its pages.

"Delulu" is short for "delusional", and "skibidi" is a slangy way of saying "cool" or "bad" that was popularised by the viral YouTube series Skibidi Toilet. Cambridge University Press published an explanation by the linguist Karen Stollznow, who described how editors reached the decision. Lexicographers, the people who compile dictionaries, closely monitor how words are used in books, newspapers and online, and in these cases it was clear that the words showed staying power.

Slang is a difficult case, because it is often fleeting. Stollznow writes that slang frequently fails to survive the vetting process. The dictionary welcomed more than 6,000 words in the previous twelve months, so being added is a real filter, not a formality.

The filter works in both directions. Words can also be removed when they fall out of use, as happened with "snollygoster", once a term for an unscrupulous politician. Removal is not taken lightly: it involves a careful review of a word's usage and popularity, just as adding one does.

Stollznow's most useful point is that a word's absence from a dictionary does not make it any less real. The reverse is also true: inclusion does not make a word correct everywhere, or good, or permanent. A dictionary describes; it does not give orders. Entries are reviewed over time, and labels such as informal can be added or changed, so the same word may be presented differently in the next edition.

That leaves the question in the headline. Part of the pleasure of slang is that it signals membership of a group, and a dictionary entry makes a word easier for outsiders to understand. Some users may find that spoils the fun; others will carry on using it until another expression comes along. The entry records use rather than deciding who gets to say the word.

Meanwhile, the practical lesson concerns audience. The same word can sound playful in a chat, strange in a presentation and risky in a job interview. Being skilled in a language means knowing which version suits which room. A dictionary entry tells you what a word means and, often, how informal it is. It cannot tell you who is listening.
