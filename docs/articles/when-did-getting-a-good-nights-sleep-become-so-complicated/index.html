Core version

You wake up feeling refreshed. Then you look at your smartwatch. It gives you a poor sleep score. Suddenly, you begin to wonder whether you slept badly after all. Can technology make sleep more stressful than it needs to be?

Smartwatches and sleep apps can estimate how long we sleep and how often we wake up. Some also show numbers for different stages of sleep. These tools can be interesting, especially for people trying to understand their routines. But the numbers are not always perfectly accurate.

In 2017, sleep researchers described a problem they called orthosomnia. They had seen patients becoming so worried about their sleep-tracker results that they focused on trying to achieve perfect scores. The term describes a pattern of behaviour discussed in clinical reports; it is not an official diagnosis for everyone who uses a watch.

Good sleep is important, but it is not something we can completely control. People sometimes wake up during the night or sleep badly before an important day. Worrying about every minute can make it harder to switch off at bedtime.

Sleep trackers may still be useful. They can help us work out our habits, such as going to bed at very different times. However, an app cannot always tell us how we feel. Someone who is concerned about ongoing sleep problems should speak to a healthcare professional rather than depend only on a score.

One reason sleep scores are attractive is that we like clear answers. If our watch says 85 out of 100, we know whether that is better than yesterday. But our bodies are not machines that work exactly the same way every night. Some people need more sleep than others, and busy days, travel or worries can change how we sleep.

A person may feel tired after a night with a good score, or surprisingly energetic after a night with a lower one. These differences do not always mean the technology has failed. The score is simply one estimate from one device. We can pay attention to useful patterns without taking every number personally. Sleep should help us live our lives, not become another thing to worry about all day.

Perhaps the best question in the morning is not 'How did my watch rate me?' It is 'How do I actually feel?' Sleep is necessary for life, not a competition that we have to win every night.

Intermediate version

Going to bed used to involve a pillow, a blanket and the hope of waking up refreshed. Today, a smartwatch may analyse the night and deliver a score before breakfast. For some people, the information is helpful. For others, it creates a new source of worry: what if they are not sleeping well enough?

A sleep score might look precise, but it is still an estimate produced by a device. Seeing a low number can make someone feel disappointed before they have even got out of bed.

Researchers described the term orthosomnia in a 2017 article in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. They reported patients who became preoccupied with the apparently imperfect sleep recorded by wearable devices. Some trusted their tracker data so strongly that they questioned their own experience of feeling rested. These were particular patients described by clinicians, not a picture of everyone who wears a tracker.

The technology has improved, but sleep is still difficult to measure precisely outside a clinical setting. Consumer devices estimate sleep stages and duration using signals such as movement and heart rate. Their reports can be useful for spotting patterns, yet they are not equivalent to a medical sleep assessment.

The difficulty is that people cannot simply decide to sleep perfectly. Trying to control every detail may turn bedtime into a stressful routine. Someone who checks a score immediately after waking might begin the day worrying about a problem they would otherwise not have noticed. That does not mean technology has no value. A tracker might help people spot patterns, provided they remember that feeling rested matters more than winning a competition against yesterday's statistics.

There are advantages to monitoring, particularly when it encourages consistent routines or helps someone explain symptoms to a healthcare professional. The danger creeps in when the measurement becomes more important than sleep itself. Persistent difficulty sleeping deserves proper assessment, regardless of whether an app considers the night successful.

There is also a psychological reason these scores feel persuasive. A precise number creates an impression of certainty, even when the measurement involves estimation and interpretation. Someone who sees an apparently poor result may start noticing ordinary tiredness more carefully and connecting it to the score. Another person could become unusually confident about their sleep because the device offers a positive rating.

Neither response necessarily reflects what a clinician would conclude. Healthy tracking therefore involves understanding uncertainty and resisting the temptation to interpret one night in isolation. It can be useful to look out for broad patterns over time, particularly when those patterns are discussed with a professional. But a number should never be allowed to replace the person's symptoms, circumstances and experience.

Perhaps we should treat sleep technology like other health tools: useful when it informs a reasonable decision, unhelpful when it creates endless pressure. Rest should be something we experience, not merely a number we try to improve.

Advanced version

The watch has judged your night's sleep before you have decided how you feel. It gives you a score, estimates your sleep stages and suggests what needs work. That information can be useful. For some people, though, the effort to get a better score becomes another reason to worry at bedtime.

In 2017, clinicians writing in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine introduced the term orthosomnia to describe patients whose pursuit of ideal sleep-tracker measurements appeared to interfere with their relationship with sleep. Their observations were case reports, not evidence that tracking routinely causes insomnia or that orthosomnia is a universally recognised medical diagnosis.

The concern arises from a gap between consumer measurements and clinical interpretation. Wrist-worn devices infer sleep characteristics from physiological signals, but estimates of individual sleep stages can be imperfect. If each displayed number is taken at face value as an authoritative verdict, normal variations may start to look like signs of failure. Numbers can inform a conversation with a clinician without becoming a diagnosis in themselves.

This creates an unusual feedback loop. A person wakes feeling reasonably well, sees a disappointing score and begins monitoring their tiredness for evidence that the device was correct. The following evening, pressure to perform well may make relaxation more difficult. The original purpose of tracking, to understand and improve rest, risks becoming secondary to achieving a result.

Yet dismissing sleep technology entirely would be excessive. Tracking can reveal irregular bedtimes and provide useful information for a discussion with a clinician. The distinction lies in whether measurements inform judgement or replace it. Persistent sleep problems require assessment based on symptoms and appropriate medical evidence, not just a consumer algorithm. The wider paradox is that an attempt to control sleep may introduce the very vigilance that makes rest difficult. Sometimes better habits require stepping away from the dashboard.

The appearance of precision gives consumer sleep scores an authority that their underlying methods may not justify. A number displayed with apparent precision can conceal uncertainty about when a person was awake or how accurately particular sleep stages were identified. Users may nevertheless interpret small nightly changes as meaningful evidence of success or failure. This illustrates a broader problem with quantified self-monitoring: measurement can alter the experience being measured.

Concern about the result may influence how a person approaches bedtime, while retrospective interpretation of fatigue may become anchored to the device's judgement. Rather than treating tracking as either a breakthrough or a threat, a proportionate approach asks whether the information leads to useful action. If monitoring primarily creates anxiety, its practical value deserves reconsideration.

The deeper issue is cultural. We increasingly quantify ordinary human experiences, from exercise to productivity and recovery. Numbers can sharpen awareness, but they also invite comparison and perfectionism. Good sleep is essential; a perfect nightly score is not. Sometimes the healthiest response to an imperfect graph may be to put the device down and carry on with the day.

Source: Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine
