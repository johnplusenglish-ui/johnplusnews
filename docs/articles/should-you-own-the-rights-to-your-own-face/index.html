Core version

Imagine opening your phone and watching a video of yourself saying something terrible. You know you never said it, and the video was never recorded. Someone has used artificial intelligence to make up a fake version of your face and your voice. What could you do about it?

This kind of video is called a deepfake. AI can now create realistic pictures, sound recordings and moving images in a few minutes. Some are made for jokes or films, but others are used to embarrass, deceive or hurt people. The real problem is that ordinary viewers cannot always tell the difference between a fake and a genuine recording.

In October 2026, Denmark announced plans for a law to protect people against certain AI copies of their appearance and voice. The proposal would make it easier to challenge the unauthorised sharing of realistic digital imitations, in other words copies that were shared without permission. It was a proposed law, not something that had already solved the problem.

Supporters say people should be able to control how others use their faces. A fake video can damage somebody's reputation, get them into trouble at work or make friends and family believe something untrue. Ordinary people may be affected too, not only celebrities and politicians, and most of them have no lawyers to fall back on. Once a fake video has spread, it is also very hard to take it down again.

However, the issue is complicated. Films, jokes and political cartoons have always copied famous people, and that is part of free speech. It is important to protect artistic freedom and public discussion while stopping harmful fakes. Laws also have to work when a video is made in one country and shared in another, which is not easy to arrange.

The internet made it easy to copy photographs. AI now makes it possible to invent actions and speech as well, so a picture is no longer proof that something happened. Denmark's proposal asks a simple but very difficult question: if somebody can create a convincing digital version of you, who should decide what that version is allowed to do?

Intermediate version

Your face used to be difficult to copy convincingly. A skilled actor, make-up artist or video editor might imitate you, but the result was rarely good enough to pass off as the real thing. Artificial intelligence has changed that. It is now possible to produce realistic footage or audio of a person saying things they never said, which raises concerns about reputation, consent and personal identity.

In October 2026, Denmark announced plans to bring in legislation addressing the unauthorised sharing of AI-generated copies of people's appearance and voices. The proposed rules would strengthen an individual's ability to challenge convincing digital impersonations. At the time of the announcement, the legislation was still a proposal rather than an established legal solution, and the detail had yet to be worked out.

Deepfakes can be used for entertainment, but the technology also creates serious risks. A fabricated video may spread false statements, humiliate a private individual or chip away at public trust in genuine evidence. Once an image has been shared widely, taking down every copy becomes extremely difficult, and the original clip tends to outlive any correction.

Supporters of the Danish approach argue that control over your own face ought to be a basic right rather than a privilege reserved for the famous. A convincing fake can cost an ordinary person a job, a friendship or simply their peace of mind. Very few private individuals have the time, money or contacts to chase every copy across the internet.

There are challenges in deciding where protection should begin and end. A realistic fake designed to deceive is different from an obvious cartoon or a clearly labelled comedy sketch. Journalists, artists and campaigners may have perfectly legitimate reasons to criticise public figures through parody. Laws must be precise enough to protect personal dignity without blocking ordinary expression, and drawing that line is a well-known sticking point in every country that has tried.

Enforcement presents another difficulty. Images can be generated cheaply and shared across national borders within minutes, while some platforms respond to complaints far more quickly than others. Even working out who was responsible for a harmful fake can be impossible, which makes it hard to crack down on the people doing the most damage. A law that nobody can enforce may still shift behaviour, but it will not reassure the person whose face has been borrowed.

Denmark's proposal reflects a wider change in how people think about privacy. The concern is no longer only who may photograph you, but who may invent a convincing version of you and send it out into the world. In an age of increasingly powerful AI tools, control over personal identity may end up mattering as much as control over personal information.

Advanced version

A video appears to show you saying something you never said. The face looks right. The voice sounds familiar. But no camera recorded it: someone made it with AI. Denmark's proposal to give people more control over copies of their face and voice starts with that unsettling possibility.

Denmark responded in October 2026 by announcing proposed legislation aimed at limiting the unauthorised distribution of realistic AI-generated reproductions of individuals' faces and voices. Its significance lies in treating a person's digital likeness as something deserving specific protection, rather than as an incidental by-product of being photographed or recorded in public. Whether the eventual text can match that ambition is another matter. The proposal should not, however, be described as a law already in force.

The potential harms extend well beyond embarrassing online jokes. Fabricated recordings can be used to impersonate people, circulate humiliating material, damage professional reputations or manipulate public debate at a decisive moment. Even once the falsehood has been exposed, the original impression often survives in screenshots, forwarded messages and private groups, where no correction ever reaches it. Damage of that kind is cumulative rather than dramatic, and it is correspondingly hard to measure or compensate.

Advocates of the Danish approach present control over one's likeness as a right rather than a privilege of the well-resourced. The argument carries particular force for private individuals, who seldom command the legal advice, publicity or platform contacts that allow a public figure to mount a rapid correction. For them, a single persuasive clip can reach a workplace or a family group long before anyone can intervene.

A workable legal framework must nevertheless distinguish harmful impersonation from legitimate expression. Satire, journalism, artistic adaptation and clearly identifiable fictional performances may all involve portraying real individuals without their direct participation. An excessively broad restriction risks becoming a convenient tool for suppressing criticism, particularly when powerful public figures are the ones complaining. Few legislative tasks are more delicate than drawing that line and making it hold.

Practical enforcement is equally challenging. Online content crosses borders almost instantly, while the technical systems designed to flag up manipulated media remain imperfect and are easily outpaced. Taking a video down from one platform achieves little if copies continue circulating elsewhere. Legal remedies may therefore need to be combined with better platform policies and a more sceptical public understanding of what digital evidence can and cannot prove.

The Danish initiative illustrates a genuine shift in the meaning of privacy. It is no longer merely a question of keeping existing personal information secret. It is also about preventing others from manufacturing a persuasive identity on somebody's behalf and setting it loose. As synthetic media becomes commonplace, societies will have to decide how much control individuals ought to retain over their own digital doubles, and who should bear the cost when that control fails. The answer will shape not only privacy law but the everyday business of believing what we see.
