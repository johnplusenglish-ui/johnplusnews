Core version

Imagine your boss saying, 'From next month, you can have Fridays off and take home the same pay.' It sounds wonderful. But what would happen to the work that normally gets done on Friday? In 2025, researchers published the results of a large international four-day week trial. It involved 2,896 employees at 141 organisations in six countries, including Ireland and the UK. The participating workplaces tried shorter working weeks without reducing employees' pay. This meant fewer working hours, not the same hours squeezed into four days.

Before the trial, companies looked for ways to work more efficiently. They cut down on unnecessary meetings and changed how tasks were organised. After six months, employees reported improvements in job satisfaction, mental health and physical health. The researchers also found less burnout compared with a group of workplaces that did not introduce the same change.

A shorter week may give people more time for family, exercise, rest or hobbies. It could also reduce the feeling that life is only about work. But not every job can be organised in the same way. Hospitals, shops and public transport still need enough staff every day.

There are other questions too. Can businesses keep serving customers properly? Would people feel pressure to complete five days of work in four? Could some employers afford extra staff? The study gives useful evidence but does not answer every problem.

For workers, a free Friday might have many uses. Parents could collect children from school or spend more time with them. Others might take a class, exercise or help an older family member. But not everyone would receive an entirely free day. Shops, clinics and restaurants might need staff on more flexible schedules so that the business stays open.

A four-day week does not have to mean the same day off for everybody. Some teams could work different days, while others might close for one extra day. The important point is whether the change genuinely reduces working time without making people rush through every task. If a shorter week simply means four very stressful days, employees might not feel the benefits that supporters promise.

The four-day week may be possible for many workplaces, especially when they improve how they operate. It is not a magic plan for every job. Still, the research encourages a bigger question: if we can work differently, why should a five-day week always be the normal choice?

Intermediate version

What would you do with an extra day off every week? Learn a language, see friends or simply recover from work? For many employees, the idea of receiving the same salary for a four-day week sounds almost too good to be true. Yet a large international study suggests it may be a realistic option for some organisations.

Imagine finishing on Thursday with a whole extra weekday ahead of you. You might finally sort out appointments, spend time with family or simply wake up without an alarm.

Research published in Nature Human Behaviour in July 2025 followed 2,896 employees. They worked at 141 organisations in Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the UK and the United States. Companies introduced shorter working weeks without cutting pay, following a period in which they tried to improve efficiency and cooperation.

Over six months, participants reported improvements in job satisfaction, burnout, mental health and physical health. These changes were not seen in the same way at twelve comparison organisations that maintained their usual schedules. The results suggest that reducing working time can support wellbeing, rather than merely shifting the same problems into a different timetable. The organisations had chosen to take part, so the results may not apply to every workplace.

However, the changes required planning. Businesses had to reconsider meetings, communication and responsibilities. There is also an important difference between genuinely reducing weekly hours and squeezing forty hours into four exhausting ten-hour days. Employees need a realistic workload, not just a new timetable and an expectation to catch up at night.

Certain industries may face greater difficulties. Care workers, transport staff and retailers provide services across the week, and reducing hours could mean taking on additional employees. Small businesses may also struggle if customer demand and staffing costs remain unchanged.

A four-day week would also change how working and family responsibilities are organised. An extra day away from paid employment might allow parents to spend more time with children or carers to support relatives. For other employees, it could mean recovery, education or greater participation in community life. These benefits are difficult to capture through a narrow measure of company productivity.

On the other hand, businesses operating across seven days may need staggered shifts, while smaller organisations could face higher staffing costs. A successful policy would need to distinguish reduced hours from compressed schedules and consider workers who already have limited control over shifts. The debate is therefore about the distribution of time as well as the efficiency of work.

The study does not prove that every employer can increase productivity while reducing time at work. Nevertheless, it provides substantial evidence that the traditional five-day schedule is not the only practical model. The real question is how companies can distribute work fairly while protecting services, salaries and employees' health.

Advanced version

A free Friday sounds simple. Making it work is harder: meetings, shifts and customer needs still have to fit somewhere. Four-day-week trials ask whether teams can change how they work, rather than merely rush five days of tasks into four. The answer matters as much as the longer weekend.

In July 2025, a study published in Nature Human Behaviour examined a six-month reduction in working hours across 141 organisations and 2,896 employees in six countries. Participants retained their pay while workplaces first reorganised tasks and collaboration. Compared with twelve control companies, the participating organisations reported improvements in burnout, job satisfaction and measures of mental and physical wellbeing.

The detail of the intervention matters. This was not simply an instruction to deliver a conventional week's workload in four compressed days. Organisations examined their processes, often doing away with unnecessary meetings or changing workflows. Individuals who reduced their hours more substantially tended to experience greater wellbeing gains, suggesting that time recovered outside work is an important part of the mechanism. A four-day arrangement also depends on what actually changes. Compressing forty hours into four exhausting shifts is a fundamentally different proposition from reducing weekly hours without cutting pay.

Nevertheless, enthusiasm should be tempered by limitations. Participating organisations were willing to experiment, and their experiences may not translate directly to every sector. Productivity outcomes are also more complicated to establish than reported job satisfaction. A successful software company's approach cannot automatically be applied to emergency services, hospitality or a small retailer operating seven days a week.

There are questions about inequality. Professional employees may have greater flexibility than people providing continuous public-facing services. Unless additional staffing and costs are addressed, a four-day week could offer generous benefits to some workers while leaving others with intensified responsibilities. A sensible assessment must therefore look beyond enthusiastic testimonials. It should examine service coverage, staffing costs, the distribution of benefits and whether improvements persist once the trial has ended.

The potential consequences extend beyond company performance. Additional non-working time could affect unpaid care, education, volunteering and the division of domestic responsibilities. Those possibilities are important because a reduction in formal working hours does not automatically produce leisure for everybody. People with caring duties may simply undertake more unpaid labour, while staff on precarious contracts may worry that fewer hours mean lower income.

The design of a four-day week therefore matters at least as much as its headline promise. Preserving pay, ensuring predictable schedules and preventing workload intensification would be central to judging whether the model represents genuine progress. Research on early adopters provides a valuable starting point, but rolling the model out more widely would require attention to sectors and workers whose circumstances differ substantially from those in voluntary trials.

The research is therefore neither a universal prescription nor an excuse to write off shorter working hours as unrealistic. It demonstrates that a different arrangement can work under particular conditions. The challenge now is identifying which aspects are transferable, who bears the costs and whether reduced working time can become a broadly shared benefit rather than an employment privilege.

Source: Nature Human Behaviour
