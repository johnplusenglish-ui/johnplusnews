Why the Evening Decision Feels Harder Than the Day's Work

Core version

You have spent the day at home answering emails, paying bills and sorting things out. You have hardly walked anywhere. Yet by the evening, even choosing a film seems difficult. Why are you so tired? Mental work uses energy too. We need to concentrate, remember information and make decisions. When we do these things for a long time, our minds can start to feel exhausted. This feeling is often called mental or cognitive fatigue.

In a 2025 study published in the Journal of Neuroscience, researchers asked people to do demanding thinking tasks. Afterwards, participants were less willing to choose a task that needed more mental effort, even when it offered a bigger reward. The study involved 28 people, so we should not treat it as an explanation for all tiredness.

The research suggests that mental fatigue can affect what we feel ready to do next. After a difficult day, cooking dinner or organising tomorrow's plans may seem much harder than usual. This does not mean we are lazy. It means our energy and willingness to concentrate may have changed.

Stress, poor sleep, illness and many other things can also cause tiredness. Feeling exhausted for weeks without a clear reason should not automatically be blamed on too many decisions. It may be important to speak to a healthcare professional.

Mental tiredness can also make ordinary choices feel surprisingly important. Imagine arriving home hungry after a busy day. You have several meals you could cook, but choosing one seems difficult. You may end up ordering food or eating the easiest thing available. This does not mean your brain cannot make decisions. It may mean you do not feel ready for another demanding task.

People sometimes find that planning meals in advance or sharing out household jobs makes evenings easier. However, turning every part of life into a strict schedule can also create pressure. Rest can include enjoyable activities that do not ask too much of us. And if feeling tired becomes a regular problem, it is sensible to look beyond a busy day for possible causes.

Short breaks, reasonable workloads and time away from demanding tasks may help some people. But there is no magic solution for everybody. What the research reminds us is simple: a busy mind can feel tired even when the body has spent most of the day sitting down.

Intermediate version

At the end of a day filled with emails, meetings and small decisions, you may feel too exhausted to do anything enjoyable. Yet when somebody asks what you have been doing, it can seem difficult to explain. There was no heavy lifting or long journey, just hours of thinking. Why does mental work leave us feeling drained?

After a day of difficult conversations, paperwork and small decisions, someone may feel worn out despite barely moving. It does not mean their tiredness is imaginary or that exercise is pointless.

Researchers describe this experience as cognitive fatigue. In a 2025 Journal of Neuroscience study, 28 participants performed demanding tasks before making decisions about further mental effort and possible rewards. After becoming fatigued, they were less willing to choose more demanding options, even when those options could bring greater benefits.

The researchers also used brain imaging to investigate how mental exertion might influence the value people attach to future effort. Their work suggests that fatigue can change our willingness to take on difficult tasks. It does not mean that the brain has simply run out of energy like a phone with an empty battery.

Everyday life contains plenty of possible sources of mental strain. Someone may need to organise a family, deal with a difficult customer or manage financial uncertainty while completing ordinary work. A day can involve constant attention and emotional pressure even when there is little physical activity. The challenge is recognising when ordinary tiredness has become a warning sign. If exhaustion persists, changes suddenly or affects daily life, it's sensible to look beyond productivity advice and seek medical guidance.

There are important limits to this explanation. The experiment was relatively small, and persistent tiredness has many possible causes, including health conditions, sleep problems and psychological distress. No study of decision-making should be used to diagnose a person's exhaustion.

The relationship between mental effort and everyday decisions becomes especially visible in the evening. After hours of concentrating, planning dinner or organising the following day can seem less attractive than choosing the easiest available option. This does not mean that a fixed supply of decision-making energy has been used up; that popular explanation is too simple.

Rather, fatigue may change how costly an additional task feels compared with its benefits. People sometimes respond by simplifying routine decisions, taking genuine breaks or sharing responsibilities at home. Yet such strategies have limits when the real problem is excessive demands or poor sleep. Being tired should not automatically become another personal efficiency challenge that requires a complicated system to manage.

Still, the findings challenge the idea that only physical activity can justify feeling tired. Mental demands are real demands. Recognising them may help people build breaks into their schedules, share responsibilities and take recovery seriously rather than dismissing exhaustion simply because they have been sitting down.

Advanced version

You have been sitting for most of the day, yet choosing dinner feels like one decision too many. The work was not visible in tired legs or heavy lifting. It was in the calls, the concentration and the problems that kept arriving. A day can be demanding without involving much movement.

A 2025 study in the Journal of Neuroscience explored how cognitive exertion shapes subsequent choices. Researchers used functional brain imaging while 28 participants completed demanding mental tasks and chose between options involving different levels of effort and reward. Following the fatiguing tasks, participants became less willing to pursue options requiring greater cognitive effort. The findings suggest that fatigue alters how future mental demands are evaluated.

That is more nuanced than the popular metaphor of an exhausted brain 'running out of fuel'. The research concerned the interaction between mental exertion and decision-making, not a simple depletion of a fixed daily energy supply. The small sample also limits how broadly its conclusions can be applied. The distinction matters because fatigue is a symptom, not a single diagnosis. Sleep, illness, medication, emotional strain and lifestyle can all contribute, sometimes in overlapping ways.

Nevertheless, the experiment connects with experiences of contemporary work. People frequently switch between analytical tasks, emotional interactions and minor administrative decisions. Each activity may appear manageable in isolation, while the cumulative demand can make further concentration unattractive. Stress and uncertainty can add another layer of strain, even without producing obvious physical effort.

It would be a mistake, however, to attribute persistent exhaustion automatically to cognitive fatigue. Sleep deprivation, medication, illness and mental health difficulties can cause similar symptoms. When tiredness is severe, unexplained or prolonged, clinical assessment may be appropriate rather than simply experimenting with productivity techniques. Instead of treating every exhausted person as insufficiently disciplined, we might ask what demands they have been carrying and whether the pace of their lives is sustainable.

There are implications beyond individual wellbeing. Employers and institutions frequently evaluate effort according to visible hours, completed tasks or physical demands. Cognitive work is harder to observe, and emotionally difficult interactions can be brushed aside as routine if they leave no tangible product. Recognising mental fatigue may therefore require organisations to reconsider scheduling, break policies and the expectation that attention can be sustained indefinitely.

At the same time, fatigue should not be used as an all-purpose explanation for persistent symptoms. Its causes are diverse, and the existence of a plausible cognitive mechanism does not rule out illness, medication effects or sleep disorders. A nuanced understanding allows us to take mental demands seriously without converting a single laboratory finding into a universal diagnosis.

The wider lesson concerns how we value effort. Better workloads, reasonable boundaries and genuine recovery may matter as much for people doing demanding mental work as they do for those performing physically strenuous jobs.

Source: Journal of Neuroscience
