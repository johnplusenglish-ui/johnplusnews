Core version

Imagine a video that begins like this: an influencer makes breakfast, looks at her phone and smiles. Another payment has arrived from a business she says almost runs itself. There is no boss, no journey to work, and money arrives while she sleeps. It is easy to see why such a video is attractive when rent and food are getting more expensive. But the camera shows the payment, not the hours spent designing, advertising and searching for the first customer.

A side hustle is work you do alongside your job or studies. It might involve tutoring, walking dogs, selling second-hand clothes or running a small online shop. These can be useful ways to earn extra cash.

The problem begins when people suggest that making money takes almost no effort. Some talk about passive income, which means money that keeps arriving after the first work is finished. But somebody still has to make the product, answer customers and find new buyers. Setting up a shop is one thing; finding customers is another. Yet many people are trying because they want more control over their money and working lives.

The figures tell a less exciting story. In a 2026 Save the Student survey, 29% of students said they had a business or side hustle. Of those, 79% had earned less than £500 in the previous year. Some gained useful skills or enough money to cover small expenses. But those earnings are far below the thousands promised in some videos.

Take digital products, such as planners or invitations sold online. You don't need to store boxes or post parcels, but you still need good designs and customers willing to pay for them. There are already thousands of similar products available. Software, advertising and selling fees can also reduce your profits, particularly when you are just starting out. And good reviews rarely appear overnight when nobody knows you.

Affiliate marketing is another popular option. People earn a small payment when someone buys a product using their special link. This can work if followers trust their recommendations. However, sharing a link doesn't mean people will buy anything. Some influencers make their own money by selling expensive courses that promise to teach others how to succeed.

None of this means side hustles are a bad idea. They can build confidence, teach business skills and sometimes turn into full-time work. Before starting, ask yourself: Who will buy what I'm selling? How much will it cost? What will I actually earn after expenses? Making money online is possible. Doing it without working hard is much less likely.

Intermediate version

The video begins with coffee and a payment notification. Its creator says a small online business covers the bills while leaving plenty of time for family, travel and sleep. What the camera does not show is the crowded marketplace, the advertising costs or the weeks when nobody buys anything. For anyone squeezed by the cost of living, the promise is powerful. The missing work is exactly what deserves attention.

A side hustle is simply an activity that brings in income alongside a job or studies. Some people tutor languages, repair bicycles or walk dogs. Others sell digital products, run small online shops or earn commissions by recommending products. These can be legitimate businesses. Yet there is a growing industry built around the promise of passive income, money that supposedly keeps coming in after the initial work has been done. The trouble is that setting something up is rarely the same as making it profitable.

The difference between ambition and earnings is clear in recent research. Save the Student's 2026 survey found that 29% of respondents had a side hustle or business. Of those, 79% had made less than £500 over the previous year. Other surveys produce different figures because they question different groups and define side hustles differently. Even so, the evidence suggests that spectacular earnings are far from typical. Small amounts of extra money can make a genuine difference, but that is not the same as being able to quit your job.

Consider digital products such as online invitations or printable planners. There is no warehouse to rent, and the same file can be sold more than once. But the costs are real: crowded marketplaces, software subscriptions, listing fees and hours spent promoting products. Affiliate marketing is different. A company pays you a commission when a reader buys something using your link, but first you need people to trust your recommendations. Building an audience is often the hardest part of the job. Earnings are the money that comes in. Profit is what remains after costs, and many beginners forget the difference.

Perhaps the most awkward question concerns the influencers themselves. If a particular online business is so profitable, why do so many people spend their time selling courses about how to start one? Some are experienced entrepreneurs who offer valuable advice. Others appear to earn more from hopeful beginners than from the businesses they are recommending. In a recent Guardian investigation, a journalist tested several popular online money-making methods and found that the costs and effort quickly undermined the promise of easy profits.

It would be a mistake to dismiss side hustles altogether. They can provide flexibility, teach commercial skills and give people more control over their income. But a healthy dose of scepticism is useful when an opportunity promises big returns for little work. Before paying for software or a training course, calculate the likely costs, research your competitors and work out where your customers would come from. Financial independence may begin with a good idea, but it rarely begins with doing nothing.

Advanced version

The pitch usually starts with an ordinary morning: breakfast, a phone and another payment arriving. The creator says the business almost runs itself. What the video leaves outside the frame is harder to price: making the product, finding customers and keeping them happy. Before envying the income, it is worth asking what work produced it.

The term side hustle covers an enormous range of activity, from private tutoring and weekend catering to online shops and affiliate marketing. There is nothing inherently dubious about earning money beyond one's main occupation. What deserves scrutiny is the way certain online ventures are presented as largely effortless sources of passive income. A product that can be sold repeatedly without being manufactured again may be inexpensive to distribute, but somebody still has to design it, market it, answer customer questions and persuade strangers to part with their money.

The gulf between social-media aspiration and ordinary earnings is revealing. In Save the Student's 2026 survey, 29% of respondents reported running a business or side hustle, yet 79% of that group had earned less than £500 in the previous twelve months. These figures describe a particular student sample, not every entrepreneur, but they provide a useful counterweight to the extraordinary success stories filling online feeds. Commercial surveys of British side hustlers similarly suggest that modest, inconsistent earnings are considerably more common than the life-changing sums advertised by prominent creators.

Digital marketplaces offer a useful illustration. Selling printable planners or design templates removes the need to hold physical stock, but low barriers to entry also attract competitors, and products that need little specialist knowledge are easy to imitate.

Affiliate marketing has its own difficulties: a commission link is worthless without attention, credibility and a customer willing to buy. Costs add up in both cases. Profit is what remains after platform charges, subscriptions and advertising, and it also has to cover the value of the hours the owner puts in.

A further complication is the business of teaching other people to make money. Some experienced creators sell genuinely useful training; others market expensive courses supported by carefully selected testimonials and vague income claims. The incentive is worth examining. A person may find it more lucrative to sell the prospect of entrepreneurship than to pursue the activity supposedly generating their wealth. A Guardian investigation published in October 2026 documented how difficult several fashionable online ventures proved in practice, reinforcing the need to distinguish exceptional cases from representative outcomes.

None of this undermines the value of entrepreneurship. Small ventures can create opportunities, top up low wages and develop skills that later become commercially valuable. The danger lies in presenting genuine enterprise as a shortcut around effort, risk and uncertainty. A prospective founder needs to know the customer, the competition, the likely costs and the time required before the first sale. Perhaps the most useful question is not whether a business can produce income while its owner sleeps, but how much work was necessary to get it there in the first place.

9Role-play: should we buy the course?

Work in pairs. Read your card, then have the conversation. Swap roles and do it again.

Student A · the excited friend

You've seen an online course that costs £300 and promises money while you sleep. You want your friend to join you. Explain why it looks like a good chance and answer your friend's worries.

Student B · the cautious friend

You're not sure the course is worth £300. Ask at least two questions about the costs, the customers and who is really making money. Suggest a cheaper way to test the idea.

Useful phrases

  • I've just seen a course that teaches you to earn money online.
  • That's a lot of money for something we can't try first.
  • Who's actually making money from it?
  • I'd feel better if we checked the reviews together.
  • What would you do if it didn't work?
  • Let's find a compromise.

Afterwards, tell the class: did you buy the course, and what made you decide?
