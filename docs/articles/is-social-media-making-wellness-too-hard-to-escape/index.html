Core version

Scroll through almost any social media app and you will meet "wellness". It might be a morning routine or a video from someone who says they know the secret to a healthier life. In November 2026, ABC News in Australia reported that some eating disorder experts are worried about this kind of content, especially for young people.

One of them is Kathleen McBride, who is 30 and lives on the Sunshine Coast in Queensland. She recovered from an eating disorder and now works at a treatment centre. She says that as a teenager, adverts taught her to worry about her body. Today, she says, similar ideas have come back with a new name, "wellness". They arrive through apps that choose what each person sees.

Money is part of the story. The Global Wellness Institute, a research group, forecast in 2024 that the world's wellness industry could be worth about nine trillion US dollars by 2028. That is a forecast, not a fact. In September, an Australian charity, the Butterfly Foundation, used the figure to ask: when wellness is big business, who really benefits?

Dr Angela Hinz is a researcher at the University of the Sunshine Coast. She says social media has a well-established effect on how young people see their bodies. She also says that many young people are very interested in being healthy, and that is not the problem. The problem is that some posts are wrong, and they can make young people trust doctors less.

The experts are careful about one point. Eating disorders are serious illnesses with many causes, and nobody in the report says that social media creates them alone. But the experts say dieting is one of the biggest risks that people can change.

What do they want? Dr Hinz hopes for stronger laws, so that harmful posts can be taken down and creators who earn money from them are held responsible. She also wants accurate health information to be easy to find. So the question is not whether all health content is bad. It is how to tell helpful advice from messages that mainly help someone sell something. If a post raises a red flag for you, take it with a pinch of salt and ask who gains. Anyone who is worried about their own eating should talk to a doctor or a support service.

Advanced version

Few words have enjoyed such an effortless rise as "wellness". It sounds benign, even virtuous. An ABC News report published in Australia on 1 November 2026 gathers eating disorder specialists who argue that, on social media, genuine health guidance and commercial diet culture have become difficult to tell apart.

The starting point is personal. Kathleen McBride, 30, recovered from an eating disorder and now works at a residential treatment centre on Queensland's Sunshine Coast. She says that as a teenager she took in messages from adverts and celebrities implying that a woman's worth depended on her body. What alarms her is continuity: the same assumptions, she argues, have been repackaged as wellness and delivered by algorithms that keep serving up such content to young people.

The economics matter, and so does the way they are described. The Global Wellness Institute, an industry research body, forecast in 2024 that the global wellness economy would grow from roughly 6.3 trillion US dollars in 2023 to about nine trillion by 2028. That is a projection, not a measured result. The Butterfly Foundation, an Australian eating disorder charity, nevertheless cited it in September to frame a pointed question: when so much money is at stake, whose interests do the messages we are sold about our bodies actually serve?

The specialists are not simply hostile to information. Dr Angela Hinz, a researcher at the University of the Sunshine Coast, calls the influence of social media on young people's body image well established, yet she also notes a genuine trend among young Australians towards being health conscious. Her worry lies in the format: short videos encourage parasocial relationships, in which viewers feel close to creators who have never met them, and combined with misinformation they can end up turning the body into a project with no finish line.

Precision is essential about what is claimed. Eating disorders are serious illnesses with many contributing factors, and nobody quoted says that social media creates them on its own. The charity and the dietitian interviewed point instead to diet culture, the notion that some bodies are inherently better than others, and describe dieting as one of the largest risk factors that can be changed. On this reading, social media is an amplifier rather than a cause, and it can blur the line between health guidance and advertising. The evidence offered is largely clinical experience and expert opinion, which says little about how widespread the problem is.

The proposed remedies reflect different views of responsibility. Dr Hinz favours stronger duty-of-care laws, so that harmful content is flagged and removed and creators who profit from it are held accountable. Mathieu Cadart, who works in a treatment centre, accepts that platforms should police content better but insists that creators carry a moral duty too. Dr Hinz argues for accurate, regulated material to be easier to find, which turns the debate towards what trustworthy guidance would look like.

If the topic affects you or someone close to you, a doctor or a specialist support service is the right place to start. For everyone else, the report leaves a practical test that is easier to state than to apply: when advice arrives with a price tag or a brand deal attached, ask who has skin in the game.
