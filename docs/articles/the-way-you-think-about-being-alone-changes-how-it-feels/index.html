A study found that articles about being alone are ten times more likely to call it harmful than helpful, and that what people believe predicts how solitude actually feels to them.

Core version

Imagine your plans for this evening are cancelled. Your phone is quiet and nobody needs anything from you. Are you disappointed, or are you secretly delighted?

Different people answer that question very differently, and so does the same person on different days. Psychologists separate two things that sound similar. Being alone is a situation: there is nobody with you. Loneliness is a feeling, and it appears when you want more connection than you have. You can feel lonely in a crowded room.

In 2025, researchers at the University of Michigan published a study in the journal Nature Communications about how we think about time alone. First they looked at how newspapers write about it. They read 144 American articles published between 2020 and 2022, and found that the picture was almost always negative. The articles were about ten times more likely to say that being alone was harmful than to say it was good for you.

Then they looked at what ordinary people believe. People who think time alone is harmful reported feeling lonelier after spending time by themselves. People who think it can be good reported feeling less lonely after exactly the same thing. The researchers also compared countries, and the countries where people felt lonelier were the countries where beliefs about being alone were more negative.

In one experiment, people who read about the benefits of spending time alone became more positive about it afterwards.

A second paper, published in 2026 by a team of Czech researchers, adds another piece. They argue that the difference is not how many hours you spend alone but whether you chose them. Time alone that you picked for your own reasons tends to rest you. Time alone that was forced on you tends not to.

The researchers are careful here. Most of their everyday evidence cannot prove that beliefs cause the feeling, and the effect may work in both directions. Lonely people may think badly of solitude because they are lonely.

Still, there is something useful in it. Time to yourself does not have to be a problem to solve. You do not have to use it to improve yourself. Reading, cooking, walking or doing nothing at all can be enough. And if you have ever felt that an empty evening was a failure, it is worth asking where you learned that.

Intermediate version

Your plans fall through. For a second you cannot tell whether you are annoyed or relieved. You were looking forward to seeing your friend, and yet an evening with the remote control and nobody to negotiate with suddenly sounds rather good.

A week later the identical empty evening feels flat and lonely. Nothing in the room has changed. Your experience of it has. That gap is why psychologists distinguish between being alone, which is a circumstance, and loneliness, which is the uncomfortable feeling that the connection you want is missing.

A 2025 study in Nature Communications, by researchers at the University of Michigan, asked where our expectations about solitude come from, and the answer was unflattering to journalism. The team analysed 144 American newspaper articles published between 2020 and 2022. Solitude was presented negatively almost throughout. The articles were roughly ten times more likely to describe being alone as harmful than as beneficial.

That matters because of what the researchers found next. In daily-life studies, people who believed that being alone was bad for them reported more loneliness after spending time by themselves. People with more positive beliefs reported less. Comparing countries produced the same pattern: where loneliness was higher, beliefs about solitude were more negative. In a randomised experiment, simply reading material about the benefits of being alone shifted people's beliefs in a positive direction.

The authors do not overclaim. Their everyday studies are not experiments, so they cannot establish that beliefs cause loneliness, and they accept that the influence may run both ways. Feeling lonely is a perfectly good reason to think badly of an empty evening.

There is no shortage of advice about spending time alone well, and some of it quietly turns rest into another task. You are invited to meditate, journal, start a hobby and return improved. The research points somewhere simpler. An afternoon with no agenda at all, walking without counting the distance or cooking exactly what you feel like, appears to do the job.

A separate paper published in 2026 approaches the same territory from another angle. Writing in Integrative Psychological and Behavioral Science, a team of Czech researchers argues that solitude and loneliness are not divided by how physically isolated you are, but by motivation. Time alone that you chose, for reasons that are yours, tends to be restorative. Time alone that was imposed on you tends not to be.

None of this means loneliness is a misunderstanding. It is painful, and telling somebody to enjoy their own company is not an answer to it. But if an empty evening arrives, it may be worth noticing how much of your reaction was decided before it started.

Advanced version

There is a particular pleasure in a cancelled plan when you are tired. An evening promised to somebody else returns to you, and with it the rare luxury of not explaining yourself. There are also evenings when the phone stays quiet and you wish it would not. The sofa is identical. The experience is not.

We tend to treat being alone as one condition with one emotional value, and it is neither. The fact of being by yourself and the feeling of loneliness come apart constantly. You can be alone and perfectly content, or surrounded by people and unreachable. You can treasure a silent afternoon and miss somebody during it.

A 2025 paper in Nature Communications by Rodriguez, Schertz and Kross set out to examine where our assumptions about solitude originate, and found a culprit close to home. Analysing 144 American newspaper articles from 2020 to 2022, the researchers found coverage of being alone was overwhelmingly negative, around ten times more likely to frame it as harmful than as beneficial. We are, in other words, instructed repeatedly that solitude is a deficiency.

The instruction appears to take. In their daily-life data, people holding negative beliefs about being alone reported greater loneliness following time by themselves, while those with positive beliefs reported less. The cross-national comparison pointed the same way, with higher national loneliness accompanying more negative beliefs. A randomised experiment showed the beliefs themselves are movable: participants who read about the benefits of solitude reported more positive views afterwards.

The authors are commendably restrained about what this establishes. Their observational studies cannot demonstrate causation, and they accept the arrow may point both ways. Loneliness is an excellent reason to regard an empty evening with suspicion.

There is an awkwardness in reporting this in a news article, which is itself a small addition to the corpus the researchers measured. The honest account is that writing about loneliness has tended towards alarm, because alarm travels, while the quieter proposition, that a good deal of time alone is unremarkable and some of it is restorative, makes a weak headline. That asymmetry is more or less what the 144 articles recorded.

A conceptual paper published in 2026 in Integrative Psychological and Behavioral Science, by Heissler and colleagues, proposes a complementary distinction. Drawing on self-determination theory, they argue that what separates solitude from loneliness is not isolation but motivation. Solitude is self-determined and chosen, and can support autonomy, competence or relatedness. Loneliness is non-self-determined, arriving through imposition or the frustration of those same needs. The practical implication is that environments which support people's autonomy may produce better solitude.

Two cautions are worth holding onto. Choice is not distributed evenly: a parent of young children and someone living far from friends do not face the same menu. And enjoying your own company is not a substitute for friendship. A good book cannot ask how you are. The goal is not to need nobody. It is to notice what you need, and to have some real chance of getting it.
