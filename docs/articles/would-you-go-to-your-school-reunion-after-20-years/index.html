Core version

When Mike walked into his school reunion in Melbourne, Australia, he was not just hoping to see old friends. He had something important to say. Twenty years had passed since he left school, but one memory had stayed with him. A boy he knew had been bullied because he was gay. Mike had not joined in, but he had not stood up for him either. Now he wanted to apologise.

Mike, a firefighter in his forties, told his story to the Guardian in October 2026. At the reunion, he found the old friend and brought up what had happened at school. The conversation was awkward. The other man ended up trying to make Mike feel better about his guilt. Mike realised that his apology had placed an unfair responsibility on the person who had suffered. Still, the two men reconnected.

School reunions often bring back powerful memories. You may remember the friendly teacher who encouraged you, the embarrassing haircut you had or the people you tried hard to impress. Some former students look forward to meeting everyone again. Others would rather stay at home than return to a room full of people who knew them as teenagers.

Part of the problem is that people change. The quiet student may become a confident adult. The most popular person in the class may have an ordinary job and an ordinary life. Yet when old classmates get together, they sometimes fall back into the same roles. It can feel as if somebody has turned the clock back twenty years, even though everyone looks different.

Social media has changed reunions too. In the past, you might have had no idea where your classmates had ended up. Today, you may already know about their weddings, children and holidays. But online photographs rarely tell the full story. A person who appears successful may be struggling, while somebody with a simple life may be perfectly happy.

So is going to a reunion a good idea? It depends on what you expect. You might catch up with someone special, or you might simply have a strange evening and laugh about it later. If you want to put something right, as Mike did, think about the other person's feelings first.

Intermediate version

Mike had been out of school for twenty years, but one memory refused to fade. As a teenager in Melbourne, he had watched a friend experience homophobic bullying. He had never joined in, yet he had not spoken up either. Now, in his forties and working as a firefighter, he was heading to a school reunion with a particular purpose: he wanted to apologise for his silence.

Speaking to the Guardian for an October 2026 article, Mike explained that he managed to find his old friend and bring up the past. The apology did not unfold quite as he had imagined. Instead, the man who had been bullied ended up reassuring Mike that he need not feel guilty. Mike came away recognising the uncomfortable irony: in trying to make amends, he had asked someone else to deal with his feelings. Nevertheless, the two reconnected over drinks, giving the story a warmer ending than he might have expected.

It is easy to see why reunions make people nervous. Few other social events bring together so many versions of ourselves at once. The confident adult who walks into the room may suddenly remember being an insecure fifteen-year-old. Old nicknames resurface, half-forgotten arguments are mentioned and somebody inevitably asks a question you would rather avoid. Even the simple business of remembering names can leave people feeling awkward and exposed.

For other former students, the attraction is obvious. They want to catch up with childhood friends, hear what became of familiar faces or revisit a period that helped shape them. A reunion can also challenge our assumptions. The class clown might have become a serious parent, while the shyest teenager could be the person with the most extraordinary stories. People who scarcely spoke at school sometimes get along brilliantly as adults, once the pressure of popularity disappears.

Social media complicates that sense of discovery. You may have followed someone's career or family life for years without exchanging a single message. Yet their online profile reveals only what they choose to show. At a reunion, a casual conversation may uncover experiences that never appeared in a photograph. Equally, comparisons about jobs, relationships and appearance can stir up old insecurities, especially if everybody seems determined to present their life in the best possible light.

Perhaps the healthiest way to approach a reunion is with curiosity rather than a scorecard. You do not have to prove that you have become more successful, attractive or interesting than the person sitting opposite you. Nor is it always wise to treat the occasion as a chance to settle every old grievance. Mike's experience suggests that an apology can matter, but it needs to leave room for the other person's wishes. Sometimes the most revealing thing about returning to school is discovering how little the old rankings matter.

Advanced version

Mike had spent twenty years thinking about the classmate he had failed to defend. Now the Melbourne firefighter was going back to school to apologise. He had not joined in the homophobic bullying, but he had stayed silent. At the reunion, he hoped to put that right.

His account, published by the Guardian in October 2026, illustrates how unpredictable such encounters can be. Mike did find the former friend and apologise, but the exchange took an uncomfortable turn. The man who had experienced the bullying ended up comforting the person who had failed to defend him. Mike subsequently recognised that his attempt to clear his conscience had inadvertently placed another burden on someone already wronged. The pair nevertheless caught up over drinks, suggesting that imperfect conversations can still open a door.

School reunions are uniquely capable of collapsing time. Adults who have built careers, families and independent identities may walk into a familiar room and immediately feel fifteen again. A nickname, a particular laugh or the sight of a former rival can bring back insecurities that seemed long settled. Unlike an ordinary gathering, a reunion comes with a ready-made social hierarchy, one that participants may have spent years escaping. Even those who arrive feeling self-assured can find themselves measuring their present lives against adolescent expectations.

Yet reunions can also undermine the stories we have told ourselves about other people. The supposedly destined high achiever may have changed direction entirely; the quiet outsider may have developed an extraordinary life. Former classmates who barely exchanged a word might discover unexpected common ground, now that they no longer need to perform for a teenage audience. In that sense, returning to school can be an exercise in empathy: the people frozen in our memories have continued to grow, struggle and reinvent themselves out of sight.

Social media adds another layer of complexity. We may think we know what became of classmates because we have glimpsed their promotions, weddings and holidays online. But an edited feed is not the same as a sustained conversation, and envy can flourish when we compare our untidy private lives with someone else's carefully polished public image. A reunion offers the possibility of a more honest encounter, although that depends on whether people are prepared to drop the performance. The temptation to keep score does not necessarily disappear when the school uniform comes off.

Mike's story raises a particularly delicate issue: can an apology help heal a past injury, or does it sometimes serve primarily the person offering it? Intentions matter, but so does the effect on the person listening. Anyone turning up at a reunion hoping to put things right might first consider whether an old classmate wants that conversation at all. The most valuable outcome may be neither forgiveness nor renewed friendship, but a more generous understanding of who everyone has become.
