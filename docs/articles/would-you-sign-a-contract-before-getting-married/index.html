Core version

Most people imagine a wedding as a day full of love, music and promises. They probably do not imagine sitting in an office with a lawyer. However, more young couples in the United States are talking about money before they get married. Some are signing a prenuptial agreement, often called a prenup. This is a legal document that explains what might happen to money and property if the marriage ends.

The Guardian reported on this on 11 November 2026. It referred to a recent Harris Poll with Bloomberg, which found that 53% of married or engaged Americans under 45 said they had signed a prenup. An earlier survey, mentioned in a 2022 article, gave a lower figure for a different age group. These surveys cannot tell us exactly how every couple feels, but they suggest that the idea is no longer only for very rich people.

Supporters say a prenup helps couples have difficult conversations early. They can talk about savings, debts and property. If one partner has a business or receives money from relatives, a clear agreement might help avoid a big argument later. Some couples say honesty about money brings them closer.

Other people find the idea uncomfortable. They worry that preparing for divorce before the wedding sends the wrong message. Why plan for a relationship to end when you are promising to stay together? If one person has much more money, they may have more power when deciding what goes into the agreement. A document is not automatically fair just because both people sign it.

The rules are different from country to country, and a prenup does not always work in the same way everywhere. Couples may need independent legal advice. The important part of the story is not that everyone should rush out and sign a contract. It is that money can be difficult to talk about, even in a strong relationship. Avoiding the subject does not make the problem disappear.

Perhaps the real choice is not between love and money. It is between talking openly about the future and hoping everything will work out. A couple can trust each other and still have sensible conversations about bills, savings and difficult situations. On the other hand, some people would rather keep their marriage free from legal documents. If you were planning a wedding, would a prenup make you feel safer or less romantic?

Intermediate version

A wedding is supposed to celebrate love and commitment, not remind people that relationships sometimes end. Yet an increasing number of young couples in the United States appear willing to discuss divorce before they even get married. Their tool is a prenuptial agreement, usually shortened to prenup, which sets out arrangements for money and property if the marriage breaks down. What used to seem like something only very rich people needed is becoming a more ordinary part of wedding planning.

A Guardian feature published on 11 November 2026 described young Americans who see prenups as a form of protection rather than a prediction of failure. The article cited a Harris Poll conducted with Bloomberg this year, in which 53% of engaged or married Americans under the age of 45 said they had signed one. That does not prove that most young couples everywhere are following the trend. Nevertheless, it illustrates how attitudes towards discussing finances before marriage may be shifting.

For supporters, the logic is straightforward. Couples talk about where they want to live, whether they want children and what kind of wedding they can afford. Why should they avoid discussing debts, savings or property? A prenup can encourage both partners to lay their cards on the table. It may help protect a family business or an inheritance and reduce uncertainty if things go wrong. Some young couples even describe the process as an act of intimacy because it demands honesty about subjects people often find embarrassing.

Critics see a different problem. Sitting down to plan for a possible divorce can feel like starting a marriage with one foot out of the door. The conversation may become particularly uncomfortable if one partner earns far more than the other. A contract that appears fair today might look very different after years of childcare, career sacrifices or unexpected illness. There is also the risk that someone signs an agreement without fully understanding its consequences, simply because they do not want to upset their future spouse.

Legal arrangements vary between countries, and the details matter. A prenup is not a magic solution, and its treatment by courts depends on local law and individual circumstances. Anyone considering one would need proper advice rather than following a trend on social media. At the same time, the debate reveals something broader: many couples are becoming more comfortable talking openly about financial independence within a shared life.

Perhaps we have created a false choice between romance and practical thinking. Successful relationships often involve difficult conversations, whether about money, caring responsibilities or plans for the future. A prenup might help some people feel secure, while others may see it as unnecessary or even insulting. The agreement itself may matter less than whether both partners can discuss it honestly. If someone you loved asked you to sign one, would you see it as a sensible precaution or a warning sign?

Advanced version

Marriage is traditionally associated with optimism, shared dreams and promises about the future. A prenuptial agreement seems to introduce a less romantic possibility: what happens if those promises cannot be kept? Yet a growing number of younger Americans appear willing to confront that question before exchanging vows. For some, discussing the division of assets is not evidence that love is fragile. It is a practical way of acknowledging that financial independence and emotional commitment can exist side by side.

An interactive Guardian feature published on 11 November 2026 examined this shift in attitudes. It cited a Harris Poll conducted with Bloomberg in which 53% of engaged or married Americans under 45 reported having signed a prenup. A previously cited 2022 survey found a different figure among married or engaged 18 to 34-year-olds, although the age groups and samples are not directly comparable. These numbers should not be treated as a precise measure of a universal trend, but they help explain why prenups are increasingly discussed outside traditionally wealthy circles.

Advocates argue that such agreements encourage openness at a moment when couples are making enormous commitments. Partners may enter marriage with different incomes, savings, debts or family responsibilities. A carefully negotiated agreement can clarify how particular assets would be treated if the relationship ended, potentially reducing future conflict. More importantly, the process may force couples to confront assumptions they have never expressed. Rather than sweeping difficult questions under the carpet, they can discuss what financial fairness actually means to each of them.

However, the idea that a signed contract automatically protects both people deserves scrutiny. Financial power is rarely distributed evenly within a relationship, and the person with greater resources may be better placed to negotiate favourable terms. Circumstances can also change dramatically after marriage. One partner might step away from a career to raise children or care for relatives, while the other continues accumulating wealth. An arrangement that seemed reasonable at the beginning could later feel profoundly unequal. Some people also feel that preparing for separation undermines the emotional significance of the commitment itself.

There are legal complications too. Prenuptial agreements are treated differently across jurisdictions, and their enforceability may depend on disclosure, independent advice and whether the terms are considered fair. It would therefore be misleading to present them as universally binding contracts or straightforward insurance against a costly divorce. The broader discussion is about negotiating uncertainty: how should two people protect themselves without making a relationship feel like a business transaction?

Perhaps the sharpest disagreement concerns what trust requires. One view holds that trusting a partner means not anticipating the worst; another suggests that trust becomes stronger when people can talk candidly about uncomfortable possibilities. Neither position can be reduced to a simple rule. A marriage involves affection, but it also involves property, labour, vulnerability and decisions with deeply personal, long-term and sometimes irreversible consequences. Would you feel more secure knowing that difficult financial questions had already been settled, or would you rather enter marriage without imagining its possible end?
