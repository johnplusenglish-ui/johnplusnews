Core version

You have paid for a week in a beautiful city, but by midday the streets are too hot to explore. You end up hiding in an air-conditioned room, waiting for the evening. This is not the summer holiday you imagined. After several very hot European summers, some travellers are making a surprising choice: instead of flying south for sunshine, they are heading north for cool air, light rain and a comfortable night's sleep.

The trend is called a "coolcation". Visitors choose places such as Scotland, Norway or Sweden, where they can walk, kayak or visit towns without planning every hour around the heat. The attraction is not bad weather for its own sake. It is the chance to make the most of the whole day, sleep comfortably and come home feeling rested rather than exhausted.

Scotland is one place benefiting from the new interest in cooler holidays. In August, the BBC met holiday-cottage owners near Glencoe who said visitors were choosing the area partly because of its weather. Some guests were even pleased when it rained. They could walk, kayak or sleep comfortably without needing air conditioning. Sweden, Norway, Finland and Iceland have also attracted travellers looking for milder summer temperatures and beautiful natural scenery.

The travel industry has noticed. Research reported by Euronews in July found growing interest in northern destinations. Norway's tourism organisation said about one in five foreign visitors chose the country partly for its cooler weather. But experts warn that it is too early to say everyone is giving up Mediterranean holidays. Spain, Italy and Greece remain extremely popular, and people choose places for many reasons, including price, food, culture and beaches.

Cooler destinations also have their own problems. Norway's Lofoten Islands, for example, have experienced crowded roads, rubbish and pressure on local communities as tourism has grown. A peaceful-looking destination can fill up quickly when everybody has the same idea. And northern countries are not protected from climate change. Scotland experienced unusual heat and wildfires during the same summer that visitors were travelling there to cool down.

Perhaps the biggest change will be when people travel, rather than where. A family might still choose Greece, but visit in May or October instead of August. Others might spend summer in the mountains or by a northern lake. Sunshine will always be part of the holiday dream for many people. But as summers grow hotter, a little rain and a comfortable night's sleep are starting to sound surprisingly attractive to some travellers.

Intermediate version

Imagine arriving for a long-awaited city break and discovering that the afternoon is too hot for sightseeing. The streets empty, the shutters close and the holiday moves indoors until sunset. After another summer of intense European heat, some travellers have decided that guaranteed sunshine is no longer a guarantee of enjoyment. They are looking north instead, for fresh air, cooler nights and the freedom to stay outside.

The trend has taken off under the awkward but memorable name "coolcation". Scotland, Norway and southern Sweden offer fjords, lochs, long evenings and temperatures that make walking or kayaking possible throughout the day. The attraction is not simply a colder thermometer. It is the freedom to explore without organising meals, journeys and sightseeing around the most punishing hours.

The weather has helped push this idea into the mainstream. Europe's Copernicus climate service reported that summer 2026 was the continent's third warmest on record, with western Europe recording its hottest summer. In August, the BBC visited a holiday business near Glencoe whose owners said customers were increasingly choosing Scotland because of its cooler climate. Some guests welcomed the rain rather than complaining about it. The kind of weather tourism advertisements once tried to play down had become a selling point.

There are signs that this goes beyond a few unusual travellers. Euronews reported growing interest in Scandinavian and northern European destinations. Separately, Norway's tourism organisation said roughly one in five overseas visitors regarded cooler summer temperatures as a reason for going there. However, tourism researchers are cautious about declaring the end of the beach holiday. Southern Europe is still enormously popular, and climate is only one factor alongside cost, transport links, food and personal preference. An internet search does not always turn into a booking.

Nor is heading north a perfect solution. In Norway's Lofoten Islands, growing visitor numbers have put pressure on roads, parking and local facilities. Moving crowds from one part of Europe to another could simply create new problems. Northern destinations are also experiencing climate change themselves. Scotland suffered a major wildfire in the Cairngorms during the summer, a reminder that cooler does not mean risk-free. And taking a longer flight to escape the heat can add to the emissions that contribute to global warming.

A more lasting change may be a shift in the holiday calendar. People who love Spain or Italy might still visit, but choose spring or autumn instead of the height of summer. Others may discover that their happiest holiday involves a mountain path rather than a sunlounger. The appetite for sunshine has hardly disappeared. But when a holiday starts to feel more like hard work than a break, it's understandable that a grey sky can suddenly look rather inviting.

Advanced version

The promise of the traditional European summer holiday was simple: travel south, escape the drizzle and come home with a suntan. That promise begins to fail when sightseeing becomes an endurance test and sleep requires an air conditioner running all night. After a succession of punishing summers, some travellers are choosing almost the opposite holiday. They want breathable afternoons, cool bedrooms and the once-unfashionable possibility of rain.

The label "coolcation" sounds as though it came from a marketing meeting, but the attraction is more substantial than the slogan. Northern destinations offer the possibility of walking, kayaking or exploring throughout the day rather than arranging every activity around the temperature. What was once marketed apologetically, such as cloud, wind and the chance of rain, can now be presented as freedom from a holiday governed by heat.

In Scotland, businesses are already turning their traditionally unpredictable weather into an asset. BBC reporting from Glencoe in August described holidaymakers seeking relief from heat further south and cottage owners surprised to find that customers actually welcomed a downpour. Farther east, travellers have been exploring Sweden's coast and Norway's fjords for similar reasons.

Euronews, reporting with AFP, described growing interest in northern destinations. Norway's tourism organisation said about one in five international visitors named cooler summer weather as a reason for their trip. The appeal is also being able to explore without organising every hour around the temperature.

Nevertheless, the evidence calls for a measured interpretation. A spike in online searches may reflect curiosity rather than a lasting change in behaviour, and Europe's Mediterranean destinations continue to draw enormous crowds. Decisions depend on affordability, food, culture and flight connections, not the thermometer alone. Scandinavia can also be expensive, while a rainy week in the Highlands is not everyone's idea of relaxation. Rather than abandoning southern Europe, travellers may increasingly visit it in spring or autumn, leaving the hottest months for mountain regions or northern coasts.

There is an uncomfortable irony in the enthusiasm for cooler destinations. In parts of Norway's Lofoten archipelago, rising tourist numbers have already brought about congested roads, overflowing parking areas and arguments about who should pay for local infrastructure. Tourism pressure does not become sustainable merely because it moves north; the crowds simply turn up somewhere else. Cooler regions are not insulated from the climate crisis: Scotland experienced a substantial Cairngorms wildfire that summer. Long-distance flights, meanwhile, remain part of tourism's environmental footprint, whatever the weather at the destination.

The more consequential shift may therefore concern how holidays are organised, rather than the geographical direction of travel. If tourists spread their trips across different months and destinations, some overcrowded regions could gain breathing space while less-visited communities benefit economically. But that requires planning, infrastructure and an honest account of environmental costs, not simply a new label attached to another fashionable escape. The coolcation captures a changing mood: sunshine is still wanted, but it is no longer the whole of a good holiday.
