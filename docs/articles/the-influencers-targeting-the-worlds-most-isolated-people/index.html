The Influencers Trying to Film the World's Most Isolated People

Some content creators try to film communities that want to be left alone. Why is this so dangerous, and who has the right to decide?

In March 2025, an American traveller called Mykhailo Polyakov travelled by small boat towards North Sentinel Island in the Indian Ocean. He carried a camera and gifts, including a coconut and a can of Diet Coke. His plan was to visit and film a community that has very little contact with the outside world. The trip ended with his arrest by Indian police.

North Sentinel Island is home to the Sentinelese, an Indigenous community that has lived separately from most other people for generations. They do not welcome visitors, and Indian law keeps outsiders out. Outsiders are not allowed to travel there without permission. Polyakov reached the shore and tried to attract attention, but he did not come across any of the island's residents before leaving.

The main concern is not simply that someone broke a rule. Contact can be dangerous for isolated communities because they may have little protection against illnesses carried by visitors. Even an ordinary infection can have terrible results. There is also an important question of respect. The Sentinelese have made it clear that they do not want contact with strangers. Their decision should matter.

Some social media creators want to make videos of places that other people cannot easily visit. Rare footage can pull in millions of views, new followers and advertising money. But an interesting video is not always a good reason to enter somebody else's home or community. Survival International, an organisation working for Indigenous rights, says that there are almost 200 uncontacted groups around the world, many living in remote forests.

Not every film about Indigenous people is harmful. Some communities choose to welcome visitors and work with filmmakers. In those cases, people can give permission and tell their own stories. North Sentinel Island is different because its inhabitants have refused contact. Technology makes it possible to travel further and film more than ever before, but it does not give people the right to go everywhere. Sometimes respecting others means accepting that they do not want to be seen.

When American traveller Mykhailo Polyakov set off towards North Sentinel Island in March 2025, he took a small inflatable boat, a camera and several gifts, including a coconut and a can of Diet Coke. His aim was to reach one of the world's most isolated Indigenous communities and record his visit. He managed to land on the island, but did not meet any of its inhabitants. The trip ended with his arrest by Indian authorities.

The Sentinelese live on a small forested island in the Andaman archipelago and have repeatedly resisted contact with outsiders. Indian law restricts access to protect the community, whose members may be especially vulnerable to infectious diseases introduced by visitors. Polyakov attempted to attract attention before leaving, but the absence of any encounter does not make the visit harmless. It placed the island's inhabitants at risk without their agreement.

The incident forms part of a wider debate about the lengths some adventurers and content creators will go to for extraordinary footage. Remote locations offer an obvious attraction in an online world where familiar images are everywhere. A video that appears to show people living beyond modern civilisation may gain millions of views and attract followers or advertising revenue. But the search for something exclusive can make other people's privacy seem less important than a creator's ambition.

Survival International, an Indigenous rights organisation, estimates that 196 uncontacted groups exist worldwide. Many have chosen to keep away from outsiders after earlier experiences of violence, land loss or disease. Describing such communities as unaware of the modern world can be misleading. In some cases, refusing contact is an informed decision about how to protect their people and way of life.

There is an important difference between filming a community that has agreed to welcome visitors and trying to reach one that has repeatedly rejected them. Some Indigenous groups work with documentary-makers, invite carefully managed tourism and use their own media to campaign for land rights. Such projects can give communities greater control over how their stories are told. Uninvited expeditions offer no similar choice, and visitors may underestimate the consequences of even a brief encounter.

Supporters of exploration sometimes argue that the public has an interest in learning about remote societies. Curiosity can certainly encourage understanding, but it does not automatically create a right to enter another community's territory. Polyakov's case highlights a difficult boundary in the age of social media. Cameras and boats can take people to places that once seemed unreachable. Respecting a community's wishes may require leaving the camera switched off and the island untouched.

The objects in Mykhailo Polyakov's boat were almost absurdly ordinary: a camera, a coconut and a can of Diet Coke. The destination was anything but. In March 2025, the American traveller headed for North Sentinel Island, home to an Indigenous community that has consistently rejected outside contact. He landed, attempted to draw attention and departed without encountering residents. Indian authorities subsequently arrested him. The episode exposed the uneasy intersection of digital ambition, personal curiosity and the rights of people who have chosen to remain isolated.

The Sentinelese inhabit a forested island in the Andaman archipelago, where legal restrictions are intended to prevent unauthorised visits. Those protections are not merely symbolic. Communities with little exposure to outside populations may be exceptionally vulnerable to infectious diseases, while past encounters with outsiders have demonstrated the potential for violence and exploitation. The fact that Polyakov apparently failed to meet anyone does not cancel out the risk created by his arrival.

His expedition reflects a broader problem in the attention economy. The more elusive a place or community appears, the greater its potential value as online content. Images presented as unprecedented can draw in enormous audiences, converting novelty into advertising income and a public profile. In that calculation, Indigenous people risk becoming scenery in somebody else's account of adventure. What might be described as exploration from the visitor's perspective can look very different to those whose boundaries have been ignored.

Survival International estimates that 196 uncontacted groups remain worldwide, many in regions threatened by logging, mining or land encroachment. The label "uncontacted" should not be mistaken for ignorance or a lack of agency. Avoiding outsiders can be a deliberate response to historical experience, including disease, violence and dispossession. To present isolation as a mystery waiting to be solved is to deny communities the capacity to decide their own future.

The distinction between consent and intrusion is essential. Indigenous groups that invite journalists or researchers can negotiate conditions, control access and decide how they are represented. Others choose to use digital media themselves to make political claims and document threats to their territory. Those partnerships differ fundamentally from an unsolicited approach to a population that has repeatedly rejected visitors. Public interest cannot be established merely by the fact that an image would attract attention.

The ethical challenge extends beyond one traveller. Satellite imagery, drones and affordable cameras have made remote places increasingly visible, tempting outsiders to equate technical access with moral permission. It is possible to learn about Indigenous rights, protect land and challenge environmental destruction without attempting direct contact with people who refuse it. The most responsible documentary decision may be one that produces no extraordinary footage at all. Curiosity is valuable, but only when it recognises that other people's right to be left alone is not ours to trade for a few million views.
