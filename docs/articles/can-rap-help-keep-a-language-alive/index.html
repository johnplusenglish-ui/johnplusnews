Core version

Rap often makes us think of English or Spanish songs. But a young Peruvian musician is helping people hear a very different language. Renata Flores mixes rap, hip-hop and other modern sounds with Quechua, an Indigenous language spoken in the Andes. Her songs are reaching new audiences and encouraging younger people to feel proud of a language that has sometimes been treated unfairly.

The Guardian wrote about Flores on 18 November 2026. Flores, who is 25, first became widely known when she was fourteen. She recorded a Quechua version of a Michael Jackson song, and her grandmothers helped her translate it. The video became a hit on YouTube and helped her begin a music career. Since then, she has continued to bring Quechua into modern popular music.

There is a personal reason behind her choice. According to the report, Flores learnt Quechua from her grandmothers because her parents had not taught it to her. They were worried that she might face discrimination. In Peru, people who speak Quechua have sometimes been treated with contempt. By singing in it, Flores helps show that the language belongs on modern stages as well as in family homes.

Her music combines traditional Andean instruments and sounds with styles such as trap and reggaeton. This mixture can surprise listeners who think old languages belong only in museums or history books. Younger musicians in the Ayacucho region are also exploring their own ways of using Quechua. They are not simply copying old songs. They are making something new while keeping an important part of their culture alive.

Music cannot solve every problem facing a language. For people to keep speaking Quechua, they also need chances to use it in schools, public services, media and everyday life. Children need to hear it and have chances to learn it. A popular song may encourage someone to take an interest, but it cannot replace years of learning or support for the people who speak the language.

Still, a song can reach people who would never open a language textbook. It can make a language sound exciting, emotional and full of life. Flores's story also asks a question about identity: do people have to choose between being modern and respecting tradition? Perhaps they can do both. If you could make a song in a language that few people around you understand, would you choose to sing in it anyway?

Advanced version

Can a musical genre associated with global youth culture help revive respect for a language that has endured generations of prejudice? In Peru, rapper Renata Flores is offering a compelling answer. By combining Quechua, an Indigenous language of the Andes, with hip-hop, trap, reggaeton and traditional instruments, she challenges the assumption that cultural heritage must remain rooted in the past. Her music is both contemporary entertainment and a public statement about the value of linguistic identity.

A Guardian feature published on 18 November 2026 explored Flores's career and a wider movement of Quechua-speaking artists. Now 25, Flores first gained widespread attention at the age of fourteen after recording a Quechua version of a Michael Jackson song, translated with help from her grandmothers. Rather than treating the viral success as a novelty, she built an artistic identity around the language. Her work has since helped bring Quechua into musical spaces where many younger listeners might not previously have encountered it.

The decision has particular significance because of her family's experience. According to the report, Flores's parents did not pass Quechua on to her because of social stigma and the fear that she would face discrimination. Indigenous languages have often been stigmatised, and speaking one can carry social consequences that outsiders may overlook. Flores's choice to learn from her grandmothers therefore represents more than a musical preference. It is an attempt to reclaim something that earlier generations sometimes felt obliged to hide in order to avoid prejudice.

Her work illustrates how preservation and innovation can reinforce each other. By blending Andean sounds with contemporary urban music, Flores refuses the idea that an Indigenous language must be confined to ceremonies, traditional folk performances or academic study. Other young musicians in Ayacucho are finding ways to express modern experiences through Quechua too. Their work can challenge stereotypes and make cultural continuity appealing to listeners who identify strongly with global popular music. A language remains alive when it is used to say new things, not only when it is remembered for saying old ones.

Still, music has limitations as a tool for linguistic survival. The success of a prominent performer does not guarantee that children can study in Quechua, access public services in it or use it without facing discrimination. A widely shared song may spark interest, but long-term change depends on families, schools, institutions and opportunities for everyday communication. Celebrating Indigenous culture in entertainment while failing to respect its speakers in ordinary life would be a shallow form of progress.

The story also complicates a familiar debate about cultural authenticity. Some might argue that traditional languages should be protected by keeping their original forms of expression intact. Flores's approach suggests the opposite: combining old and new can be a way of resisting disappearance rather than surrendering to global culture. Her music does not ask younger listeners to choose between modern identity and inherited language. It invites them to inhabit both. If preserving a language means keeping it alive, should communities be encouraged to change how it sounds and where it is heard?
