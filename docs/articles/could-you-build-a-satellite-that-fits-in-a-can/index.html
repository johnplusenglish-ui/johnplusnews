Core version

Hold a drinks can in your hand. Now imagine that this can is a model satellite. It is small, light and a little silly. But in the European Space Agency's CanSat project, a model satellite the size and shape of a can is a real school challenge.

A CanSat is a model of a real satellite, built to match the size and shape of a soft drink can. It doesn't go into orbit. Students design it, build it, test it and launch it, and ESA helps to coordinate the project. Teams have three to six students aged 14 to 19, and a teacher or mentor guides them.

Every CanSat must do one job: measure temperature and pressure. Then each team chooses a second job of its own. The ESA article doesn't say what those jobs were, so we can only imagine them. This is the part where a team can ask a question that it finds interesting.

The can is launched to about one kilometre above the ground. It can also be released from something like a drone, a platform or a balloon.

In June 2026, about 170 students and teachers from 26 countries met in the Netherlands, at ESA's technology centre. The event was called CanSat Space Engineer for a Day. National competitions were held in those countries before that. ESA says the event itself was not a competition. On the last day, each group presented a feasibility study, which is a plan that explains whether an idea could really work.

This is a report of a past event, not an invitation to join it. Not every country takes part, and you can't build a CanSat just by reading about it. A CanSat is also not a real satellite in orbit.

So here is your challenge on paper. Your model satellite can measure one extra thing. What would you choose? And which job would you like in the team: designer, builder, tester or presenter?

Intermediate version

Take a soft drink can and imagine that it has to do the work of a satellite. It sounds like a joke until you read how seriously the European Space Agency (ESA) treats it. In the CanSat project, a can-sized satellite is the whole challenge.

A CanSat is a simulation of a real satellite, built to match the size and shape of a soft drink can. It doesn't go into orbit. Instead, students design, build, test and launch it as a school project that brings several subjects together, and ESA coordinates the programme. The rules are simple. Every CanSat has to measure temperature and pressure. On top of that, each team chooses and completes a second mission of its own. The ESA article gives no examples, so the choice stays open: a team can decide what question is worth answering. The can is launched to about one kilometre up, or it can be deployed from a drone, a platform or a balloon.

In June 2026, ESA held the CanSat Space Engineer for a Day event at its technology centre in the Netherlands. About 170 students and teachers from 26 countries took part. National competitions had been held in 22 ESA member states and in Canada, Lithuania, Slovakia and Malta, but the event itself was described as non-competitive. On the final day, every group presented a feasibility study, an honest plan about whether its idea could really work.

That detail is interesting. A feasibility study rewards a team that can explain its choices and its limits, not only one that gets a dramatic result. A team with a modest measurement that it understands well may learn more than a team with an exciting idea that it can't test.

Advanced version

The constraint is almost comically small: a satellite that has to fit inside a soft drink can. Constraints like this are useful in engineering because they force choices. You can't carry everything, so you have to decide what matters.

That is the idea behind the CanSat project run by the European Space Agency (ESA). A CanSat is a simulation of a real satellite, built to match the size and shape of a soft drink can, and it doesn't go into orbit. Students design, build, test and launch it as a school project that brings several subjects together, working in teams of three to six aged 14 to 19 with a teacher or mentor.

Every CanSat has a compulsory mission: it must measure temperature and pressure. Each team then selects and completes a secondary mission of its own, and ESA's article gives no examples, which leaves the real decision to the students. Deployment is modest by space standards. The can is launched to about one kilometre or released from a drone, a platform or a balloon.

In June 2026, about 170 students and teachers from 26 countries gathered at ESA's technology centre in the Netherlands for CanSat Space Engineer for a Day. National competitions had been held in 22 ESA member states and in Canada, Lithuania, Slovakia and Malta, yet ESA described the event itself as non-competitive. On the final day, each group presented a feasibility study.

That choice of final task says a lot. A feasibility study asks a different question from a contest. It asks whether the idea could work, what it would need and where it could fail. It doesn't ask whose can went highest. It treats a team as a group of engineers explaining a plan, and it values an honest account of risk as much as a successful launch.

The team size matters too. Three to six students is small enough for everyone to have a real job, and big enough that nobody can do everything. A designer imagines the can, a builder fits the parts together, a tester tries to break it and a presenter explains it to people who weren't there. Disagreement about the second mission is almost guaranteed, because two good ideas rarely fit into one can.

A feasibility study also changes what success means when something goes wrong. If a first test fails, a team that only wanted to win has little to say. A team that was asking a question can ask which measurement failed, what the design didn't allow for, and what it would change. That is much closer to how real projects work.

A CanSat simulates parts of a satellite mission; it does not go into orbit. The small space forces a real engineering choice: what is worth fitting inside? So on paper, which extra measurement would you add, and what would you want to find out if the first test failed?
