Core version

At a party, you meet someone new. After asking their name, what do you usually ask? For many people, the next question is about work. We often talk about our jobs before we talk about our interests, families or dreams. But does a job really tell someone who we are?

Work is a big part of adult life. We spend many hours doing it, and it can give us friends, confidence and a reason to get up in the morning.

In a 2023 American survey by Pew Research Center, 39% of employees who were not self-employed said their job or career was very or extremely important to their identity.

For some people, this is a good thing. A nurse may feel proud to help patients. A musician might love being known for their music. A skilled worker may enjoy solving difficult problems. Being good at a job can make someone feel useful.

However, problems can appear when work becomes the most important part of life. What happens if someone loses their job, takes up a new career or retires? They might lose more than an income. They may also miss their daily routine and the feeling that others need them.

Other parts of life matter too. A person can be a parent, a neighbour, a volunteer, a football fan or a friend. They may be creative, funny or kind without receiving any money for those qualities. Having several interests can make change easier.

A job title can also hide important details about a person. Two people may both work in offices, but have very different lives. One might spend weekends looking after an older relative, while the other teaches children to play football. People who do not have paid jobs can still work hard at home or in their communities.

Parents and carers often do important work that does not appear on a business card. Some people feel proud when others ask about their career. Others may find the question uncomfortable, especially if they are looking for work. A more open question can give people freedom to talk about the part of life that matters most to them.

Perhaps the question at a party should not always be, 'What do you do?' We could ask, 'What do you enjoy?' instead. A career can be an important chapter in someone's life. It does not have to be the whole story.

Intermediate version

When two strangers meet, a familiar question often arrives within minutes: 'What do you do?' It sounds innocent, but the answer can shape how we judge each other. Someone might introduce themselves as a teacher or engineer rather than explaining what they are passionate about. Has employment become a convenient way to describe our entire identity? It is a question worth turning around: if you had to introduce yourself without mentioning your occupation, what would you say? For some people, the answer takes a moment to come.

For many adults, work provides more than a salary. It gives their week a structure, introduces them to colleagues and offers opportunities to develop skills. According to a Pew Research Center survey in 2023, 39% of American employees who were not self-employed described their job or career as extremely or very important to their sense of identity. Many others considered it somewhat important.

Strong professional pride can be positive. People who find meaning in their work may feel motivated to improve and contribute to society. But there is a risk in connecting self-worth too closely to a job title. Redundancy, retirement or a change of industry can suddenly make someone question who they are without that role.

There is also a social difference between careers. Some jobs receive public admiration, while other essential roles are rarely celebrated. If employment becomes the main measure of someone's value, we may start confusing a person's salary or status with their character. Even apparently harmless compliments can reinforce the problem. Telling someone they are 'so successful' because of their title may overlook whether they are happy, generous or making a difference outside the office.

Psychologists often encourage people to build up sources of meaning beyond paid employment. Friendships, community involvement, learning and creative interests can provide a sense of belonging even during difficult career changes. Of course, people working long hours may have little energy left for these activities.

There is another reason why professional labels can be misleading: they leave unpaid contributions almost invisible. A parent looking after children, a person caring for a relative or somebody supporting a local community may perform demanding, socially valuable work without a recognised occupation. If we use salaries and job titles as shortcuts for judging importance, those contributions risk being overlooked. The pressure can also vary according to circumstances.

Someone enjoying an established career may welcome questions about work, while a person recently made redundant may find them painful. We cannot completely avoid sensitive topics in conversation, but we can leave room for different answers. Asking what somebody has been interested in lately, or how they spend their time, may tell us something more personal and less dependent on employment status.

The goal is not to stop caring about work. It is to give other parts of life space to grow. Perhaps we need to become more curious about the person behind the professional label and remember that identity can change without disappearing.

Advanced version

"What do you do?" is an easy way to start a conversation. The answer usually names a job, although it leaves out most of a life. When work fills the week and supplies the people someone knows, that convenient introduction can start to feel like the whole story.

Try turning the question round: 'What has been keeping you busy lately?' The answers might reveal interests and responsibilities that a title leaves out.

A 2023 Pew Research Center survey found that 39% of US employees who were not self-employed regarded their job or career as extremely or very important to their overall identity. The figure is not evidence that everyone is obsessed with work. It does, however, illustrate how deeply professional roles can become woven into people's understanding of themselves.

This attachment is not inherently unhealthy. Meaningful work can create competence, belonging and pride, while specialised skills may take years to develop. A teacher might value opening opportunities for students; an engineer might enjoy solving problems that matter. Professional identity becomes more complicated when recognition, confidence and relationships depend almost entirely on continuing in the same role. There is a further complication: professional identity is often reinforced by institutions. Job applications, networking events and social media profiles encourage us to package a complicated life into a neat career narrative.

Redundancy and retirement reveal the fragility of that arrangement. A person can lose not only income but also colleagues, status, daily routines and a familiar answer to the question of who they are. Career changes, meanwhile, may feel like personal failures even when they are sensible responses to economic circumstances or changing ambitions.

The issue also has a social dimension. Occupational prestige can influence how others treat us, although the importance of a person's work to society does not always line up with its salary. Someone who performs essential but poorly paid labour deserves no less respect than somebody with an impressive title. A wider definition of achievement need not diminish professional ambition. It may simply make people less vulnerable when a promotion falls through or an industry changes beyond recognition.

Professional identity is also shaped by the assumptions other people make. Employment frequently operates as a shortcut for estimating education, income or status, even though those judgements can be unreliable and socially divisive. Meanwhile, unpaid contributions such as caring for relatives, raising children and supporting community organisations may be demanding without carrying the prestige of a formal title.

The language of identity can therefore reinforce a hierarchy of occupations while overlooking much of the work that holds societies together. It also matters how freely individuals can choose their relationship with employment. Somebody supporting a family through insecure jobs has different options from a professional considering an early retirement. Encouraging a wider sense of self is valuable, but it should not become another individual responsibility imposed on people whose lives are already constrained by economic circumstances.

A broader identity is more resilient, and gives a person more to fall back on: friend, learner, parent, neighbour, artist or simply someone with values that survive changes in employment. Perhaps the healthiest relationship with work is neither total devotion nor indifference. It is allowing a career to contribute to a meaningful life without making it the only source of meaning.
