Why Is France Making Less Wine?

France expects one of its smallest harvests in decades. The striking thing is not the national total but how differently the regions fared.

Core version

A grape harvest is a kind of report card on the weather. In France, the report card for 2026 has just arrived, and it is a difficult one. After a summer of heatwaves, the agriculture ministry estimates that the country will produce about 35 million hectolitres of wine. That would be 2% less than last year, and 14% below the average of the five years before it.

Last year was already poor. According to the International Organisation of Vine and Wine, which collects figures on wine around the world, France produced less in 2025 than in any year since 1957.

Heat is the main reason, though not the only one. Rain later in the season helped a little. French growers have also reduced the amount of land they use for vines by about 1.9% in the past year, so there are simply fewer plants to pick from.

What is unusual this year is how different the regions are. Champagne had a very hard season. The heat made the grapes build up sugar quickly, so growers had to pick much earlier than normal. The harvest there finished in September for the first time, and production is around half of last year's. Burgundy and Beaujolais are expected to be about 30% below their five-year average.

In Bordeaux, however, production is expected to rise by 10% compared with last year. In the south-west, it is up by around 12%.

Why would some areas do better in a hot summer? Southern growers have lived through hot years before, and they have changed how they work. Many now leave more leaves on the vine so that the leaves shade the grapes. Rain in May helped as well.

The man who chairs a national wine advisory board said he had never seen so much variation between regions in his career.

There is some good news too. The ministry and the industry say the grapes are in excellent health, with none of the fungus that wet weather can bring. One Champagne producer picked only three-quarters of a normal crop, but said that for him the quality this year was exceptional.

These are still estimates. The final figures come later, and one hot summer is one year rather than a whole story.

Intermediate version

France expects to produce around 35 million hectolitres of wine this year, according to estimates published by its agriculture ministry in October. That is 2% down on 2025 and 14% below the average of the previous five years. The comparison matters because 2025 was not a generous benchmark either: the International Organisation of Vine and Wine recorded it as France's weakest production since 1957.

A series of summer heatwaves is the main explanation, slightly offset by later rain. A second factor is less dramatic but worth noting. The area planted with vines fell by about 1.9% over the year, so part of the decline is a matter of land rather than weather.

The national figure hides something more interesting. “It's a level of heterogeneity I've never experienced before,” said Jérôme Despey, who chairs the wine advisory board at the public agency FranceAgriMer and grows vines in the southern Hérault department. The regional estimates bear him out.

Champagne had the worst of it. Extreme heat accelerated the build-up of sugar in the grapes, and therefore of natural alcohol, which forced growers to pick exceptionally early. The harvest there wrapped up in September for the first time, with production down by about half on both last year and the five-year average. Burgundy and Beaujolais are projected to come in roughly 30% below their five-year average.

Bordeaux went the other way. Despite a 5% reduction in its planted area, production there is expected to rise 10% on last year, though that still leaves it 11% below the recent average. The south-west is up about 12%, essentially a return to its previous four-year level.

An early harvest is not simply an inconvenience of the calendar. Picking dates are chosen to balance sugar, acidity and flavour, so when heat pushes sugar up faster than the rest of the grape develops, a grower has to choose which quality to sacrifice. Finishing in September, in a region whose rhythm has always run into October, means that decision was taken under pressure.

Despey's explanation for the southern result is adaptation rather than luck. Earlier heat pushed growers there to change their methods, including leaving more foliage on the vine so that the leaves shade the fruit. Rainfall in May helped as well.

Quality is a separate question from quantity, and here the news is better. The ministry and the industry both stressed the harvest's “excellent health”, free of the fungi and moisture-related diseases that a wet year brings. Claude Mandois, a Champagne producer who picked three-quarters of an average volume, described his own harvest as exceptional in appearance and in taste.

Two cautions are worth keeping. These are forecasts rather than final results. And a single hot summer is weather. What the regional differences describe is an industry that has already started planning for a pattern.

Advanced version

France's 2026 wine harvest is estimated at about 35 million hectolitres: 2% below 2025, and 14% below the average of the five preceding years. Since the International Organisation of Vine and Wine logged 2025 as the country's weakest production since 1957, this is a poor year measured against a poor year. The headline is easy to write and not especially informative.

The more revealing number is the spread. Champagne, where extreme heat accelerated the accumulation of sugar and therefore of natural alcohol, harvested exceptionally early and finished in September for the first time, with volumes down roughly half on both last year and the five-year average. Burgundy and Beaujolais are projected around 30% below their five-year average. Bordeaux, meanwhile, expects a 10% increase on 2025 despite a 5% cut in planted area, and the south-west is up some 12%. Jérôme Despey, who chairs FranceAgriMer's wine advisory board and farms in the Hérault, called it a degree of variation he had never encountered.

That spread resists the simplest telling of the story. If heat alone explained everything, the hottest regions would have suffered most, and the south did not. Despey's account is that southern growers have been through this before and changed their practice, including leaving more canopy on the vine so the leaves shade the fruit rather than exposing it. Add useful rainfall in May, and the result is a region that absorbed a bad summer better than cooler ones with less reason to prepare.

Three distinctions are worth holding on to here. The first is between a forecast and a result: these are ministry estimates, and the final accounting arrives later. The second is between weather and climate. One summer of heatwaves is an event, whereas an industry visibly rebuilding its vineyard management around recurring heat is closer to evidence of a trend, which is why the adaptation is the more significant detail. The third is between volume and quality, which do not move together. The ministry and the industry both emphasised the harvest's excellent health, free of the fungal and moisture-related damage a wet year inflicts. Claude Mandois, a Champagne producer who brought in three-quarters of a normal crop, described what he did pick as exceptional to look at and to taste, which is a statement about his own vineyard rather than a forecast for the vintage.

There is also a quieter fact underneath the weather. The planted area fell about 1.9% in a year, and Bordeaux's by 5%. Some of the shortfall is therefore not grapes that failed but vines that are no longer in the ground. The report does not say why those hectares went, and the absence is itself worth noticing. A harvest figure measures the sky, but it also measures decisions growers made long before the summer began, and a vineyard that has been grubbed up does not come back the following year because the weather improves.
