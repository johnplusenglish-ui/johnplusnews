AI Did Not Take Their Jobs. They Left Anyway

A report on workers quitting white-collar careers finds something stranger than job losses: people leaving jobs that still exist, because the work no longer feels like theirs.

Core version

Imagine you are good at your work. You have spent ten years learning to do it well. Then a new tool arrives, and within a few months the job feels like something else. You still have it. You still get paid. But the part you used to enjoy has quietly disappeared.

That is the situation described by several workers in a report in The Guardian. Nobody made them redundant. They all chose to leave.

Chris, who did not want his real name printed, spent more than ten years as a software engineer. He said artificial intelligence had taken a lot of the thinking and the fun out of writing code, and he was worried about being replaced. He left and joined the police in a large American city. Working directly with people in his community, he said, gives him a satisfaction he never found in engineering.

Kara, who also used a different name, was an advertising producer. Her company told her team to use AI tools and, in the same conversation, warned them about the legal dangers. She was the person responsible for checking that everything was allowed. The stress never stopped. She left to concentrate on acting and took a job in a coffee shop, and she said the change made her feel lighter.

Another worker, Hanna Horvath, left an editing job to work for herself. She made an important point. AI does not have to take your job in order to change how you feel about it. Many editors, she said, had stopped editing and had become managers of work that a machine produced.

It is worth being careful with the numbers. There is no evidence that AI is causing mass job losses. The Economist reports that AI has created about a million jobs in the United States, against about 200,000 job losses blamed on it. But 71% of Americans told the Pew Research Center that they expect AI to mean fewer jobs over the next twenty years.

So these workers are not only responding to what has happened. They are responding to what they have been told will happen.

Intermediate version

When people discuss AI and employment, the question is usually simple. Will a machine replace me? There is a second question that gets asked far less often, and for some workers it has already been answered. What happens if the job survives, and the part of it you loved does not?

A Guardian report spoke to white-collar professionals who have left established careers. None of them was pushed out. They went voluntarily, into work they believed was either more secure or closer to what they actually valued.

Chris, who asked for a pseudonym, had spent more than a decade in software engineering. He said AI had removed much of the thinking and the enjoyment from coding, and that he expected to be displaced eventually. He now works as a police officer in a major east coast city, and describes a satisfaction in dealing with his community that engineering never gave him.

Kara, also using a different name, produced advertising. Her employer pushed the team towards AI video and voice tools, then warned them about the legal dangers in the same conversation. As producer, she was the one responsible for clearance at a moment when AI-generated likenesses remain a legal grey area. She left for acting, supported by shifts as a barista, and said the decision made her feel lighter.

Vanessa Hsia left public relations after nearly ten years to study comparative literature. She described an industry chasing quantity instead of quality and reaching for AI instead of thinking. The promise she had been sold, that working hard would bring respect and a fair wage, had stopped looking guaranteed to her.

The most useful observation came from Hanna Horvath, who left an editing job at a personal finance company. AI, she pointed out, does not have to replace your job in order to change your relationship with it. Her colleagues, she said, had turned into project managers for material the machine generated.

The evidence does not support panic, and the article is careful about this. The Economist counts roughly a million American jobs created by AI against about 200,000 losses attributed to it. There is no sign of mass redundancy. What has changed is expectation: 71% of Americans told the Pew Research Center they believe the technology will mean fewer jobs within twenty years.

Brendan Moore, an economist at the University of Notre Dame, suggests those fears come from the warnings of technology executives and from layoffs that companies blame on AI, some of which would probably have happened regardless.

A prediction does not need to come true to change a life. These workers acted on one.

Advanced version

The familiar story about artificial intelligence and work ends with somebody being replaced. A quieter story is unfolding alongside it, and it has a more uncomfortable shape: the job remains, the salary is intact, and the person leaves anyway.

The Guardian's report opens with Mrinank Sharma, an AI researcher who resigned from Anthropic this year and published his resignation letter on X, where it was read more than fifteen million times. He wrote of a world in peril. He also announced his next move, which was to study for a degree in poetry. Since then, the paper reports, workers well outside the technology industry have been reaching similar conclusions by different routes.

Their accounts share a structure. Vanessa Hsia spent nearly a decade in public relations before leaving for a master's in comparative literature, describing an industry chasing quantity over quality and substituting AI for critical thinking. An anonymous engineer at a large technology company said the career he had built over ten years was, in his words, fundamentally gone. Chris, who requested a pseudonym, moved from software engineering into policing after AI drained the thinking and the enjoyment out of writing code.

Hanna Horvath supplies the sentence that organises all of it. AI, she argues, "doesn't have to replace your job" to change what the job is. Having left an editing role, she described colleagues who no longer edited anything and had instead become project managers supervising machine-produced text. That is not redundancy. It is a quiet substitution of one occupation for another behind an unchanged job title.

The economic picture refuses to cooperate with the fear. The Economist puts roughly a million American jobs created by AI against some 200,000 losses attributed to it. Dario Amodei, Anthropic's chief executive, predicted in May 2025 that AI would eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs before the decade ended. That has not happened. Brendan Moore, an economist at Notre Dame, points out that workers are absorbing warnings from technology executives alongside layoffs that firms attribute to AI, when many of those companies would have cut staff anyway.

So the fear is running ahead of the evidence, and it is still producing consequences. Seventy-one per cent of Americans expect fewer jobs within twenty years, and some of them are not waiting to find out. There is something almost rational in it: if you believe your field is closing, leaving early while you still have the choice is a defensible move.

The harder question sits underneath. If a tool removes the most absorbing part of a role, who decides whether that is a gain? Efficiency and loss can be the same event viewed from two desks. The time a machine saves can be spent making the work better or simply producing more of it, and very few of the people doing the work are being asked which.
