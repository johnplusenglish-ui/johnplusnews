Core version

You fill a bag with clothes you no longer wear and leave it at a charity shop. It feels like a good ending: the shop can raise money and someone else can enjoy the clothes. Then the bag is opened. One top is damaged, another is made from poor material and a third is unlikely to sell. Instead of earning money for the charity, some of your donation may cost it money to remove.

That is happening more often as charity shops receive more clothes than they can sell. In October 2026, one charity operation in south London said it received as many as 100 bags a day. Only about one quarter of the items were suitable for resale. Staff still had to sort through everything, using time and money that could have supported the charity's main work.

This problem is connected to fast fashion, which means producing inexpensive new clothing styles very quickly. Social media helps trends catch on, and shoppers are encouraged to buy things they may not need. Research by Oxfam in 2026 found that 32% of clothes in British wardrobes were never worn. Making all those clothes requires water, energy and materials, even if they are hardly ever used.

There are ways to reduce waste. People can buy fewer clothes, choose items that last longer, repair small problems and use second-hand shops. But recycling clothes is often difficult, especially when different materials are mixed together. From July 2026, large companies in the European Union were also banned from destroying most unsold clothing and shoes, with some exceptions.

Affordable clothes are important, especially for families on limited budgets. The answer cannot simply be to tell everyone to buy expensive brands. Clothing companies also need to make better products and avoid creating more waste than necessary. Before buying something new, it may help to ask whether you will still want to wear it next year, though that is easier for some households than others.

Intermediate version

A bag left outside a charity shop carries a comforting promise: these clothes will find new owners and help a good cause. Inside the sorting room, the story can be very different. Staff open the bag and discover damaged seams, weak fabric and garments with almost no resale value. The donation has not removed the waste. It has transferred the difficult decision, and sometimes the disposal bill, to the charity.

A hospice charity in south London told a reporter that up to 100 bags can arrive in a single day. Only around a quarter of the items inside were suitable for sale. Every item still had to be checked and handled. Meanwhile, resale apps allow people to sell their most desirable pieces directly, potentially leaving charity shops with a greater share of the clothes nobody wants. A generous gesture can therefore become an unexpected expense.

The situation reflects the growth of fast fashion, an industry built around low prices and rapidly changing trends. Clothing can be advertised online, bought within seconds and replaced before it has worn out. Social media encourages impulse purchases by showing shoppers a constant stream of new looks. In its 2026 State of Style report, Oxfam found that 64% of people surveyed in Britain had bought clothes they were not actively looking for after seeing something online. The charity also estimated that nearly a third of clothing in British wardrobes was never worn.

That creates an enormous environmental cost. Producing garments requires water, energy and raw materials. Transport and packaging add to the impact, while synthetic fabrics may cause further problems when washed or discarded. Recycling is not always possible because many garments contain combinations of fibres that are difficult to separate. Donating unwanted clothing is better than throwing away usable items, but it does not undo the resources used to manufacture them.

Governments have begun to respond. Since July 2026, large companies in the EU have been prohibited from destroying most unsold clothes and shoes, with limited exceptions for goods that cannot safely be reused or sold. The rules encourage businesses to repair, donate or resell products instead. However, preventing destruction does not necessarily reduce the number of cheap garments being produced in the first place.

Consumers can make a difference by cutting back on new purchases, repairing clothes and choosing second-hand items, but it would be unfair to place all responsibility on shoppers. Affordable clothing matters, and not everyone can pay more for supposedly sustainable brands. Retailers must also consider durability and the volume of new products they put out. The simplest question may be one we rarely ask while shopping: will this still be worth wearing after the excitement of buying it has disappeared?

Advanced version

The donation bag provides a reassuring ending to the story of an unwanted garment. The wardrobe is clearer, the clothes avoid the bin and a charity may earn money. In the sorting room, that neat narrative can collapse. British charity shops report being inundated with clothing that is too damaged or too poor in quality to sell. The consumer has passed the garment on, but the social and environmental cost has not disappeared; it has simply arrived at somebody else's door.

The pressure is visible at St Raphael's Hospice in south London, where reporting in October 2026 described as many as 100 bags arriving each day and only a minority of their contents being fit for sale. Disposal costs eat into charitable income. At the same time, resale platforms encourage people to sell desirable pieces themselves, leaving shops to inherit more of the garments with little value. The charity sector is absorbing part of the cost of fast fashion's constant replacement cycle. Charities, in effect, are picking up the bill for decisions taken long before the bag reached their door.

Oxfam's 2026 State of Style report illustrates how consumption has become entwined with digital influence. Nearly two-thirds of people surveyed had purchased clothing they had not intended to buy after encountering it online, and 32% of items in British wardrobes were reportedly never worn. Among younger adults, algorithmic recommendations exerted an especially strong influence. Clothing is increasingly marketed not as a durable possession but as a quick response to a passing mood, event or online trend. Durability, on that model, is close to irrelevant.

The environmental consequences extend well beyond household clutter. Textile production consumes substantial water, energy and raw materials, while synthetic fibres and complex fabric blends complicate recycling. A garment may move from retailer to wardrobe to donation bag within months, despite having required extensive resources to manufacture and transport. Resale and donation can prolong the useful life of clothing, but they cannot make excessive production environmentally harmless. Every additional garment carries a cost that no amount of reselling can write off.

European policymakers have begun to intervene. Since July 2026, large companies have faced a prohibition on destroying most unsold apparel and footwear, with defined exceptions. The measure is intended to encourage resale, repair and reuse rather than disposal of perfectly serviceable goods. It marks a shift towards holding businesses responsible for what happens after products fail to sell, although it does not directly challenge the relentless pace at which new collections are launched.

Cheap clothes meet a real need, and buying fewer, more expensive items is not an option for every household. Shoppers also have limited control over how well clothes are made. Manufacturers choose materials, shops encourage purchases and governments decide which disposal costs businesses bear. Charity shops are dealing with the result. They cannot solve overproduction on their own.

9Role-play: what to do with the old clothes

Work in pairs. Read your card, then have the conversation. Swap roles and do it again.

Student A · the housemate who wants to donate

You and your housemate have six bags of old clothes. You want to give everything to a charity shop this weekend. Explain why, and listen to your housemate's idea.

Student B · the housemate who wants to sell

You want to sell the best clothes online and give the rest to charity. Explain why, and ask what the charity shop will do with damaged items. Try to agree on a plan.

Useful phrases

  • I'd like to give everything to the charity shop.
  • That's a fair point, but it'll take a lot of time.
  • What if we sell the best pieces online?
  • I'm not keen on that, because it's a lot of work.
  • How about we split the bags in two?
  • Let's check every item before we decide.

Afterwards, tell the class: what did you decide to do with the clothes, and why?
