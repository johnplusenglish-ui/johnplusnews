Fast Fashion: The Clothes Nobody Wants

Donated clothes are piling up in charity shops, and much of it cannot be sold. What is fast fashion doing to the clothes we no longer want?

Before you read3 questions
  1. How often do you buy new clothes?
  2. What do you do with clothes you no longer wear?
  3. Have you ever regretted an online clothing purchase?

Core version

It's easy to buy cheap clothes online. You see a jacket, like the colour and order it in a few seconds. A week later, you might find something else you prefer. When the jacket no longer interests you, you put it in a bag for a charity shop. You may feel pleased that someone else will use it, but the story does not always end that way.

Many charity shops now receive more clothes than they can sell. Some donations are damaged or made from poor-quality material. Shop workers must spend time checking everything, and items that cannot be sold may cost money to remove. In October 2026, a report described how one charity shop operation in south London received up to 100 bags of donations a day. Only about one quarter of the items were suitable for resale.

This problem is connected to fast fashion, which means producing inexpensive new clothing styles very quickly. Social media helps trends spread, and shoppers are encouraged to buy things they may not need. Research by Oxfam in 2026 found that 32% of clothes in British wardrobes were never worn. Making all those clothes requires water, energy and materials, even if they are hardly ever used.

There are ways to reduce waste. People can buy fewer clothes, choose items that last longer, repair small problems and use second-hand shops. But recycling clothes is often difficult, especially when different materials are mixed together. From July 2026, large companies in the European Union were also banned from destroying most unsold clothing and shoes, with some exceptions.

Affordable clothes are important, especially for families on limited budgets. The answer cannot simply be to tell everyone to buy expensive brands. Clothing companies also need to make better products and avoid creating more waste than necessary. Before buying something new, it may help to ask whether you will still want to wear it next year. A cheap T-shirt is not always a bargain if it is only used once.

Intermediate version

The bag of clothes outside a charity shop looks like a small good deed. Someone has cleared out a wardrobe, hoping the items will find new owners and raise money for a good cause. Yet charities are increasingly discovering that many donations cannot be sold. Cheap, damaged or poorly made clothes may end up costing money to dispose of, turning a generous gesture into an unexpected expense.

In October 2026, reporting from a hospice charity in south London described donations arriving in enormous quantities. Staff sometimes received up to 100 bags a day, while only around a quarter of the contents were suitable for resale. The rest had to be sorted and dealt with, using staff time and money that could otherwise support charitable services. Meanwhile, resale apps allow people to sell their best-quality items directly, which may leave charity shops with a greater share of unwanted clothing.

The situation reflects the growth of fast fashion, an industry built around low prices and rapidly changing trends. Clothing can be advertised online, bought within seconds and replaced before it has worn out. Social media encourages impulse purchases by showing shoppers a constant stream of new looks. In its 2026 State of Style report, Oxfam found that 64% of people surveyed in Britain had bought clothes they were not actively looking for after seeing something online. The charity also estimated that nearly a third of clothing in British wardrobes was never worn.

That creates an enormous environmental cost. Producing garments requires water, energy and raw materials. Transport and packaging add to the impact, while synthetic fabrics may cause further problems when washed or discarded. Recycling is not always possible because many garments contain combinations of fibres that are difficult to separate. Donating unwanted clothing is better than throwing away usable items, but it does not undo the resources used to manufacture them.

Governments have begun to respond. Since July 2026, large companies in the EU have been prohibited from destroying most unsold clothes and shoes, with limited exceptions for goods that cannot safely be reused or sold. The rules encourage businesses to repair, donate or resell products instead. However, preventing destruction does not necessarily reduce the number of cheap garments being produced in the first place.

Consumers can make a difference by buying less often, repairing clothes and choosing second-hand items, but it would be unfair to place all responsibility on shoppers. Affordable clothing matters, and not everyone can pay more for supposedly sustainable brands. Retailers must also consider durability and the volume of new products they introduce. The simplest question may be one we rarely ask while shopping: will this still be worth wearing after the excitement of buying it has disappeared?

Advanced version

The clothing donation bag has acquired an almost reassuring place in modern consumer life. An unwanted garment can be passed on, perhaps raising money for a charity and avoiding the waste bin. Yet this neat narrative is becoming increasingly difficult to sustain. British charity shops report being inundated with low-quality donations, some of which are so worn or unsuitable that they cost more to handle than they could ever earn through resale.

Reporting in October 2026 described the pressure on St Raphael's Hospice in south London, where staff may receive as many as 100 bags of donations a day, with only a minority of items fit for sale. Disposal costs divert money away from charitable work, while the growth of second-hand platforms has changed what people choose to donate. Desirable pieces can be sold directly through apps; shops may consequently inherit a greater share of garments with little commercial value. The charity sector is absorbing costs generated partly by an industry that thrives on encouraging constant replacement.

Oxfam's 2026 State of Style report illustrates how consumption has become entwined with digital influence. Nearly two-thirds of people surveyed had purchased clothing they had not intended to buy after encountering it online, and 32% of items in British wardrobes were reportedly never worn. Among younger adults, algorithmic recommendations exerted an especially strong influence. Clothing is increasingly marketed not as a durable possession but as a quick response to a passing mood, event or online trend.

The environmental consequences extend well beyond household clutter. Textile production consumes substantial water, energy and raw materials, while synthetic fibres and complex fabric blends complicate recycling. A garment may move from retailer to wardrobe to donation bag within months, despite having required extensive resources to manufacture and transport. Resale and donation can prolong the useful life of clothing, but they cannot make excessive production environmentally harmless.

European policymakers have begun to intervene. Since July 2026, large companies have faced a prohibition on destroying most unsold apparel and footwear, with defined exceptions. The measure is intended to encourage resale, repair and reuse rather than disposal of perfectly serviceable goods. It marks a shift towards holding businesses responsible for what happens after products fail to sell, although it does not directly challenge the relentless pace at which new collections are launched.

There is a danger in presenting individual shoppers as the sole cause of the problem. Low-cost clothing serves a real need, particularly for households facing financial constraints, and buying fewer but more expensive garments is not a realistic prescription for everyone. Manufacturers determine durability, retailers shape purchasing behaviour and policymakers decide which costs businesses must bear. The central question is therefore not whether charity shops can find a better way to deal with discarded fashion, but whether the industry can succeed without producing so much clothing that almost nobody wants to keep.
