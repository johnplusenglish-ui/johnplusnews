Core version

Imagine your favourite bag has a small hole. You have two choices. You can hide the hole with a stitch that nobody can see. Or you can cover it with a bright patch that everybody can see. Which choice feels more like you?

The public library in San Francisco, in the United States, advertised a workshop for teenagers aged 13 to 18. It was listed for 19 April 2026 and called "Mending and Upcycling". The teacher was Lucy Ornelas, a textile artist who teaches a fashion design class for a youth arts group called YAX.

The listing said that teenagers would learn three skills: visible mending, patching and hand-stitching. Visible mending means that you don't hide a repair. You make it part of the design. A patch is a piece of cloth that covers a hole. Hand-stitching means sewing with a needle and thread, with no machine.

People were asked to bring one small item that needed repair, no bigger than a hand. The goal was to give clothes a new life and to make less textile waste. Upcycling means turning something old into something better or more useful. Space in the workshop was limited.

A repair can be different things. You can mend a hole so the bag works again. Or you can give the bag a new look, with a patch in a surprising colour. Some people like repairs to be invisible. Other people like them to show, because the repair tells a story.

This was an event listing for a planned workshop, so it tells us what the library advertised. It doesn't tell us how many people came, what they thought or how much waste they saved. And not everyone loves visible repairs.

So back to your bag. Would you hide the repair or show it? And what would a patch say about you?

Intermediate version

Your favourite bag has a small hole in the bottom. You could hide the damage with a careful stitch, cover it with a bright patch, or buy a new bag. Each choice says something slightly different about you. Which one would you pick?

The San Francisco Public Library advertised a workshop for ages 13 to 18, listed for Sunday 19 April 2026 and called "Mending and Upcycling with Youth Art Exchange". Lucy Ornelas, a textile artist who teaches fashion design for the youth arts group YAX, was to lead it.

According to the listing, teenagers would learn essential textile repair skills, including visible mending, patching and hand-stitching. They were encouraged to bring one textile item that needed repair, no larger than the palm of a hand. The stated goals were to upcycle clothing and reduce textile waste, and space was limited. The listing also credited the library's Youth Advisory Board interns, who helped plan programmes for young people.

Two ideas are worth separating. Mending repairs damage so that something works again, and it can be invisible. Upcycling changes what an object is for, or makes it better than it was, so a torn T-shirt might become a bag. Visible mending sits between them. It fixes the damage, but it shows the fix as part of the design.

That is why a repair can say something. A neat invisible stitch says that nothing happened. A bright patch says that the object has a history and that someone chose how to deal with it. Some people find that more interesting than a new item, while others prefer clothes that look as if nothing has ever happened to them. Neither taste is wrong.

Remember what the source is. It's an event listing, so it tells us what was advertised, not how many people attended, what they thought or how much waste they saved. We shouldn't assume that repaired clothes are embarrassing or that expensive clothes are needed to take part.

So here is a question for your own wardrobe. If one item had a small tear, would you hide the repair, make it a feature or choose another solution? And what would you want that choice to say about you?

Advanced version

A tear can disappear under a careful repair, or become the most noticeable part of a bag. A bright patch makes a choice visible: someone decided to keep the item and change its look. A mending workshop offers the tools for doing that. What the repaired piece should look like is still up to its owner.

The occasion for it is modest. The San Francisco Public Library listed a workshop for ages 13 to 18 on Sunday 19 April 2026, called "Mending and Upcycling with Youth Art Exchange". Lucy Ornelas, a San Francisco textile artist and instructor for the Fashion Design class at YAX, a nonprofit that offers free after-school arts programmes to public high school students, was to teach it.

According to the listing, participants would learn essential textile repair skills, including visible mending, patching and hand-stitching, and were encouraged to bring a textile item that needed repair, no larger than the palm of a hand. The stated goals were to upcycle clothing and reduce textile waste. It also mentions the library's Youth Advisory Board interns, who had spent the school year planning programmes for young people.

The vocabulary of repair is worth unpacking. Mending restores function. Upcycling changes purpose or raises an object's status, so a damaged item becomes something new. Visible mending does something slightly different: it keeps the original purpose but refuses to hide the damage. It turns a flaw into a decision, which is why it appeals to people who like objects to carry their history.

But that taste isn't neutral. When a person who can easily afford new clothes wears a visible repair, it can read as a choice. When a person with less money wears the same repair, others may read it as necessity. The viewer's assumptions do a lot of work here, which is why a workshop that teaches the skill is only part of the story. Who is allowed to look creative, and who is assumed to be struggling?

The limits of the source matter. A listing says what was advertised. It doesn't report how many people came, what they made or thought, or how much waste was reduced, and it says nothing about whether the participants agreed about any of this. Nor should we assume that repaired clothes are embarrassing, or that expensive clothes are needed to take part.

What it does give us is a practical, low-cost way to ask about taste. So if you owned one item with a small tear, would you hide the repair, make it a feature or choose another solution? And who are you imagining when you decide how it should look?
