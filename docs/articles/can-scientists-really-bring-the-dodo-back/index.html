Colossal Biosciences has rebuilt most of the dodo's genome and hopes to edit a Nicobar pigeon. Critics ask what the finished bird would actually be.

Core version

The dodo was a large bird that could not fly. It lived on the island of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean until it died out in the seventeenth century. Today, the bird is famous as a symbol of extinction. But scientists at an American biotechnology company are trying to do something extraordinary: create a living bird with some of the dodo's characteristics.

The company, Colossal Biosciences, uses genetic technology to study animals that no longer exist. Genes are the instructions inside a living thing that help control how it grows and develops. In October 2026, the company announced progress in its dodo project. It said it had rebuilt most of the dodo's genetic information and developed tools to find the genes linked to features such as the shape of its head and beak. It hopes to make careful changes to a living relative of the dodo.

That living relative is the Nicobar pigeon. It is a colourful bird found on islands in Asia, and scientists believe it is the dodo's closest living relative. The basic idea is to study the genetic differences between the birds and change some of the pigeon's genes.

However, this is not like bringing an original dodo back from the dead. The scientists have not produced a living dodo, and nobody can simply copy one from an old bone. Even if the project succeeds, the result may be a new bird that resembles a dodo in some ways, rather than exactly the same species that disappeared centuries ago.

Some conservation experts think the research could lead to useful new ways of protecting endangered animals. Others believe that the money would be better spent saving species that are still alive. They also ask where a new dodo-like bird would live. The original birds disappeared after people arrived on Mauritius and introduced animals that disturbed their habitat. Any future bird would need a safe place to survive.

For now, the dodo remains extinct. Colossal's announcement is about progress in genetic research, not a successful return. But the project raises an interesting question. If humans helped cause an animal's disappearance, should we try to bring it back, or should we focus on preventing the next extinction? The answer could shape how conservation works for many years to come.

Intermediate version

The dodo has been extinct for more than three centuries, yet it may be one of the most recognisable birds in history. The large, flightless creature once lived on Mauritius, where hunting and animals introduced by humans helped to wipe it out. Now a US biotechnology company wants to create a living bird with some of the dodo's distinctive features. Whether that would count as bringing the dodo back is another question.

Colossal Biosciences announced fresh progress in October 2026. The company said it had reconstructed about 95% of the dodo's genome, the collection of genetic instructions that helped determine how the bird developed. It is also using an AI-assisted system to pick out changes linked to features such as the dodo's unusually shaped beak. Researchers hope to introduce selected changes into the genes of a living relative, the Nicobar pigeon.

This is where the idea becomes complicated. The Nicobar pigeon is not a dodo, even though the species share an evolutionary history. Changing some of its genes could eventually produce a bird that looks or behaves more like the extinct species. But it would not necessarily recreate the original animal, complete with the same behaviour, development and place in the ecosystem. Colossal has not yet produced a living dodo or dodo-like bird through this programme.

Supporters argue that research into gene editing and reproduction could produce tools useful for conservation. If scientists become better at protecting genetic diversity or helping endangered birds reproduce, living species may benefit. The company is also working with people in Mauritius to explore how suitable habitat might be restored. Without a safe environment, even an impressive scientific achievement would have little chance of surviving outside a laboratory.

Critics question the priorities. De-extinction projects attract publicity and investment partly because famous animals are involved. Meanwhile, many less glamorous species are disappearing right now. Some researchers also object to the word 'resurrection', which suggests an exact return that current technology cannot deliver. They argue that a genetically modified relative would be a new creation rather than the original species brought back to life.

The dodo project is only beginning to take shape and remains an experiment with an uncertain outcome. Its value may eventually lie in techniques developed along the way rather than in producing a perfect copy of an extinct bird. Even so, the ambition forces us to confront an uncomfortable question about conservation. If we can spend enormous resources trying to reverse one historic loss, how much should we spend preventing the ordinary, less famous losses taking place around us today?

Advanced version

The dodo is the bird everyone knows is gone. A large, flightless species that vanished from Mauritius in the seventeenth century, it has become shorthand for extinction. In October 2026, the US-based Colossal Biosciences announced further progress towards engineering a bird with some of the dodo's defining characteristics. The ambition is striking. What success would actually mean is much less clear.

Colossal says it has reconstructed about 95% of the extinct bird's genome, the full set of its genetic instructions, and developed an AI-assisted system to identify the genetic changes linked to features such as the unusual shape of its head and beak. Its proposed route involves introducing selected changes into a living relative, the Nicobar pigeon, and addressing the formidable reproductive challenges involved in producing genetically modified birds. These are genuine areas of technical research, but no recreated dodo has emerged, and the distance between identifying promising genetic targets and producing a viable animal remains considerable. Nor is that a gap which funding and publicity alone will bridge.

The deeper difficulty is conceptual. Scientists may be able to piece together a genetic approximation of the bird, but a species is not simply a list of instructions waiting to be reassembled. Genes interact with development, behaviour, environment and the experience of generations. A bird engineered to resemble the dodo might share some physical characteristics with its extinct ancestor without reproducing its full biology or ecological role.

For this reason, a number of evolutionary biologists argue that 'de-extinction' is a misleading description. A proxy species, however remarkable, should not be confused with the recovery of an organism that has genuinely vanished.

There is also a practical question about what such a bird would do once created. Mauritius has changed profoundly since the dodo's disappearance. Introduced predators, habitat destruction and other human influences helped drive the original species towards extinction. Releasing a dodo-like bird without grappling with those pressures would make little ecological sense.

Colossal says it is collaborating with Mauritian partners on habitat preparation, a reminder that restoration depends as much on ordinary conservation work as on sophisticated genetics. Habitat preparation is slow, unglamorous work, and it would have to succeed before any engineered bird could be released with any prospect of surviving.

Advocates see wider benefits in the research. Methods developed for gene editing, reproduction and the preservation of genetic diversity might eventually help threatened species. Critics counter that spectacular resurrection projects attract disproportionate attention and resources while existing wildlife faces immediate and measurable dangers. Both arguments have merit, although the potential benefits of experimental biotechnology should not be mistaken for benefits already demonstrated in conservation programmes.

For now, the dodo belongs to history rather than to the forests of Mauritius. The company has advanced aspects of a research programme; it has not done away with extinction. Perhaps the most useful contribution of such ambitious science will be to sharpen rather than dissolve the distinction between restoration and imitation. A convincing likeness could be a remarkable scientific achievement. Whether it would repair the ecological loss that made the dodo famous is an altogether more demanding question.
