What Happens if the Advice Turns Out to Be Wrong?

A poor restaurant suggestion costs you an evening. A mistaken medical or financial choice is far harder to undo, and a chatbot only knows the version of events you typed.

Core version

You have two job offers and cannot decide which one to accept. One pays more, but the other sounds more interesting. You ask an AI chatbot for help. It quickly sets out the advantages and disadvantages. Should you follow its advice?

AI tools can explain difficult information, compare options and help us come up with questions. But giving information is different from making a decision for someone. A computer may not understand your family, personality or feelings as well as a close friend does.

In a 2025 survey, Pew Research Center asked Americans about different uses of AI. Many people supported using it for tasks such as weather forecasting and medical research. They were much less comfortable with AI taking a role in very personal decisions, including deciding whether two people should be together.

There are practical risks too. AI can make mistakes or sound confident when its information is wrong. If someone asks about a serious health problem or an important financial choice, acting on an incorrect answer could cause harm.

This does not mean we should give up on AI completely. It might help us prepare for a conversation with a doctor, compare questions to ask an employer or understand the meaning of a document. We can use its ideas and still look up important facts or check them with qualified people.

The way we ask AI questions can make a big difference. Suppose you are thinking of moving to another country. Instead of asking, 'Should I move?', you could ask, 'What costs should I think about before moving?' You could then check the answers on official websites and talk to people who have made a similar move. This keeps the tool in a helpful role.

The same idea works for money and health questions: ask for explanations or a list of questions to take to a professional, not an answer you must follow. We should also remember that chatbots may not know when our information is incomplete. A friend might stop and ask why we want to make a change. A chatbot may give a detailed answer before it understands the whole situation.

Perhaps the best role for AI is not to tell us what to do. It is to help us understand our choices more clearly. The final responsibility still belongs to us, especially when the decision could change our lives.

Intermediate version

Imagine asking an AI chatbot whether to move abroad, end a relationship or accept a new job. Within seconds it produces a convincing answer, carefully organised into advantages and disadvantages. The advice may be useful, but how much authority should we give it when the consequences are personal?

A useful test is to ask what happens if the recommendation turns out to be wrong. A poor restaurant suggestion is inconvenient; a mistaken medical or financial decision can be much harder to undo.

Pew Research Center surveyed more than 5,000 US adults in June 2025 and published the findings that September. Respondents were more willing to accept AI involvement in analysing complex information, such as forecasting weather or developing medicines, than in intimate areas of life. A clear majority opposed AI playing a role in judging whether two people could fall in love.

The distinction makes sense. AI can process large quantities of text and identify patterns, but it does not have human experience or direct knowledge of someone's circumstances. It may also provide inaccurate information in an extremely confident tone. An answer that sounds compassionate is not necessarily reliable.

Nevertheless, ruling out AI altogether would mean losing some genuinely useful support. Someone facing a difficult career decision could ask a chatbot to point out considerations they had overlooked. A patient could use it to understand medical terminology before speaking to a doctor. In both cases, the tool can improve preparation without replacing professional judgement. If you ask it about a complicated relationship, it only knows the version of events you have chosen to type.

There are questions about privacy as well. Sharing intimate relationship problems, financial details or health information with a digital service may expose sensitive material. Users should understand how their data may be handled before treating a chatbot like a confidential adviser.

One way to reduce the risks is to improve the questions we ask. A person considering a move abroad could request a checklist of costs and practical issues rather than asking a chatbot to decide whether they should leave. Someone facing a complicated medical appointment could ask for an explanation of unfamiliar terms and then confirm it with their clinician.

These uses treat AI as a tool for preparing decisions, not as a substitute for the people responsible for them. Users should also check official sources, question unsupported claims and avoid handing over unnecessary personal information. A fluent answer can hide important gaps: the chatbot may not know local regulations, family circumstances or recent changes. The ability to challenge its output is therefore part of using it intelligently.

The central issue is not whether AI is intelligent enough to have an opinion. It is whether we understand its limitations. A sensible approach may be to ask AI better questions rather than allow it to make our decisions for us.

Advanced version

An AI chatbot can seem like an ideal adviser when a decision feels difficult: it replies instantly, organises your options and never seems impatient. The help may be useful, but a confident answer can still miss important details. Its neat presentation should not settle the decision for you.

Pew Research Center surveyed 5,023 US adults in June 2025 and published the findings that September. Respondents were more open to AI helping with analytical tasks, such as weather forecasting, than with intimate personal matters. Roughly two-thirds thought AI should have no role in judging whether two people could fall in love. Those findings describe US opinion, rather than what the technology can reliably do.

Practical value and decision-making authority should be separated. AI might help a prospective employee identify questions about a contract, or help a patient understand unfamiliar medical language. Such uses are different from trusting a chatbot to recommend a particular treatment, relationship decision or risky investment without qualified human oversight. But fluent language is not the same as sound judgement. An AI system may overlook missing information, reproduce biases or present an uncertain suggestion with more confidence than it deserves.

The technology can be inaccurate, may lack crucial context and sometimes presents uncertainty poorly. An additional concern is privacy: people may disclose information to a chatbot that they would hesitate to share with almost anybody else. The convenience of an apparently attentive conversational partner can obscure how the service handles sensitive data.

There is a counterargument. Human advisers can be biased, impatient, expensive or unavailable. For those who cannot easily fall back on professional expertise, a digital tool that helps them explore possibilities may be valuable. The solution is therefore unlikely to lie in ruling out AI assistance altogether. AI can help someone find questions they had missed. The answers still need checking, and serious decisions may need help from someone qualified to advise.

Effective use of AI depends partly on the distinction between generating options and selecting among them. A chatbot might identify practical considerations for a career change, explain legal terminology or help a patient prepare questions for a consultation. These tasks can improve the quality of subsequent decisions without requiring the system to make them.

The greater danger arises when its polished presentation glosses over missing context, uncertain evidence or errors in factual assumptions. People may mistake a coherent argument for one grounded in their specific circumstances. A robust approach therefore combines independent verification, appropriate expert advice and careful handling of private information. It also requires resisting the temptation to regard apparent empathy as evidence that the system understands personal consequences in the way a human adviser might.

What matters is maintaining a distinction between support and delegation. A useful system should make its limitations visible and encourage verification. Before a serious decision, a person could ask a chatbot for a checklist of questions, then take that list to someone qualified to answer them.
