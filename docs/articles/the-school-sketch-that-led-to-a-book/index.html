Core version

Imagine you are in the audience at a school event. A storyteller is on the stage. You do not want to speak in front of everyone, but you do want to do something. So you take out a pencil and start to draw.

That is what Bethany did. She is 15 and lives in Cambridgeshire, in England. An author called Chip Colquhoun visited her school to tell stories. Bethany drew a portrait of him while she listened. She says that drawing helped her to focus.

Colquhoun loved the drawing. He said that it wowed him. He used her portrait in one of his books for teachers. Later, he asked her to draw the pictures for a new children's book, The Bestest Story in the World Ever! The book came out in September 2026. Bethany says it felt strange and unreal to see her pictures in print.

Bethany finds it very hard to talk to some people, especially people she does not know well. This is called situational mutism. She says that illustrating has given her a lot more confidence. She can now do things that she could not do a few years ago. Her mother says that Bethany still needs support, but that she now has the chance to feel proud.

Bethany is now working on a third book. She often draws in the evening and does her schoolwork earlier in the day.

What can we learn? A person who is quiet may have a lot to say in another way. Think about how you could encourage a friend who makes something. A kind and specific comment, like "I love the colours here", is better than a vague "good job". What would you say?

Intermediate version

Imagine you are sitting in the audience at a school storytelling session. You would rather not speak in front of everyone, but you do not want to sit with nothing to do either. So you take out a pencil and start to draw.

That is how Bethany, a 15-year-old from Cambridgeshire, began a project that became a real book. When the author Chip Colquhoun visited her school to give a storytelling session, she drew a portrait of him. She told BBC Radio Cambridgeshire that she drew it to concentrate. She needed something to draw, and he was right there.

Colquhoun was impressed. He said that her portrait wowed him, and that he was excited by the idea that she had used drawing to help her focus. He later used the portrait in one of his books, a guide for teachers on using storytelling. Then he asked her to illustrate his children's book The Bestest Story in the World Ever!, which was published in September 2026.

Bethany says that seeing her illustrations in print felt weird and unreal. She describes her first portrait as very stylised and says it is not the style she draws in now, although she thinks it was good for her skill level at the time.

The BBC report also explains that Bethany has situational mutism. In her words, she speaks to some people and not to others, mainly people she does not know or does not feel comfortable with. Her mother, Fiona, says that Bethany has full language skills but that in certain situations she cannot use them, and that it comes from anxiety. Bethany still cannot go anywhere outside the house or school on her own.

Bethany says that illustrating has improved her confidence a lot. According to her mother, she has been to two book launches and has been interviewed by two radio stations, with support. She is now working on a third book and on three drawings for another project. She usually draws in the evening and does her schoolwork earlier in the day.

It would be wrong to read this as a story in which art cured a problem. The report does not say that, and Bethany's mother is clear that support is still needed. What it does show is that a person who finds speaking hard may have another way to take part, and that being noticed in a way that suits her helped.

Think about a friend who makes something creative. How could you encourage them without putting them under pressure? A specific comment, a curious question and an optional suggestion can all help more than a vague "good job".

Advanced version

During a school storytelling visit, Bethany drew the author in front of her. She later told the BBC that drawing helped her concentrate. The author, Chip Colquhoun, noticed the portrait. That small exchange led to something she had not expected: an invitation to illustrate a book.

The girl, Bethany, was in the audience at a school storytelling session given by the author Chip Colquhoun. She drew a portrait of him. She told BBC Radio Cambridgeshire that she did it as a way to concentrate, because she needed something to draw and he was right there. Colquhoun said that the portrait wowed him, and that he was excited by the idea that she had used drawing to help her focus.

What followed was practical. He used the portrait in one of his books, a guide for teachers on using storytelling, and later asked her to illustrate his children's book The Bestest Story in the World Ever!, published in September 2026. Bethany says that seeing her work in print felt weird and unreal. She now describes that first portrait as very stylised, and says it is not the style she draws in today.

The report also gives context that is important for reading the story fairly. Bethany has situational mutism. She says that she speaks to some people and not to others, mainly people she does not know or does not feel comfortable with. Her mother, Fiona, says that Bethany has full language skills but that in some circumstances she cannot use them, and that it comes from anxiety. This is not rudeness, and it is not a choice.

Bethany says that illustrating has improved her confidence a great deal and made her able to do things that she would have been too nervous to do a few years ago. According to her mother, she has been to two book launches and has been interviewed by two radio stations, and she is now working on a third book and on three drawings for another project. Her mother is also clear about the limits: Bethany still cannot do these things alone, and she still cannot go anywhere outside the house or school without support.

This is not a story in which a talent solves a problem, and the report does not say it is. Creative success is not what makes a person deserve support. What the story does show is that when someone finds one way of taking part difficult, another may be available, and being noticed in a way that suits them can make a real difference.

It also shows something about the person who noticed. Colquhoun did not ask Bethany to speak. He looked at what she had made and answered it with a real job. Encouragement that responds to a person's actual work, in the form that suits them, is often more useful than general praise.

Try it

Imagine a classmate has drawn a picture. In pairs, write three pieces of feedback for the picture: one specific observation (for example, "I like how the colours change at the top"), one curious question and one optional suggestion. Then write a vague "good job" and compare. Which kind of note would help you most if it were your picture, and why? Use an invented drawing. Sharing personal artwork is always optional, and nobody has to talk about private matters.
