Could the Sauna Be a New Place to Meet?

Small cabins along the coast offer heat, a cold dip and conversation without alcohol. Regulars drop in at the same time each week, so familiar faces soon appear.

Core version

On a cold winter morning in Ireland, a group of people sit together inside a small wooden room. Outside, the wind blows across the sea and the water looks freezing. Inside, it is very hot. They are not at a gym or a luxury hotel. They have come to a sauna near the beach, and some of them have never met before.

Saunas have become much more popular in Ireland in recent years. Some are beside the sea, while others are in town centres or at special events. Visitors usually book a short session and pay for it before they arrive. They sit in the heat, talk quietly and sometimes go outside for a quick swim in cold water. Plenty of people turn up on their own and join in anyway.

For a long time, the pub has been an important place in Irish social life. Friends meet there after work, families celebrate special occasions and neighbours catch up on local news. But a pub does not suit everybody. Some people do not drink alcohol, others find the noise tiring, and a few simply want a different way to spend time together.

A sauna offers something unusual: people can talk without loud music, televisions or phones. Everyone puts their phone away before going in. They might discuss work, the weather or nothing important at all. A person who arrives alone may leave having spoken to several strangers. That is one reason some people see saunas as a new kind of community space, a relaxed place where it is easy to get to know somebody.

Of course, a sauna cannot replace everything a pub does. It costs money, the high temperatures are not suitable for everybody, and it is not a place to spend an entire evening. There is also a big difference between enjoying a new activity and making a friendship that lasts for years.

Still, the popularity of seaside saunas suggests that many people want more welcoming places where they can relax and meet others. Ireland is not necessarily saying goodbye to the pub. It may simply be finding another way to bring people together.

Intermediate version

It is a curious picture of modern Ireland: strangers sitting shoulder to shoulder in a wooden sauna while Atlantic waves crash a few metres away. Some have come for the heat, some for a cold-water dip, and others simply for a conversation. What they all share is a desire to wind down somewhere that is neither home nor work.

Saunas have been springing up along the Irish coast and in towns, often as compact cabins that can be booked for a short session. The tradition is strongly associated with Finland. In Ireland, its popularity has a distinctly local character: dramatic weather, sea swimming and the chance to exchange a few words with people you would otherwise never meet. Because a session is short, it is easy to fit around work or family, and nobody is signing up for a whole evening. Regulars tend to drop in at the same time each week, so familiar faces soon appear.

The trend has encouraged comparisons with the Irish pub. For generations, pubs have offered much more than alcohol. They have been places to celebrate, exchange news and keep friendships alive. Yet anyone who is avoiding alcohol, watching their spending or tired of noisy evenings may struggle to find a similarly welcoming alternative, particularly in a small town where the options are limited.

A shared sauna changes the usual rules of socialising. Phones are put away, people are dressed simply and nobody needs to organise a formal activity. Conversation can develop naturally, and it is unusually easy to strike up a conversation with a stranger when everyone is sitting still, waiting for the heat to build.

Social researchers sometimes call such informal gathering places "third places", meaning spaces outside the home and workplace that help communities stay connected. The argument behind the term is straightforward: without somewhere easy to drop into, people rely entirely on arranged meetings, and those tend to fall away as life gets busier.

That does not mean saunas are an answer to Ireland's loneliness problem. A paid session is not necessarily affordable, especially for anyone who would like to go every week, and the experience may be uncomfortable or medically unsuitable for some people. A fifteen-minute chat with a stranger is also very different from building a dependable social network, the kind that people fall back on when something goes wrong.

The interesting question is not whether saunas will replace pubs. It is why so many people welcome another way to gather. Perhaps the popularity of these cabins reflects a wider shift towards alcohol-free socialising, wellbeing and activities that feel a little less organised than everyday life. Perhaps it simply reflects how pleasant it is to be warm while the weather does its worst outside.

Advanced version

Picture a winter morning on Ireland's Atlantic coast. Rain moves across the horizon, the water is bitterly cold and, inside a timber cabin, half a dozen strangers are discussing everything from local gossip to their working lives. For some visitors, the attraction is company as well as heat. A shared cabin offers a place to talk without a drink in anyone's hand.

Sauna operators have found enthusiastic customers in coastal communities and urban areas alike. Sessions typically combine intense heat with time outdoors, sometimes including a brief plunge into the sea. The contrast between the two appears to be central to the attraction. While the ritual draws on northern European tradition, its appeal in Ireland seems to rest as much on sociability as on physical relaxation. What brings some people back may be the company as much as the temperature.

The pub remains a culturally significant institution, but it does not meet everybody's needs. Those abstaining from alcohol, or simply seeking a quieter atmosphere, may find that opportunities for informal face-to-face contact are surprisingly limited. The sauna offers an alternative setting in which conversation is neither the stated purpose nor an awkward interruption, and in which nobody is obliged to explain why they are not drinking. That absence of obligation is easily underestimated: in a pub, turning down a round invites comment, whereas in a sauna there is nothing to turn down.

Urban sociologists have long emphasised the importance of "third places": accessible environments beyond domestic and professional life where relationships develop without elaborate planning. A sauna is by no means accessible in the economic sense, since every visit has to be paid for, yet the shared physical experience does strip away some familiar social barriers. Titles and occupations count for little when everyone is sitting in a towel, waiting for the next wave of heat, and the usual markers of status give way to something more level. Conversation that would feel intrusive across an office desk becomes unremarkable.

There are limits to the comparison. Commercial wellness businesses cannot simply take over from enduring community institutions, and confident claims about health benefits should not disguise the fact that intense heat and cold-water immersion carry real risks for some individuals. Nor can an hour booked in advance quite replicate the unplanned dropping-in that made the pub a social institution in the first place. A pleasant encounter on a Saturday morning does not guarantee a deeper sense of belonging either. Warmth and sociability, agreeable though they are, are not the same thing as community.

Nevertheless, the trend raises a more substantial question than whether saunas are fashionable. If people increasingly seek out spaces free from screens, alcohol and organised entertainment, what does that reveal about the opportunities for connection in ordinary public life? The answer is unlikely to be especially flattering to the places most people pass through every day. Ireland's new social ritual may be telling us as much about what has quietly disappeared elsewhere as about the pleasures of getting warm.
