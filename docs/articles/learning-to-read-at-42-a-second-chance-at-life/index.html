Core version

Peter Lloyd could drive a lorry across Britain, remember complicated routes and look after his family. But there was one thing he could not do: read a food label. For most of his adult life, he kept this problem hidden. At the age of 46, an ordinary trip to the supermarket finally persuaded him to ask for help.

Peter grew up in Lancashire and was partially deaf from birth. At school, it was difficult for him to hear the teachers, especially when other children were talking. He moved to a different school, but he still struggled with reading and writing. He left education at 16 without qualifications. Finding reading hard did not mean he was not clever. Like many adults who find reading difficult, he learnt ways to manage. He remembered roads, used pictures and numbers, and asked his wife to help with letters and forms.

During the Covid lockdowns, Peter went shopping for milk that was safe for his wife and son, who had food allergies. In the supermarket, he realised that he could not understand the information on the cartons. He worried about buying the wrong one. Back at home, he told his wife how difficult things had become. She encouraged him to contact Read Easy, a charity that helps adults learn to read.

Peter started meeting a volunteer coach called Alison twice a week. They worked through simple exercises, slowly building his skills and confidence. Sometimes the work was frustrating, but this time he did not give up. He had a personal goal: to read the first Harry Potter book from beginning to end. After three years of lessons, he finished it. Reading was no longer just something he needed for shopping or work. It was something he could enjoy.

Today, Peter can read recipes and share books with his son. He also talks publicly about his experience to encourage other adults to seek help. Many people struggle with reading, even if they have jobs and families. Some are afraid that others will judge them. Peter's story shows why that fear can be so damaging. Learning to read has not changed his past, but it has opened up a very different future.

Intermediate version

For years, Peter Lloyd found ways to avoid reading in public. As a lorry driver, he could remember complicated routes and recognise road signs, but a letter arriving through the post could leave him feeling helpless. He had built a working life and raised a family while hiding a difficulty that many people assume only children face. Then, aged 46, he found himself unable to complete a simple supermarket shop.

Peter was born partially deaf in Lancashire and struggled to follow lessons in noisy classrooms. Moving schools did little to help. He left at 16 without qualifications and with very limited reading skills. Yet he was resourceful. His strong visual memory helped him navigate Britain's roads, and later a talking satnav made driving easier. His wife stepped in when he needed to understand documents or fill in forms. From the outside, there was little to suggest how much effort ordinary tasks required.

The turning point came during the Covid lockdowns. Peter was shopping for milk that would be safe for his wife and son, who have food allergies. Unable to read the labels properly, he became frightened of making a dangerous mistake. At home, he finally explained how much he was struggling. His wife suggested Read Easy, a charity offering individual reading support to adults. Peter was hesitant because earlier attempts to learn had ended badly, but he decided to give it another go.

He was matched with volunteer coach Alison, and they began meeting twice a week. The early lessons involved going back to basics, practising sounds and building words before moving on to longer texts. Progress was gradual. There were difficult days, but Alison helped him see that mistakes were part of learning rather than proof that he could never succeed. Peter set himself a goal that was both ambitious and personal: finishing the first Harry Potter novel.

Three years later, he reached the final page. What mattered was not only the achievement itself but also the freedom it represented. Peter could now choose a book, follow a recipe and help his son with everyday tasks that had previously been out of reach. He became an ambassador for Read Easy, encouraging people in similar situations to take the first step.

Low literacy does not mean a lack of intelligence, yet some adults wait years before asking for help. Peter's experience reveals how easily reading difficulties can remain hidden in a society built around written information. He hopes to earn an English GCSE and eventually help others. A trip to the supermarket exposed a problem he had spent decades concealing. It also gave him the reason to change his life.

Advanced version

The trouble began in the dairy aisle. Peter Lloyd, a lorry driver from Lancashire, was trying to buy milk suitable for his wife and son, both of whom had allergies. Surrounded by cartons covered in information he could barely decipher, he realised how easily a routine errand could become a serious mistake. He was 46, with a job and a family, but he had spent much of his life covering up the fact that he struggled to read.

Partially deaf from birth, Peter had found the classroom an unforgiving place. Competing voices made lessons difficult to follow, and a move to another school failed to provide the support he needed. He left education at 16 without qualifications, carrying into adulthood a problem that society often treats as a personal failure. Yet his working life demonstrated considerable ingenuity. He learnt routes through visual memory, recognised landmarks and relied on technology and his wife's help to negotiate paperwork. Most people take reading for granted, and few notice the elaborate system of workarounds that sustained what looked like independence.

The supermarket incident occurred during the Covid lockdowns and stripped away the reassurance those strategies had provided. Peter could no longer brush aside the practical risks of not understanding written information. Encouraged by his wife, he contacted Read Easy, an adult-literacy charity, despite the disappointment of previous attempts to learn. He was paired with a volunteer coach, Alison, who offered regular one-to-one sessions and something Peter had rarely experienced at school: the freedom to progress without embarrassment.

Twice-weekly lessons began with fundamental reading skills. It was painstaking work, requiring him to confront difficulties he had spent decades avoiding, but small improvements gradually accumulated. He also set himself a concrete ambition: to finish the first Harry Potter novel. Choosing a book associated with childhood might have exposed him to ridicule. Instead, it gave the process a meaning beyond exercises and assessments. Three years later, he had read it from cover to cover.

The achievement did not erase his earlier experiences, but it changed his relationship with everyday life. Recipes became accessible, books became a source of pleasure, and tasks that once required another person's help could increasingly be handled alone. Peter has since spoken publicly as an ambassador for Read Easy and hopes to obtain an English GCSE before pursuing work that supports others. His willingness to discuss a previously guarded struggle is significant in itself, not least because so few people in his position are prepared to do so publicly.

Adult literacy is frequently misunderstood. People may have limited reading skills for reasons involving disability, disrupted education or inadequate support, none of which is a reliable measure of intelligence. The greatest obstacle can become the expectation that an adult ought already to know how to read, an assumption that encourages silence.

Read Easy offers support, but asking for it can still feel daunting. Peter's decision to speak openly may help someone else take that first step. Peter's story challenges the idea that learning follows a fixed timetable.
