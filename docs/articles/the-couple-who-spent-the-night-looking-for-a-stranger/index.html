The Couple Who Spent the Night Looking for a Stranger

A backpacker lost a wallet holding a month's money in Darwin in 1989. Two strangers visited four hostels to give it back, then disappeared.

Core version

In 1989, Simon Kelsey was a young British man at the end of a year in Australia. He had finished university, flown out and spent ten months working long hours on building sites along the east coast. The work was hot and hard, but he loved it, and by the end he had saved enough money to travel slowly home.

It was his last night in Darwin, in the north of the country. He was out with a group of travelling friends, and the next morning they were all flying to Timor, hoping to move from island to island all the way to Singapore. In his pocket was a wallet with $1,000 in cash inside. In those days, people carried cash, and that money was enough to live on for a month.

At some point in the evening, the wallet fell out of his pocket. He did not notice. He went back to the hostel and went to bed.

At 1.30 in the morning, somebody knocked loudly on his door. Half asleep, he opened it. Outside stood the hostel manager and an elderly couple he had never seen before. The couple were holding his wallet.

They had found it and decided to return it. There was no phone number inside, only a student identity card with his name on it. So they had gone from one backpacker hostel to another, asking for him. His hostel was the fourth one they tried. Every single dollar was still inside the wallet.

Think about what that meant. It was the middle of the night. They were not young. They had no reason to believe they would ever find him, and nobody would have known if they had given up after the second hostel.

He was tired and had been drinking, so he thanked them and went back to bed. It was only the next morning, on the way to the airport, that he understood what they had done. Without them, he says, his whole trip would have been ruined. He never got the chance to thank them properly.

Instead, he made himself a promise. When somebody needs help and he can give it, he goes further than he has to. He does not ask for anything back. He just tells the person to pass it on to somebody else.

Intermediate version

Some acts of kindness are small enough to forget by the following week. Others stay with a person for nearly forty years. Simon Kelsey's story, told to the Guardian's Katie Cunningham as part of a reader series about the kindness of strangers, is firmly in the second category, and the details are what make it so.

In 1989, Kelsey had just finished university in the UK and gone travelling in Australia. He spent most of the year on the east coast, working long hours on building sites in the blistering sun, and by his own account he loved every minute of it. After ten months he had saved enough to begin making his way slowly home.

The plan began in Darwin. He and a group of travelling friends had one last night out before a flight to Timor, from where they intended to island-hop all the way to Singapore. Cash was king in those days, as he puts it, and the $1,000 in his wallet represented roughly a month on the road. Somewhere in the course of the evening, with the beers going down easily, the wallet fell out of his pocket. He noticed nothing, and went to bed still unaware.

At 1.30am there was loud knocking at his door. He stumbled out of bed and opened it to find the hostel manager standing beside an elderly couple he had never met. They were holding his wallet, and every dollar was still in it.

What they had done to get there is the part worth sitting with. The wallet contained no phone number, only a student ID card with his name. So the couple had worked their way around the backpacker places of Darwin, asking at each one, until they found the right hostel. It was the fourth they had tried, and by then it was the middle of the night.

Kelsey admits he did not rise to the occasion. Half asleep and the worse for the evening's drinks, he thanked them and went back to bed. Only the next morning did the scale of the effort become clear to him. Without the couple, he says, the trip he had worked ten months for would have been ruined.

He never learnt enough about them to thank them properly, and he has never stopped wishing he could. What he did instead was make a private pledge, to himself and in a sense to them, that he would always go the extra mile when he had the chance to help someone. When he does, he asks only that they pay it forward. He likes to think that the couple in Darwin have quietly helped a great many people they will never meet.

Advanced version

There is a particular arithmetic to a lost wallet. You calculate what was in it, what cannot be replaced, and how long it will take to recover. In Darwin in 1989, Simon Kelsey never had to do the sum, because the answer arrived at his door at half past one in the morning, carried by two people he had never met and would never see again.

The circumstances are ordinary enough. Kelsey had finished university in the UK and spent a year in Australia, most of it working long hours on east coast building sites in punishing heat, which he remembers with unmistakable affection. Ten months of that had bought him the means to travel slowly home. He was in Darwin for a final night out with the friends he would fly to Timor with the next morning, intending to island-hop to Singapore. Cash was king then, as he says, and the $1,000 in his wallet was about a month of freedom.

He lost it without noticing and went to bed unaware. The knocking woke him at 1.30am: the hostel manager, an elderly couple, and his wallet with every dollar inside.

The effort behind that moment is the thing the story turns on. There was no phone number to call. There was a student ID card with a name, which in a town full of backpacker hostels narrows nothing very much. So the couple went looking, hostel by hostel, in the middle of the night, and his was the fourth they tried. They could have left the wallet with a police station or a bar. They could, of course, have kept it. Instead they chose the most laborious route available and then, having completed it, went home.

Kelsey's own account is honest about his response, which is part of why it reads truthfully. Bleary and the worse for the evening, he thanked them and went back to sleep. Comprehension arrived with the morning, by which time they were gone and unthankable. Thirty-seven years later he still describes wishing he could have done it properly.

What he did instead is the quietly interesting part. Unable to repay the couple, he converted the debt into a standing policy: go further than strictly necessary when someone needs help, and ask the person to pay it forward rather than back. It is a sentimental idea that happens also to be a practical one, since a debt that cannot be settled with the lender can only be settled with everyone else.

A single recollection, published nearly four decades after the event, is not evidence about human nature, and nobody should pretend otherwise. It is one man's memory, offered to a newspaper series collecting such things. But it does illustrate something that is easy to lose sight of: the cost of the kindness fell entirely on the couple, and the benefit has been compounding ever since, in places they never heard about and to people they never met.
