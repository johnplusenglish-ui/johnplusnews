Core version

Look around your bedroom when you turn off the light. Is it completely dark? You may notice light from the street, a phone charger or a television. These small lights are common, but researchers are interested in how they affect our sleep.

Our bodies follow a daily rhythm. Light helps tell us when to be awake, and darkness helps prepare us for sleep. Bright light at the wrong time may make this rhythm less clear. The problem is not only what we do before bed. Light can also enter a room while we are sleeping.

In 2026, researchers published a review of 15 studies involving more than 765,000 participants. In the combined results, sleep problems were 27% more common in the group exposed to the most artificial light at night than in the group with low or no exposure. As an example only, if 10 in 100 people in the low-light group had sleep problems, 27% more would be about 13 in 100. This was a link, not proof that the light caused the problems.

There are other reasons people sleep badly, including noise, stress, illness and irregular working hours. Some of the studies also had problems measuring exactly how much light people experienced. That makes it difficult to know how large the effect is for any one bedroom.

Still, a dark room can be a sensible place to start. Thick curtains or blinds may block streetlights. Turning off unnecessary lamps and moving bright devices away from the bed are simple changes. Not everyone can control lighting outside their home.

During the day, daylight helps us stay awake and understand what time it is. In the evening, many people enjoy softer light, especially when they are getting ready for bed. A room that is comfortable in the afternoon may not be the best room for sleeping at night. Some people need a small nightlight for safety, particularly when they get up to use the bathroom.

That is an important reason not to tell everybody to switch off every light. The aim is to avoid unnecessary brightness while keeping the home safe. It can also help to have regular sleeping and waking times. Light is only one part of a much bigger picture of healthy sleep.

The research does not mean one small light will ruin your night. But it suggests that the environment around us deserves attention. We often think about how many hours we sleep. Perhaps we should also think about what our bedrooms are like during those hours.

Intermediate version

When you put out your bedroom light, darkness does not always follow. Streetlamps shine through curtains, a television flashes in the corner and electronic devices display tiny coloured lights. These details are easy to ignore, but researchers increasingly suspect that nighttime lighting can affect sleep quality.

Human sleep is influenced by the body's internal clock, which responds partly to patterns of light and darkness. Artificial light late in the evening may interfere with signals associated with preparing for sleep. Researchers also want to understand the effects of exposure during the night itself.

A systematic review published in Environmental Research in March 2026 combined evidence from 15 studies involving more than 765,000 people. Participants with the greatest exposure to artificial light at night had a 27% higher prevalence of sleep disturbance than those with little or no exposure. The result was an association, and most included studies had some risk of bias.

That distinction matters. People living in brightly lit areas may also experience traffic noise, stressful conditions or other factors linked to poorer sleep. Measuring the light somebody receives is difficult, particularly when researchers rely on outdoor estimates rather than measurements inside bedrooms. Not everybody can simply install perfect blinds. People renting a room or working night shifts may have little control over their sleeping environment, so practical solutions need to be realistic.

Nevertheless, there are practical reasons to pay attention to lighting. Curtains that block streetlights, reduced screen brightness before bed and shutting down unnecessary devices may create a more comfortable sleep environment. People working nights or living in busy cities may have fewer options.

The timing of light exposure is just as important as its presence. Brightness during the morning and daytime helps the body distinguish waking hours from the night. Excessive illumination close to bedtime may send less helpful signals. A practical approach is therefore not to make homes dark all day, but to provide appropriate lighting at different times. Safety also matters.

An older person moving around the house at night may reasonably prefer a low-level light to walking through a completely dark room. Researchers and public-health advisers must balance such needs instead of turning darkness into an absolute rule. Alongside lighting, consistent routines, manageable noise and any underlying medical conditions influence sleep quality. Good advice recognises the whole environment.

No single change guarantees good sleep, and persistent difficulties can require professional advice. But the study offers a useful reminder: sleep is influenced by our surroundings as well as our routines. Sometimes improving a bedroom begins with noticing the small sources of light we stopped seeing years ago.

Advanced version

A bedroom can appear dark while remaining surprisingly illuminated. Light leaks around blinds, a phone charges beside the bed and the glow of nearby buildings reaches through the curtains. Because these sources are so familiar, we tend to tune them out. Yet growing research suggests that artificial light at night deserves a place in discussions about sleep health.

A charger's glow is one example of small light in a bedroom. The review did not test chargers or any single source. It looked at overall exposure to artificial light at night, which the studies measured in different ways and often estimated rather than recorded at the sleeper's eyes.

Our circadian system responds to the daily pattern of light and darkness. Exposure at biologically inappropriate times may alter signals involved in preparing for sleep. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Environmental Research in March 2026 examined epidemiological research on nighttime illumination and sleep disturbance.

Across 15 studies involving more than 765,000 participants, the authors found that higher exposure was associated with poorer sleep. In the studies suitable for pooled analysis, those with the greatest exposure had a 27% higher prevalence of sleep disturbance compared with groups receiving little or no artificial light at night. The figure describes relative association, not an individual's probability of having insomnia. Observational research cannot isolate every influence. People exposed to more nighttime light may also differ in working hours, neighbourhood conditions or existing health problems, all of which complicate interpretation.

The limitations are substantial. Most studies had a high risk of bias, and estimates of nighttime illumination are not always accurate representations of what reaches a sleeper's eyes. Residents of brightly lit neighbourhoods may also face additional environmental pressures, including noise and irregular working conditions. Observational evidence cannot easily disentangle these factors.

Even so, the findings support reasonable measures to reduce unnecessary exposure: properly fitted blinds, dimmer evening lighting and removing bright electronic displays from the bedside. These adjustments may be easier than changing a demanding work schedule, although cost and housing conditions still influence what people can do. The sensible conclusion is modest rather than alarmist: reduce unnecessary light where possible, without turning another aspect of sleep into a source of relentless anxiety.

A simplistic reading of the evidence could lead to the conclusion that less artificial light is always better. In reality, the biological effects of illumination depend on timing, intensity, duration and individual circumstances. Daytime light exposure helps maintain a clear daily rhythm, while adequate nighttime visibility can prevent accidents. Residents with mobility difficulties may need carefully positioned low-level lighting even when darkness would otherwise be preferable.

This creates a design challenge rather than a demand to eliminate every light source. Buildings and streets can potentially be illuminated where necessary while reducing unnecessary glare or light spilling into bedrooms. The review's findings therefore have implications for architects and city authorities as well as individuals deciding whether to buy blackout curtains.

There is also a wider policy question. Public lighting supports safety and access, so plunging streets into darkness is not a practical solution. Better-designed lighting could balance those benefits with reduced disturbance to residents. A good night's sleep may depend partly on decisions made far beyond the bedroom walls.
