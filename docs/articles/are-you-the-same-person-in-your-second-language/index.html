Are You the Same Person in Your Second Language?

A study of 106 Greek-English bilinguals found that the language they used did not change their moral decisions, although it did shift how some of them described themselves.

Core version

Do you feel like a different person when you speak a second language? Many people say yes. Some feel funnier in one language, or braver, or more careful. Some feel that a part of them gets lost in translation. A natural question follows: does your sense of right and wrong change too?

A team of researchers set out to find out. In a study published in 2026, they worked with 106 people from Greece. All of them had Greek as their first language, and their English was upper-intermediate or advanced. Half did the whole study in Greek. The other half did all of it in English.

Everyone read four short moral problems. Two were about hurting one person to save others. Two were about honesty: whether to lie on a CV to get a better chance of a job, and whether to reveal a partner's infidelity. People said what they would do and how strongly they felt. They also answered questions about how important being a moral person was to them.

The results were not what some people expect. The language did not change what people decided. The Greek group and the English group made the same kinds of choices. There was one small difference in how people saw themselves. Those who used Greek said that being a moral person was more important to them than those who used English did. But the two groups did not differ in how far they said they would act on their beliefs. Some also reported stronger feelings in English, but those feelings did not change their choices.

The clearest pattern came from personal values. People who said that morality was central to who they are felt stronger emotions, and they were more likely to say no to lying or harming someone. This was especially true for the everyday honesty problems. Many people may stick to their guns, whichever language they use.

There are limits. Only 106 people took part, and all of them spoke Greek and English. The problems were imaginary, so we cannot be sure that people would act the same way in real life. Earlier studies gave mixed results too, so one study does not settle it.

Still, it gives a comforting answer. Your first language may feel like second nature, and your second may feel strange, but the core of who you are may travel with you.

Intermediate version

Anyone who has lived in a second language knows the odd feeling of not being quite the same person. You might be more direct, more polite, more playful or more reserved. Some people say a part of themselves gets lost in translation. Researchers have wondered how deep this goes, and whether it reaches as far as our sense of right and wrong.

There is a reason to ask. Earlier research suggested a foreign-language effect: when people judge classic dilemmas in a second language, they are more willing to accept harming one person to save several. One explanation is that a second language feels less emotional than the language we grew up with. But the evidence has been mixed, and some studies have found little or no effect.

Irini Mavrou and Andreas Kyriakou set out to look at it differently. Their preregistered study, published online in Bilingualism: Language and Cognition in August 2026, involved 106 native Greek speakers with upper-intermediate or advanced English. Half completed the study entirely in Greek and half entirely in English. Each person judged four dilemmas: two about sacrificing one life to save others, and two about honesty, namely lying on a CV and revealing a partner's infidelity. They also completed a questionnaire about moral identity, which includes moral self and moral integrity.

The headline result is that language did not have a reliable overall effect on moral decisions. Switching from Greek to English did not make people more or less willing to sacrifice one person to save several. There was one difference in self-image: people who used Greek rated being a moral person as more important than people who used English. Moral integrity, which is about commitment to acting on your principles, was the same in both groups. Emotions varied. People often reported stronger feelings in English, but those feelings did not reliably explain their choices.

What did predict behaviour was moral identity. People with a stronger sense of themselves as moral reported stronger emotional reactions and were more likely to reject dishonest or harmful acts. The effect was clearest in the everyday honesty scenarios rather than in the extreme life-or-death ones.

It is important to take the findings with a pinch of salt. They come from one study with 106 people who all spoke the same two languages, so they may not apply to other bilinguals. The dilemmas were hypothetical, and different people took part in each language, so the study cannot show how any one person would change if they switched. Moral identity was also measured by self-report. The researchers also describe their work in an article for The Conversation, so it is worth reading that account alongside the paper itself.

Still, the take-home message is gentle. A second language may nudge some feelings or self-descriptions, but it does not seem to override a person's basic values. At the end of the day, who you are mattered more than which language you were using.

Advanced version

For about a decade, one of the more striking findings in bilingualism research has been the moral foreign-language effect. When people weigh sacrificial dilemmas, such as whether to harm one person to save several, they tend to make more utilitarian choices in a second language than in their first. A popular explanation is emotional distance: the first language carries childhood and family values, while the second feels more deliberate. The evidence, however, has been uneven, and some studies find little effect.

Irini Mavrou and Andreas Kyriakou argue that this literature has paid close attention to which language people use and little to the person making the decision. Their study, published online in Bilingualism: Language and Cognition in August 2026, tests that idea. They set out to correct that. The study was preregistered, and the analysis used both conventional and Bayesian statistics. The 106 participants were native Greek speakers with upper-intermediate or advanced English, and each did the whole task in either Greek or English. They judged four dilemmas, two involving sacrificial harm and two involving honesty, lying on a CV and revealing a partner's infidelity, and completed a questionnaire measuring moral self and moral integrity.

Language did not reliably affect decisions. The two groups were no more or less likely to take the sacrifice option. Moral identity told a different story. Those with a stronger moral self and integrity reported stronger emotional reactions and favoured rule-based choices, particularly in the honesty scenarios. Language did leave a trace: Greek speakers rated moral self higher, which the authors link to a first language's association with early family values, but moral integrity stayed stable across both groups.

The emotional picture is a mixed bag. Participants generally reported stronger emotions in English, yet reported stronger emotional intensity in Greek for the sacrificial dilemmas. A mediation analysis found that emotional intensity did not reliably explain any link between language and choice. In other words, English could make people feel more, and still leave their decisions untouched, which does not back up the neat idea that emotional distance drives the foreign-language effect.

There are good reasons to hold the findings lightly. The sample is small and culturally specific, and the study involves one language pair. None of this is set in stone. The dilemmas were hypothetical, and the devil is in the detail: because each person used only one language, the design compares groups, not individuals. Self-report carries its usual weaknesses, and the authors themselves point out that the results may not generalise beyond Greek-English bilinguals. An account written by the researchers for The Conversation, and then republished elsewhere, deserves to be read next to the peer-reviewed paper. It would be wrong to say that switching language automatically changes moral choices, but it would be equally wrong to say the language never matters.

What the study offers is a shift of emphasis. If a stable moral identity carries across languages, the question is not simply which language you are using, but who is using it. That is a more modest claim than the headlines about a second-language moral compass, and probably a more accurate one.
