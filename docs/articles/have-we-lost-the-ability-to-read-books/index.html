Core version

You read the same paragraph twice, but it still does not make sense. Your phone made a sound a few minutes ago; one message became a video, then another. The book is still open, but the story has disappeared from your mind. Many people say they want to read more and even buy books they are excited about. The difficult part is staying with one page when a screen keeps offering something new.

Reading for pleasure has become less common in some places. A study published in 2025 looked at how Americans spent their time between 2003 and 2023. It found that the number of people reading for pleasure on an average day had fallen. However, other research shows that many Americans still read at least one book in a year, including e-books and audiobooks. It is important to understand that these are different ways of measuring reading habits.

Phones may be part of the problem. They offer messages, games, videos and news at any moment. A book asks us to stay with one story or subject for longer. That can feel difficult when we are used to changing activities every few seconds. But reading is not only about concentration. People also need time, quiet places and books they find interesting.

In August 2026, the novelist Naomi Alderman described an eight-week plan to help people get back into reading. The idea was similar to a beginner's exercise programme: start with a short time each day and slowly increase it. Someone might begin with ten minutes and then work towards longer reading sessions. The aim was not to finish as many books as possible, but to enjoy the habit again.

There are plenty of ways to make reading easier. Choose a book that interests you rather than one you think you should read. Switch your phone off for a while, or listen to an audiobook when travelling. Reading with a friend can also help. Finishing a chapter may not seem like a huge achievement, but regular practice can make it easier to concentrate. A book does not need to compete with everything on your phone. Sometimes it simply needs ten quiet minutes.

Intermediate version

You return to the novel after checking one message and realise that you have forgotten who is speaking. The same paragraph must be read again. Nothing is wrong with the book, and the desire to read is still there; the protected attention has gone. For people who once became absorbed in stories for hours, that change can feel less like a new habit than the loss of an old ability.

There is evidence that reading patterns have changed. A study published in 2025 analysed American time-use data collected over two decades and found that the proportion of people reading for pleasure on an average day had fallen from around 28% in 2004 to 16% in 2023. However, a 2026 Pew Research Center survey found that about three-quarters of American adults had read at least part of a book in the previous year. The findings are not contradictory: reading something during a whole year is very different from reading regularly.

Smartphones are an obvious suspect. They offer endless opportunities to switch activities, while a book requires sustained attention. But it would be too simple to blame every change on technology. Long working hours, financial pressures and family responsibilities also leave people tired. Some readers struggle to find books that interest them; others feel they should be reading difficult or important titles instead of something they might actually enjoy.

In August 2026, novelist Naomi Alderman proposed an eight-week reading programme inspired by the popular Couch to 5K approach to running. The idea was to rebuild reading stamina gradually, beginning with manageable sessions before increasing the time spent with a book. It treats concentration as something that can improve with practice, rather than a talent a person either has or lacks.

That approach may be particularly useful for people who have fallen out of the habit. Choosing a short, enjoyable book is often better than forcing yourself through a classic that feels like homework. Keeping your phone out of reach, reading on public transport or joining a small book group can also make regular reading easier. Audiobooks and e-books offer other ways to enjoy stories, especially for people whose lives are already busy.

The real concern is not that everyone should spend hours reading novels. It is that losing the habit of sustained attention may make longer, more complex writing feel increasingly difficult. Books give us time to follow ideas and characters without constant interruption. Recovering that experience does not require a dramatic lifestyle change. It may start with putting the phone away, opening a book and allowing ourselves to be bored for a few minutes before the story takes over.

Advanced version

The novel is open and the intention is sincere. Then a notification interrupts, one message leads to a headline and the reader returns unable to remember the previous paragraph. The book has not become less interesting in ten minutes; the thread of attention has been cut. Increasing numbers of people describe this experience even when reading was once one of their favourite activities. The problem may be less a rejection of books than the disappearance of the routine that once protected them.

Research suggests a more complicated picture than the familiar claim that nobody reads any more. A 2025 study drawing on two decades of American Time Use Survey data found that the proportion of adults reading for pleasure on an average day fell from approximately 28% in 2004 to 16% in 2023. Yet Pew Research Center reported in 2026 that around 75% of American adults had read at least some of a book during the previous year, a measure that has remained comparatively stable. Both findings can be true. Occasional reading may persist even as the practice of reading regularly becomes less common.

Smartphones undoubtedly compete for attention, offering constant opportunities for novelty and interruption. A novel asks readers to remain within an unfolding argument or imagined world; a phone promises a succession of immediate rewards. But attributing every change to digital technology glosses over other pressures. Exhaustion, precarious working lives, limited access to books and the absence of quiet spaces can all undermine the habit. Reading also becomes less inviting when treated as a test of cultural sophistication rather than a source of pleasure.

In August 2026, the novelist Naomi Alderman proposed a programme modelled on the Couch to 5K running scheme. Her eight-week approach encouraged people to rebuild reading stamina through progressively longer sessions, beginning with manageable periods rather than ambitious targets. The analogy is useful because it replaces an unhelpful moral judgement with the idea of practice. Someone who struggles to concentrate is not necessarily lazy or incapable; they may simply have lost a routine that needs rebuilding.

There is reason to avoid romanticising books as inherently virtuous or dismissing audiobooks and digital formats as inferior. Reading takes different forms, and sustained engagement with a complex text can occur on a screen as well as on paper. Nor is finishing more novels automatically a measure of intelligence. The more persuasive argument concerns the capacity to follow ideas over time, tolerate uncertainty and resist the expectation of constant stimulation. Those abilities matter beyond literature, including in education and public debate.

The practical response may be disarmingly modest. Read something genuinely appealing, set aside a short period free from notifications and allow the habit to develop without turning it into another productivity target. A book need not compete with a phone on the phone's terms. Its pleasures are slower, less immediate and sometimes demanding. That difference is precisely what makes reading worth preserving, not as a badge of refinement, but as a way of paying attention to something long enough for it to change how we think.
