Core version

When we think about cannabis, we often imagine a modern argument about health, laws and personal choice. But the plant has a much longer history. Archaeologists studying an ancient tomb in Siberia have found evidence that people may have used cannabis for its mind-changing effects about 2,800 years ago. What makes the discovery special is that scientists found more than old stories or drawings. They found chemical evidence in human hair.

The research was published in the journal Science on 5 November 2026. The tomb belonged to members of the Scythian culture, whose people lived across parts of Central Asia and were known for horse riding. The researchers discovered cannabis seeds and analysed preserved hair from the burial site. The hair contained a substance called cannabinol, which can form when THC, the main intoxicating chemical in cannabis, breaks down.

That matters. Seeds alone might show that people had cannabis, but they would not prove why. The plant can also be used for making rope, cloth or oil. Chemical traces in human hair give scientists stronger evidence that people were exposed to its active compounds. Researchers therefore believe cannabis may have been used for its intoxicating effects. They still cannot say exactly how people took it.

Why might the ancient Scythians have used it? We do not know for certain. The tomb was an important burial place, so the plant may have played a part in special ceremonies. Some ancient writers described the Scythians using smoke from plants, but written accounts can be difficult to check. Modern scientific tests offer new clues that can support or challenge old ideas about the past.

The discovery does not tell us how strong ancient cannabis was or how often people used it. It also does not mean every person in that society took drugs. Scientists must explain the evidence carefully. The hair and seeds are real, but many details about the people's lives remain a mystery.

The story reminds us that humans have used plants for many reasons for thousands of years. Some plants were food, some were medicine and others were part of ceremonies. The same plant could have several purposes. Finding out about these habits helps us see ancient people as curious and complicated, much like people today. Intermediate version

Cannabis is often discussed as if it were a modern issue, connected to changing laws, medical uses and arguments about personal freedom. Yet people have known about the plant for thousands of years, using it for fibre, oil and other purposes. A discovery in southern Siberia now provides unusually direct evidence that some ancient people were also exposed to its mind-altering compounds. The clue was not a written record but traces of chemicals preserved in human hair.

The study, published in the journal Science on 5 November 2026, examined a princely tomb associated with the Scythians, a horse-riding culture of the Eurasian steppe. Researchers led by archaeologist Gino Caspari of the University of Bern and the Max Planck Institute analysed cannabis seeds recovered from the tomb and hair belonging to people buried there. The evidence dates back roughly 2,800 years, to the early eighth century BC.

The hair contained cannabinol, a substance that can form when THC, cannabis's main intoxicating compound, breaks down. This matters because finding cannabis seeds by themselves would not establish that anybody used the plant to become intoxicated. Cannabis also has practical uses, including producing textiles and oil. Chemical traces in human tissue offer stronger evidence of exposure to compounds associated with its psychoactive properties. The scientists describe the discovery as the oldest directly dated evidence of this kind.

The burial context raises fascinating questions. Did cannabis play a part in ceremonies connected to death, or was it something people used in everyday life? Ancient writers, including the Greek historian Herodotus, described Scythian practices involving plant smoke, but such accounts were written long after some of the events they described. The new evidence gives researchers something physical to investigate, though it does not reveal exactly how the plant was prepared or consumed.

There are important limits to what can be concluded. The researchers cannot determine the THC strength of the original plants from these traces, and the evidence does not show that cannabis was common throughout Scythian society. Nor does its ancient use tell us whether present-day cannabis is safe or how modern laws should work. Historical evidence can explain what people did without settling what people ought to do now.

Nevertheless, the discovery is a good example of how science can change our understanding of history. A few preserved hairs can provide information that ancient writers never recorded. It also challenges the idea that our ancestors were somehow less curious about plants and their effects on the body. The plant we argue about today had several roles long before modern states existed. Advanced version

Few plants provoke as much contemporary debate as cannabis. Depending on whom you ask, it is a medicine, a recreational drug, a commercial opportunity or a public health concern. Yet its place in human history extends far beyond modern legal arguments. An archaeological discovery in southern Siberia has provided unusually strong physical evidence that people were exposed to cannabis's intoxicating compounds approximately 2,800 years ago, offering a glimpse into a practice that previously rested largely on interpretation and historical accounts.

Reported in the journal Science on 5 November 2026, the research focused on a princely tomb associated with the Scythians, a horse-riding culture whose influence spread across the Eurasian steppe. An international team led by Gino Caspari, affiliated with the University of Bern and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, studied preserved cannabis seeds and human hair recovered from the tomb. The findings were dated to the early eighth century BC and offer what the researchers describe as the oldest directly dated evidence of the intoxicating use of cannabis.

The crucial clue was cannabinol detected in the hair. This compound can develop as THC, the principal intoxicating component of cannabis, breaks down over time. Seeds alone would have been much less informative, since cannabis was historically valuable for making fibre, oil and other products. Detecting chemical residues in human tissue allows researchers to make a more specific argument about exposure to the plant's psychoactive constituents. It does not, however, establish precisely how the substance was taken or what the experience felt like.

The setting adds another layer of intrigue. The material came from an elaborate burial context, suggesting a possible connection to ceremonial practices. Ancient accounts, notably those attributed to the Greek historian Herodotus, describe the Scythians using plant smoke, although such texts must be treated with caution. Combining archaeological evidence with scientific analysis helps researchers move beyond speculation, but the evidence remains incomplete. It is tempting to imagine a detailed ritual, yet the physical remains cannot reveal the participants' intentions or the meaning they attached to the plant.

Equally important is what the discovery does not prove. The chemical traces cannot establish the original plants' THC concentration, the prevalence of cannabis use or whether it occurred in ordinary daily life. Evidence from one burial site cannot be generalised to an entire society. Nor should historical use be confused with a modern endorsement of any particular policy or health claim. Something's ancient origin may make it culturally interesting without making it harmless or desirable.

The wider significance lies in the way tiny biological remains can reshape our picture of human behaviour. Rather than treating ancient societies as remote and fundamentally different, the finding reveals their complicated relationships with plants, substances and cultural practices. The same crop could serve practical, symbolic and intoxicating purposes, depending on the context. Scientific methods are increasingly allowing archaeologists to investigate such distinctions. When modern societies debate cannabis, should its long history influence the conversation, or should present-day evidence about health and harm carry all the weight?
