Core version

Grazia Incampo and Teresa Calia have known each other since they were girls. Both are now close to ninety, and most days they are still at work in a bakery in Altamura, a town in the south of Italy. They make bread and pasta the way they have always made them. Somebody films it. In the videos they cook, they sing, they disagree quietly about the right way to do things, and they laugh a great deal.

Millions of people have watched. Visitors now travel to the town and wait outside the shop. When the queue is long, the two women come out with a tray of fresh crackers and hand them round.

Somewhere along the way, the internet found a name for what they seem to have. It is called nonnamaxxing. A nonna is an Italian grandmother. Maxxing means pushing something as far as it will go. Put the two together and you get a joke about copying the habits people imagine an Italian grandmother has: cooking at home, walking to the shops, sitting with friends, taking a long time over lunch.

It is a strange word for a slow life. Maxxing usually belongs to videos about better sleep or more protein, and that advice can start to sound like a list of things you are getting wrong. Then along comes a woman of eighty-eight with a pan on the stove and a neighbour at the door.

Perhaps the appeal is simple. A meal is not only food. A walk is not only a number on a watch. An afternoon does not always need a result.

It is worth remembering that the video is short and the life is long. Not every Italian grandmother cooks, and plenty of them have phones and busy jobs. The past was hard work for most people, and no habit can promise anybody a long life.

One writer has already made a joke of the whole thing. In the Guardian, Polly Hudson suggested that next year we try minning instead, which means doing less rather than more. It is an opinion in a newspaper column, not a plan for anybody's life.

Still, the two friends in Altamura are not following a plan. They are doing what they have always done, together, and letting somebody film it. That may be the only part anyone can copy.

Intermediate version

At a bakery in Altamura, in the south of Italy, Grazia Incampo and Teresa Calia make the bread the town is known for. They have been friends since childhood and are both approaching ninety. In the videos that made them famous, they knead, they sing, they correct each other, and they are plainly enjoying themselves. The food looks good. The friendship looks better.

Their audience runs into the millions, and visitors now queue outside the shop to watch them work. When the queue gets long, the two of them bring out a tray of crackers for the people waiting.

Online, their appeal has acquired a label: nonnamaxxing. The word joins nonna, Italian for grandmother, to maxxing, internet shorthand for taking something to its limit. The idea is to borrow the habits associated with an Italian grandmother: making food from scratch, walking, keeping up with neighbours, giving ordinary things enough time to be enjoyable.

It sits oddly next to the other kinds of maxxing. Online, almost any habit can be turned into a project, from protein intake to sleep quality. Some people mean it seriously and others are mocking the whole language of self-improvement. Either way, the steady supply of advice can leave you with the feeling that there is always one more part of your life to repair.

Nonnamaxxing seems to point the other way. Rather than treating a walk as a target to hit, you might use it to say hello to somebody. Rather than making dinner into a photograph, you might stay at the table long enough to have a conversation. The attraction is easy to recognise, and it is less about Italy than about wanting more connection and less performance.

The picture does deserve a second look, though. Italian grandmothers are individuals rather than a lifestyle. Some have worked long hours all their lives, some order takeaways, some dislike cooking. A short sunny video leaves out a great deal of labour, and no amount of pasta or gardening can be promised to lengthen anybody's life.

There is a neat contradiction in it, too. Watching videos about slowing down is itself an evening spent looking at a screen, and buying the correct apron in order to bake the correct loaf is one more test to pass. The part that was supposed to be enjoyable, which is the meal and the company, can disappear behind the effort of getting it right.

Writing in the Guardian, the columnist Polly Hudson suggests minning instead: doing less rather than maximising everything. It is a joke in a column, not a measured movement. But perhaps Incampo and Calia were never offering instructions. Two women who have been friends for eighty years are not a method anybody can follow.

Advanced version

Grazia Incampo and Teresa Calia have been friends since they were children. At a bakery in Altamura, in Puglia, they make the bread and pasta the region is known for, and the clips have found an audience far larger than the town. It is tempting to read their appeal as a recipe: food made by hand, a friendship of eighty years, an unhurried afternoon, and the rest of us might work out how to live.

The internet has supplied the name already. Nonnamaxxing attaches nonna, the Italian for grandmother, to maxxing, the suffix for taking something to its limit. It borrows a fond image of the Italian grandmother, cooking, walking, gardening, staying close to her neighbours, and offers those habits as the opposite of a life spent between screens and obligations.

The phrase is funny mainly because of the company it keeps. Maxxing is also fixed to protein, productivity, sleep and appearance, as though an optimal setting were waiting to be found in every hour of the day. Sometimes the language is sincere and sometimes the exaggeration is the joke. Either way most people recognise the feeling underneath: a suspicion that even their free time could be run better.

What makes the nonna image attractive may be what it leaves out. A meal is not fuel and it is not content; it is a reason to sit down together. A walk needs no justification from a watch. Talking to a neighbour can be the point. Set against the volume of advice about self-improvement, these ordinary acts look almost radical, which says more about the advice than the acts.

A carefully framed video is still a narrow window. Italian grandmothers share neither a personality nor a timetable, and older generations were hardly spared demanding work and money worries. Nor does a garden or a particular kind of flour guarantee anyone a longer life. Compressing a varied group of women into one comforting type tends to overlook the individuals the trend claims to be admiring.

Then there is the problem of simplicity as a performance. If a slower life requires the right linen, everything cooked from scratch and the results documented, it has quietly acquired a long list of tasks. What is a pleasant choice for one person is an expensive piece of organisation for another.

There is also a difference between making time and having it made for you. A long lunch is a lovely idea until somebody counts the shopping, the cooking and the washing up, and in most families that work has historically fallen to women, usually on top of a job. The honest version has everybody in the kitchen, rather than one person producing a peaceful afternoon for everyone else.

Polly Hudson's counter-proposal in the Guardian is minning: doing less, rather than maximising. It is commentary rather than evidence of anything, but it exposes the question underneath both labels. What is the extra room actually for, another admirable routine or an afternoon worth being present in? Incampo and Calia are under no obligation to answer. They are two old friends at work, and the thing people are watching may simply be that.
