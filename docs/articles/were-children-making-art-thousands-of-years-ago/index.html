Advanced version

Archaeology usually deals with things that are broken, buried and silent, so it is unusual when it offers something as personal as a fingerprint. In November 2026, a team led by Laurent Davin, from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, published a study in PLOS One of 154 small clay objects from three Natufian sites in Israel. The Natufians lived in some of the earliest villages, before agriculture, and the objects date from roughly 15,000 to 11,650 years ago.

The collection includes 15 figurines, most of them bird-shaped, together with others that appear to show animals and people. One fragment may represent a gazelle's face, and some pieces keep traces of red colouring. The authors argue that this is the earliest clay figurative tradition yet known in Southwest Asia, which challenges the idea that such clay objects became common only with Neolithic farming. Davin says the finds change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in the first villages.

The most original part of the study is its method. The ridges on a child's fingertips are narrower than those on an adult's, so the researchers measured the ridge breadth in prints left in the clay and compared it with prints from modern people who had shaped clay. That allowed them to estimate the age of the person behind each mark. The results suggest that children and adults both took part, and that the children were not simply watching. Their prints appear in the shaping of the objects and, in one case, in a red residue that was smoothed by a child's finger.

One human-shaped figure carries the print of a young adult, partly covered by the print of a child. The authors read this as an adult passing the figure to a child while the clay was wet, which they call a possible rare sign of knowledge passed on through hands-on learning. They also suggest that children who made the bird figures were probably guided by an adult tradition. These are interpretations. A fingerprint records who touched the clay and in what order, but it cannot reveal intention.

The limits are considerable, and the authors acknowledge many of them. The study is described as preliminary. Only two of the objects are complete, so identifications rely partly on comparison with other finds. The origin of the clay, the way it was hardened and the nature of the red pigment have not yet been worked out, and age estimates rest on a comparison with modern fingerprints. Nobody can say whether the children were playing, practising a craft or learning beliefs, and phrases such as art lessons would read too much into the evidence.

None of this makes the finding trivial. It adds one piece of the puzzle to a larger picture in which skills, symbols and knowledge were handed down across generations in the first villages. The children left their mark, literally, and the careful response is neither to dismiss it nor to invent a classroom around it.
