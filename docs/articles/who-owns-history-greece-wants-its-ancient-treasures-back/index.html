Who Owns History? Greece Wants Its Ancient Treasures Back

For more than 200 years, some of Greece's most famous ancient sculptures have been displayed in the British Museum. Now, the Greek prime minister is increasing pressure on Britain to return them. But who really owns these pieces of history?

Intermediate version

Walk through the British Museum in London and you will discover some of the world's most important historical objects. Among them are beautiful marble sculptures that once decorated the Parthenon, a famous ancient temple in Athens. The sculptures, often called the Elgin Marbles, are approximately 2,500 years old. For more than two centuries, they have been at the centre of a disagreement between Britain and Greece. Now, the Greek government wants to bring them home.

In October 2026, Greek prime minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis announced that he would increase pressure on Britain to return the sculptures. He argued that they should be reunited with the other surviving pieces in Athens, where visitors could see them close to the ancient building they originally belonged to. But how did these Greek treasures end up in London?

In the early 1800s, Greece was controlled by the Ottoman Empire. A British diplomat called Lord Elgin received permission from the Ottoman authorities to carry out work at the Acropolis. Between 1801 and 1805, his workers removed a large number of sculptures and transported them to Britain. In 1816, the British government bought Elgin's collection, and the sculptures became part of the British Museum. The British Museum maintains that the objects were obtained legally under the rules of that time.

However, Greece strongly disagrees. It argues that the sculptures were taken when the Greek people had no control over their own country. For Greece, this is not simply a disagreement about ownership. The sculptures are part of its national history, culture and identity. The Greek government believes they should be displayed together in the Acropolis Museum in Athens, near the Parthenon itself.

The British Museum offers a different argument. It believes that displaying objects from different countries together helps visitors understand world history and the connections between cultures. There is also a legal problem. Under a British law passed in 1963, the museum cannot normally give away objects from its permanent collection.

A temporary loan might be possible, but Greece does not want to accept an arrangement that requires it to recognise British ownership. Recent discussions have therefore failed to produce an agreement. Nevertheless, Mitsotakis believes there may still be a solution. Greece has suggested sending other valuable ancient objects to Britain for temporary exhibitions in exchange for the return of the sculptures.

The debate raises questions that go far beyond these particular works of art. Many museums around the world contain objects taken from other countries during periods of war, foreign rule or colonial expansion. Some people believe these objects should be returned to their countries of origin. Others argue that international museums allow millions of visitors to enjoy and learn from different cultures.

For now, the Parthenon sculptures remain divided between London and Athens. But after more than 200 years, the question remains unanswered: should historical objects belong to the people who currently possess them, or to the countries where they were created?

Advanced version

For more than two centuries, some of ancient Greece's greatest artistic achievements have been separated from the monument they were created to adorn. In London, visitors to the British Museum can admire magnificent marble sculptures once attached to the Parthenon, the fifth-century BCE temple overlooking Athens. Thousands of kilometres away, other surviving sections of the same monument are displayed in the Acropolis Museum, within sight of their original home.

To Greece, this separation represents an unresolved historical injustice. To the British Museum, the sculptures form part of a collection that allows visitors to explore the interconnected history of human civilisation. Now, the longstanding dispute has returned to the diplomatic agenda. In October 2026, Greek prime minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis pledged to intensify efforts to secure the sculptures' reunification in Athens, acknowledging that years of negotiations had produced little progress.

The controversy dates back to the early nineteenth century, when Thomas Bruce, the seventh Earl of Elgin, was serving as Britain's ambassador to the Ottoman Empire. At the time, Greece was under Ottoman rule. Elgin obtained authorisation from Ottoman officials and arranged for numerous sculptures to be removed from the Acropolis between 1801 and 1805. The collection was subsequently sold to the British government in 1816 and transferred to the British Museum.

Whether Elgin's actions were legitimate remains fiercely contested. The British Museum maintains that the removal took place with permission from the recognised authorities of the period and that its ownership was established through lawful acquisition. Greece disputes the moral and legal foundations of that claim, arguing that foreign rulers had no legitimate authority to dispose of objects so central to Greek cultural identity.

For Mitsotakis, the issue goes beyond national pride. He maintains that the surviving sculptures were conceived as elements of a unified artistic monument and that displaying them together would restore their historical and aesthetic relationship. The purpose-built Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009, was designed partly to demonstrate that Greece could preserve and exhibit the sculptures to international museum standards.

Yet the British Museum has its own institutional philosophy. Its trustees argue that presenting objects from different civilisations alongside one another enables visitors to discover connections between cultures and historical periods. The dispute is complicated further by legislation. Under the British Museum Act 1963, trustees are generally prevented from permanently removing objects from the museum's collection.

Although temporary loans are legally possible, the museum requires borrowing institutions to acknowledge its ownership. Greece has resisted such arrangements, arguing that they would undermine its claim to the sculptures. Recent discussions have explored potential compromises, including cultural exchanges involving significant Greek antiquities. Mitsotakis has proposed a broader partnership that would allow important objects from Greek collections to be exhibited in Britain while the Parthenon sculptures were reunited in Athens. Whether such an arrangement could overcome the legal obstacles and disagreement over ownership remains uncertain.

The debate also extends far beyond London and Athens. Museums across Europe and North America face growing demands to return cultural objects acquired during colonial expansion, armed conflict or periods of foreign domination. Supporters of restitution argue that institutions must confront the historical circumstances under which collections were assembled. Others maintain that international museums can preserve cultural heritage while making it accessible to audiences who might never travel to its country of origin.

The Parthenon Marbles dispute brings these competing principles into unusually sharp focus. Both sides recognise the sculptures' extraordinary historical significance. What they cannot agree on is who has the authority to decide where they belong. For now, the ancient masterpieces remain divided. The larger question, however, continues to challenge the museum world: can an object be legally owned by one institution while another nation maintains a compelling claim to its cultural and historical identity?
