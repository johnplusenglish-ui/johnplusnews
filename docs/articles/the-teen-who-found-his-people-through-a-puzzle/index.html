Core version

Imagine you get a puzzle as a present. You play with it alone, and you get better. Then one day you meet other people who love the same puzzle. What happens next?

That is the story of Thomas, a 15-year-old from Ingleby Barwick, in the north of England. He got some Rubik's Cubes as a Christmas present. A Rubik's Cube is a puzzle that you solve by twisting it. The cube was invented by Erno Rubik in 1974, and at least 350 million have been sold.

Thomas learned to solve the cube from a family friend. Soon he started going to competitions. Now he solves a normal cube in about nine seconds, on average. That is his own skill after lots of practice, not what every learner can expect. He also competes in a different puzzle called the clock. In May 2026, he was the sixth fastest in the UK for the clock.

But Thomas says that speed is not the main reason he likes cubing. He says he mostly enjoys the social side. He says everyone at a competition speaks the same cube language, and they all get on well.

His mother, Louise Allinson, runs a Rubik's Cube club at the local library with him. She says that competitions have given Thomas confidence and social skills. She also says that the people at the events are very supportive. Children do want to win, she says, but everyone is happy for whoever wins.

In May 2026, Allinson helped to arrange a cube competition in Darlington, with more than 60 competitors from all over the UK. It was the first one in their area. Intermediate version

Imagine you are given a puzzle that you can only solve alone. At first, you spend hours twisting it and failing. Then someone shows you a method, you improve, and one day you find that other people love the same thing. How does that change the puzzle?

That is roughly what happened to Thomas, a 15-year-old from Ingleby Barwick, near Stockton-on-Tees, in England, according to a BBC report published on 16 May 2026. He first became interested when he was given some Rubik's Cubes as a Christmas present. The cube, a puzzle solved by twisting, was invented by Erno Rubik in 1974, and at least 350 million have been sold worldwide.

Thomas learned from a family friend and soon began going to competitions. He now averages about nine seconds on a standard 3x3 cube. His main event, though, is a different puzzle called the clock, in which he was ranked sixth fastest in the UK at the time of the report. It is worth remembering that this ranking comes from May 2026, and that the clock and the standard cube are different events.

What stands out in the report is that he does not describe speed as the main attraction. He says it is mainly the social side that he enjoys, and that everyone at a competition speaks the same cube language and gets on well.

His mother, Louise Allinson, runs a Rubik's Cube club at their local library with him. She says that going to competitions has given Thomas confidence and social skills, and that the community is a wonderful and supportive one. She adds that children want to win, but that everyone is happy for whoever does.

Allinson also helped to arrange for the UK Cube Association to hold a competition at the Hopetown Railway Museum in Darlington on 16 and 17 May 2026. More than 60 competitors from around the UK were due to take part, and she described it as the first competition on Teesside.

These are the views of Thomas and his mother, not a study. They do not show that puzzles improve everyone's social skills, and a club is not a guarantee of friendship. But the story does show how a solitary activity can become a reason to meet people: you exchange techniques, you improve together, and you are happy when someone else gets a fast time.

It is striking how he describes the appeal. He says it is mainly the social side that he enjoys, and that everyone at a competition speaks the same cube language and gets on well. A shared language is one of the quickest ways to turn a room of strangers into a group.

His mother, Louise Allinson, runs a Rubik's Cube club at their local library with him. She says that going to competitions has given Thomas confidence and social skills, and she describes the community as wonderful and supportive. She also makes an honest point about competitions: children want to win, she says, but everyone is happy for whoever does.

Allinson helped to arrange for the UK Cube Association to hold a competition at the Hopetown Railway Museum in Darlington on 16 and 17 May 2026. More than 60 competitors from around the UK were due to take part, and she described it as the first such event on Teesside. A library club, a local competition and a clear first step all make a hobby easier to join.

It is important to read the report carefully. The comments come from Thomas and his mother, not from a study, so they do not show that puzzles improve everyone's social skills or that every club is as welcoming as this one. They do show a hobby in which competition and kindness exist at the same time, which is not what people always expect.

That is the interesting part. We tend to think that competition makes people rivals, and that friendship needs a break from competing. In this community, the opposite seems to be true: improving together, comparing times and cheering for the winner are all part of the same activity. Perhaps a good hobby community is one where you can join before you are good, and where the person who beats you is glad to explain how.

Try it

Then explain what an experienced member should say and not say. A Rubik's Cube is not required, and nobody has to speak about their own hobby, school or personal life unless they want to.
