How does that compare with other things? In 2013, GTA V made about US$800 million on its first day, so the new game made about five times as much. In 2019, the film Avengers: Endgame earned about US$1.2 billion at cinemas around the world in its opening, which was a record at that time.

On 20 November, Take-Two said that day-one retail sales had reached about US$3.79 billion. It described this as the biggest entertainment launch in history. The company also said that 32.5 million copies were sold before the game came out, a record for any platform. Pre-orders opened in June, so the game was selling like hot cakes long before launch day. It is worth noticing the small print: the company says the figure is based on its own estimates up to the end of 19 November, worldwide. No independent source has confirmed it.

The numbers invite comparisons. GTA V made about US$800 million on its first day in 2013, so Reuters says the new game made roughly five times as much. Marvel's Avengers: Endgame took in about US$1.2 billion at cinemas worldwide in 2019, which was then the biggest film opening ever. On paper, one day of GTA VI sales is well above that.

Yet the comparison is not as fair as it looks. Retail sales usually means what customers spent, not what the publisher keeps, because shops and online stores take a share. A game costs far more than a cinema ticket, so fewer people may be behind the number. A film's opening is also counted over several days. Comparing a game with a film is a bit like comparing apples with oranges, and the company has not said how much of the total came from pre-orders.

The cost side is just as uncertain. Reuters says Take-Two has spent an estimated US$1 billion or more on production, which would make GTA VI one of the most expensive entertainment projects ever, but this is an estimate, not an official budget. The company has not published one. An analytics firm, Sensor Tower, estimated about US$2.36 billion earlier in the launch. That figure was a different kind of measurement, taken at a different time, so it is not a direct contradiction, but it shows how much the numbers depend on who is counting.

So can one game be worth more than a blockbuster? Perhaps, in one narrow sense: if the company's estimate holds, shoppers spent more on it in one day than the biggest film opening took at cinemas. Take-Two's boss has said the company is not ready to declare victory, and one analyst quoted by Reuters says the timing of the next online mode matters more than first-day sales. A game is a different ball game from a film, because the money keeps coming in only if people keep playing.

Advanced version

When Grand Theft Auto VI went on sale on 19 November 2026, its publisher had already told the world to expect something enormous. By the following day, Take-Two Interactive, the parent of Rockstar Games, had a number to back it up: about US$3.79 billion in day-one retail sales, which it called the biggest entertainment launch in history. The game is rated Mature, meaning it is intended for adult players.

The details matter. The company says the figure is based on its own estimates up to the end of 19 November, worldwide, and no independent source has confirmed it. It also says the game sold 32.5 million copies before release, a record across all platforms, after pre-orders opened in June. Neither number splits the sales by console, by digital against boxed copies, or by pre-order against launch day, so we cannot tell how much of the total was a first-day rush and how much was demand built up over months.

Reuters offers a yardstick. GTA V made about US$800 million on its first day in 2013, so the new game made roughly five times as much. Cinema supplies a more dramatic comparison. Marvel's Avengers: Endgame took in about US$1.2 billion worldwide when it opened in 2019, a record at the time, and one day of GTA VI sales is well above that. That is the sense in which a game can be said to be worth more than a blockbuster.

It is also a sense that should be treated with caution, because the comparison risks comparing apples with oranges. Retail sales usually means the money customers spent, not the income the publisher keeps after shops and online stores take their share, and a game costs much more than a cinema ticket. A film's opening is counted over several days and shows how many people turned up, while a game's day one shows how much people were willing to pay. Even an independent estimate differs: Sensor Tower, an analytics firm, put the early figure at about US$2.36 billion, but it was measuring something different at a different moment.

The costs deserve the same scepticism. Reuters says Take-Two has spent an estimated US$1 billion or more on production, which would make GTA VI one of the most expensive entertainment projects ever. The word that matters is estimated. The company has not published a budget, and the number should be taken with a pinch of salt. High spending does not guarantee success, but it does raise the bar: a game that costs so much must keep earning long after the first day.

That is why the company's own tone is cautious. Take-Two's chief executive said earlier in November that it was not ready to declare victory, and an analyst quoted by Reuters says the most important question for the company's share price is the timing and size of the next GTA Online, more than unit sales. A record day is a remarkable result, but it is only the opening line of a longer story.

9Role-play: buy it now or wait?

Work in pairs. Read your card, then have the conversation. Swap roles and do it again.

Student A · the fan

A big new game comes out today. You want to buy it now, on the first day. Explain why you can't wait and try to persuade your friend to buy it too.

Student B · the careful friend

You think it's better to wait. Ask about the price, the reviews and the other things you could spend the money on. Decide if you will buy it.

Useful phrases

  • I can't wait any longer, it's out today!
  • Why don't we read some reviews first?
  • It's a lot of money for one game.
  • That's a fair point, but I'd hate to miss out.
  • What would you do with the money instead?
  • Let's wait a week and then decide.

Afterwards, tell the class: who was more convincing, and did your friend buy the game on the first day?
