Core version

You are waiting for a friend who is ten minutes late. What do you do? Many of us immediately pull out our phones. We watch a video, check the news or scroll through social media. There is always something to pass the time with, almost anywhere and at any time.

You might think this means people feel less bored than in the past. However, two psychologists writing in the journal Communications Psychology in 2024 discussed evidence that boredom may actually be increasing. They suggested that digital entertainment could be one possible reason.

A phone offers thousands of choices. But when we move quickly from one short video to another, it may become harder to enjoy one thing for long. We get used to something new appearing every few seconds. An ordinary conversation or a slow afternoon can then seem less exciting.

This does not mean that phones are bad or that everybody is more bored today. We use them to talk to friends, learn new things and organise our lives. The researchers were discussing a possible explanation for a bigger change, not proving that every minute online creates boredom.

Boredom can sometimes tell us something useful. Maybe we need a change, a challenge or more contact with other people. It can also encourage us to start a conversation, take up a new activity or simply notice the world around us.

People do not all feel boredom in the same way. A young child may find a long car journey difficult, while an adult might enjoy looking at the scenery. Someone who works in a busy hospital may welcome ten quiet minutes. A person who spends the whole day alone may want more conversation and excitement. This means there is no perfect amount of entertainment for everyone.

The key is understanding why a moment feels empty. Are we tired, lonely or simply looking for something to do? A new video may take our mind off it for a short time, but it might not answer the real need. Sometimes making a plan with a friend or learning something new can be more satisfying. At other times, doing nothing is exactly what we need.

Next time you have five empty minutes, you could try putting your phone away. You might feel uncomfortable at first. Or you might notice something interesting. Either way, being bored for a moment is not necessarily a problem that needs an immediate solution.

Intermediate version

Standing in a queue used to involve looking around, daydreaming or chatting to a stranger. Today, it often takes only seconds before somebody reaches for their phone, and for many of us that movement has become second nature. Entertainment is constantly available, so why do researchers suggest that boredom may be becoming more common?

Think about the last time you stood in a queue without taking out your phone. Did you look around, start a conversation or immediately feel that something was missing?

In a 2024 perspective article for Communications Psychology, researchers Katy Tam and Michael Inzlicht discussed evidence of rising boredom and proposed a possible explanation: digital media may sometimes make boredom worse instead of relieving it. Their article presents an interpretation of existing research, not a single experiment proving that smartphones cause a worldwide increase.

Part of the problem may be the speed with which we consume content. Short videos and endless feeds encourage us to move on as soon as our attention weakens. Having unlimited alternatives can make an ordinary activity feel disappointing. Instead of becoming absorbed in one experience, we give up on it and look around for something more stimulating.

Yet it would be unfair to blame all digital entertainment. People use technology for meaningful learning, creativity and keeping up with the people they care about. Boredom is also influenced by working conditions, emotional health and whether daily activities offer a sense of purpose. A phone can provide genuine enjoyment or simply help someone pass a difficult journey. Being bored is not always a sign that something has gone wrong. It may simply be the point at which our minds stop receiving instructions and begin looking for something of their own to do.

Researchers distinguish between an occasional boring moment and persistent boredom that interferes with wellbeing. The first can be perfectly normal. It may signal a need for a new activity or provide a chance for our thoughts to wander. The second deserves more attention, particularly when it is connected with low mood or isolation.

Boredom also depends on expectations and circumstances. A quiet afternoon can feel peaceful to someone who has had a demanding week but unbearable to a person who feels isolated or stuck. That difference matters when discussing the effects of technology. A phone might connect somebody to friends when they have limited opportunities to meet in person, while the same device could encourage another person to avoid activities they would enjoy more.

The researchers' hypothesis therefore should not become a rule that everybody needs less screen time. It invites us to question a particular pattern of automatic consumption. If we repeatedly check for novelty without becoming satisfied, the answer may involve changing what we do with our devices rather than simply counting the minutes we spend using them.

Perhaps the interesting experiment is not deleting every app but noticing our habits. If every pause is immediately filled, we may never discover whether we need entertainment, connection, rest or a new challenge. Sometimes the ability to tolerate a little boredom gives us room to make that choice.

Advanced version

Modern life offers an extraordinary solution to an ancient human problem: whenever boredom appears, we can summon an almost limitless supply of stimulation. There is always something to watch, yet waiting a few seconds can still feel unbearable. The phone offers an effortless escape, but it may also make ordinary, unstimulating moments harder to tolerate.

In a 2024 perspective published in Communications Psychology, Katy Tam and Michael Inzlicht examined this paradox and proposed that digital media might contribute to the very feeling it promises to eliminate. Their argument is a research-informed hypothesis, not definitive evidence of a single cause. Nevertheless, it raises a useful challenge to the assumption that more entertainment necessarily means greater satisfaction.

Digital platforms often reward rapid switching. A video loses our attention and another begins; an article seems slow and we move on to a new tab before boredom can set in. This can create expectations of immediate novelty against which ordinary activities struggle to compete. The problem is not merely the quantity of content, nor that modern entertainment is uniquely harmful. It is the habit of disengaging whenever stimulation declines, which can prevent us from noticing the difference between a passing lack of stimulation and deeper dissatisfaction.

Boredom itself is more complicated than an empty schedule. Someone can be busy yet deeply bored if their activities feel meaningless. Conversely, a person sitting quietly might be content, curious or creatively absorbed. Persistent boredom can be distressing, but brief periods without obvious stimulation may prompt reflection or reveal unmet needs.

There is also a risk of nostalgia. Earlier generations did not automatically lead richer, more attentive lives, and digital tools can support meaningful friendships, education and artistic work. The effects depend on what people do online, how they do it and what alternatives are available in their lives. None of this requires a heroic digital detox. Leaving the phone in a pocket during a short journey might simply reveal how often we reach for it without deciding to.

The experience of boredom is shaped by context, which complicates any attempt to establish a simple relationship with digital media. Someone recovering from an exhausting shift may welcome passive entertainment, while an isolated teenager may use online communities to find connection unavailable nearby. The same platform can therefore provide relief, meaningful engagement or an unsatisfying sequence of distractions.

The useful question is not merely how long people spend online but whether their use corresponds to their needs and intentions. Habitual switching may prevent sustained involvement in an activity, whereas deliberate participation in a class, community or creative project may have the opposite effect. Treating all screen time as interchangeable obscures those differences and risks replacing one simplistic explanation with another.

The more useful question may concern our tolerance for mental silence. If every gap is filled before we can register it, boredom becomes something we react to automatically rather than understand. Learning when to put a device away may not eliminate boredom, but it could help us recognise what our attention is actually looking for.
