Why Does the Generation Gap Feel So Wide?

We talk constantly about Gen Z, millennials and boomers. Research suggests those labels can explain less than we imagine.

Core version

A family is trying to plan a weekend away. The grandmother picks up the phone and calls everybody, one by one. Her grandson sends a single message to the group chat and puts his phone down. Three hours later nobody has answered it. She thinks he is being rude. He thinks she is wasting an afternoon. Neither of them is trying to annoy the other. They simply have a different habit.

There is an easy word for this: generation. We say that young people do things one way and older people do things another way, and the argument stops there. The explanation is quick and it feels true. It can also hide a great deal.

Some of the difference is real. Eurostat reported in April 2026 that 89.3% of people aged 16 to 29 in the EU had used social networks during 2025. Across the wider population aged 16 to 74, the figure was 67.3%. That is a genuine gap in one everyday habit.

A habit is not a personality, though. The numbers show who opens an app. They say nothing about who is patient, careless, generous or hard-working. Two people of the same age can disagree about almost everything. Two people born forty years apart can want exactly the same thing. A quiet nineteen-year-old and a quiet sixty-year-old may understand each other better than either of them understands a noisy cousin of their own age.

Researchers find this hard to separate. Some things change simply because a person grows older. Some things change for everyone at once, like a new piece of technology or a bad year for the economy. Only a little of it really belongs to one group of people born at a similar time.

Forgetting that has a cost. A stereotype about age can land on anyone. In a European survey in 2023, 45% of people thought age discrimination was widespread, and 52% said age was the main thing that could count against someone applying for a job. Older workers lose out, and so do younger ones.

So the grandmother and the grandson have a choice. One of them can say that people of that age never listen. Or one of them can ask a better question: how would you prefer to arrange this? The second question tends to get an answer.

Intermediate version

One colleague books a meeting. Another replies that a two-line message would have done the job. Both think the other has misunderstood how work is supposed to happen, and both reach for the same explanation: it must be a generation thing.

The phrase is useful because it ends an argument quickly. It is also everywhere. Marketing campaigns, newspaper columns and management courses all sort people into Gen Z, millennials and boomers, until the categories start to feel like facts about human beings rather than labels invented to describe them.

Some of the evidence behind the feeling is solid. Eurostat reported in April 2026 that 89.3% of people aged 16 to 29 in the EU used social networks during 2025, compared with 67.3% of the population aged 16 to 74. Younger people in Europe really are online in greater numbers, and that shapes how they arrange their lives.

What the figure cannot do is explain why. A difference in behaviour might come from age itself, because people at a particular life stage have different amounts of free time and responsibility. It might come from the period everyone is living through, since a new app or a financial crisis arrives for all ages at once. Only what is left belongs to a particular group of people born around the same time, and separating those three things is genuinely difficult.

Workplaces have tested the labels and found them wanting. A 2020 evidence review by the US National Academies examined whether generational categories were useful for managing staff and concluded that they were not. The categories mixed up age, career stage and historical change so thoroughly that decisions based on them were unlikely to be sound.

There is a further problem for readers outside North America. The familiar generation names grew out of American history, with its own wars, economic cycles and cultural moments. Applied to Spain, Ukraine or Argentina, they carry an assumption that everyone lived through the same decades in the same way, which is rarely true.

Meanwhile the labels do real damage. A 2023 European survey found that 45% of people believed age discrimination was widespread, and 52% named age as the main characteristic that could disadvantage a candidate during recruitment. That pressure falls on the youngest applicants as well as the oldest.

None of this means age is irrelevant. It means age is one explanation among many, and usually not the most interesting one. Two colleagues who assume they have nothing in common because of a birth year will probably stop looking, and they will miss the ordinary question underneath the argument, which is how each of them actually prefers to work.

Advanced version

Few explanations are as satisfying, or as lazy, as a generation. Someone books a meeting that could have been a message, or sends a message that should have been a meeting, and the disagreement is filed under a birth year. The label arrives before anybody has asked what the other person was actually trying to do.

The raw differences are not imagined. Eurostat reported in April 2026 that 89.3% of EU residents aged 16 to 29 had used social networks during 2025, against 67.3% of the population aged 16 to 74. That is a substantial gap in how two groups organise their communication, and it would be strange if it left no mark on their expectations of one another.

The difficulty begins when a measured behaviour is promoted into a personality. Survey researchers distinguish between three forces that are easy to confuse. Age effects reflect where somebody stands in life, and they move with the person. Period effects reach everyone at once, as a pandemic or a new platform does. Only a genuine cohort effect belongs durably to those born in a particular stretch of years, and isolating one requires data that most commentary never consults.

A 2020 evidence review by the US National Academies reached an unglamorous conclusion: popular generational categories tend to blur age, career stage, historical context and individual experience, and they are not a sound basis for workforce decisions. The labels survive anyway, partly because they are convenient and partly because they sell.

What they conceal is the size of the variation inside each group. Any birth cohort contains people separated by income, education, city, health and temperament, and those differences are usually larger than the average gap between one cohort and the next. A statement that begins with the average and ends with an individual has quietly discarded almost everything that made the individual predictable.

They also travel badly. The vocabulary of boomers and millennials was built around North American history, and it quietly assumes that a given decade felt the same everywhere. A reader in Kyiv, Seville or Buenos Aires may recognise the words without recognising the experience they are supposed to describe, which is a reasonable warning against treating any of it as universal.

The cost of the shorthand is measurable. A 2023 European survey found 45% of respondents considered age discrimination widespread and 52% identified age as the characteristic most likely to count against a job candidate, a perception that penalises the very young alongside the old. A category invented to describe people ends up deciding what they are permitted to be.

Perhaps the gap feels wide partly because we narrate it so constantly. The habit of explaining a disagreement by cohort makes each side expect less of the other, and expectations have a way of arranging the evidence to suit themselves. It is easy to overgeneralise from one irritating colleague to an entire decade of births. The alternative is slower and almost always more accurate: ask the person in front of you what they need, and let the answer be about them.
