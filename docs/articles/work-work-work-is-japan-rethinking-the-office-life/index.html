Core version

Imagine your work is finished and your bag is packed. But your manager is still at their desk, so you sit down again and pretend to be busy. Some workers in Japan describe this situation. In some offices, employees have felt that leaving first looks disloyal, even when there is nothing useful left to do. After work, there may also be pressure to join colleagues for drinks. The result is a long day in which time at the office can matter more than the work itself.

That culture has had serious consequences. Japan has a word, "karoshi", which means death from overwork. Extremely long hours and work-related stress can damage both physical and mental health. The government has introduced rules and campaigns to reduce overtime, encourage holidays and make workplaces safer. Yet changing a rule is easier than changing the silent expectations between a worker, a manager and a team.

In October 2026, Japan's health and labour ministry published another report on preventing deaths and illnesses linked to excessive work. The report examined workers' compensation cases and efforts to improve working conditions. It showed that the problem has not gone away, even though some employers have changed how they operate.

At the same time, Japan faces a shortage of workers. Its population is ageing, and many industries are finding it difficult to take on staff. Some businesses believe that giving employees more choice about working longer hours could help them remain competitive. In September 2026, the government changed the way it advises companies about a recommended monthly overtime limit. That was not the same as removing all legal limits, but it worried people who had fought for better working conditions.

Not every worker wants the same arrangement. In a labour ministry survey reported in March 2026, 30% of respondents said they wanted to work fewer hours, while about 11% wanted to work more. Many were happy with their current hours. Younger workers may also have different expectations about free time, family life and the relationship between work and personal identity. Some employers are responding with more flexible schedules and less formal office rules.

The difficulty is finding a balance between what businesses need and what people can reasonably give. A shorter working day is not automatically a happier one, especially if employees must complete the same amount of work under more pressure. But long hours are not proof of commitment either. Japan's debate asks a question that matters in every country: should success at work be measured by the hours people spend at their desks, or by the quality of what they do?

Intermediate version

The official working day is over, but nobody moves. One employee has finished every task and wants to clock off in time for the train home; the manager is still at the desk. Leaving first should be an ordinary decision, yet in some Japanese workplaces it can feel like a public statement about loyalty. That small moment helps explain why office reform is not only about legal limits. It is also about the pressure people read in the room.

The dangers are real enough to have produced a specific word: "karoshi", death caused by excessive work. Campaigning and high-profile cases have led to reforms intended to reduce overtime, encourage annual leave and recognise the damage caused by workplace stress. In October 2026, the labour ministry's latest white paper again examined compensation claims and prevention. Its continuing existence shows that written rules have not removed the problem.

Now the debate is moving in a complicated direction. Japan has an ageing population and a severe labour shortage in sectors ranging from care services to manufacturing. Some employers want more flexibility to offer additional hours to people who choose to work them. From September 2026, the government stopped universally urging companies to hold overtime to a recommended 45 hours a month. Existing legal protections were not simply abolished, but critics feared that weakening the message could make it harder for employees to refuse extra work.

Survey results suggest that workers themselves are divided. A labour ministry survey was reported in March 2026. Of the workers who answered, 30% wanted shorter hours and around one in ten wanted longer ones. Most were satisfied with their current schedule. That hardly amounts to an overwhelming demand for a return to late nights at the office. There is also a difference between willingly working extra hours for better pay and feeling that your career depends on never saying no.

Japan's changing social habits offer another clue. Traditional after-work drinking sessions are becoming less central for some younger employees, while businesses increasingly experiment with flexible arrangements and more relaxed rules. Tokyo's metropolitan government has offered a four-day working-week option to eligible staff, though it is not a national requirement. Such changes suggest a growing recognition that caring responsibilities, personal interests and rest deserve space alongside professional ambition.

The challenge is that Japan cannot solve its labour shortage simply by telling people to work harder. Greater productivity, better technology and workplaces that attract people who might otherwise drop out of the labour market all have a role. Ultimately, the most meaningful change may be cultural: creating offices where leaving on time is normal rather than something an employee feels compelled to explain.

Advanced version

At the end of the working day, the decisive fact may not be whether the work is finished but whether the manager has stood up. An employee who leaves first risks appearing less committed; one who remains can display loyalty without producing anything useful. That tension sits at the heart of Japan's long struggle with office culture. Physical presence has often functioned as a visible measure of dedication, even when it becomes a poor measure of achievement.

The consequences have sometimes been devastating. Japan's term "karoshi", death from overwork, reflects a history of workloads associated with serious illness and suicide. Public campaigning, prominent cases and legislative reform have made the issue impossible to dismiss. The labour ministry's October 2026 white paper continued to examine compensation claims, mental-health risks and prevention. Progress is evident, but workplace expectations can survive long after official policy changes.

What makes the current debate particularly revealing is the pressure coming from the opposite direction. Japan's ageing population and shrinking pool of working-age people have left businesses struggling to recruit. Employers have pressed for greater flexibility, arguing that employees who wish to earn more should have opportunities to work additional hours. From September 2026, the labour ministry stopped universally urging businesses to adhere to a recommended 45-hour monthly overtime ceiling. This was a change in guidance, not a wholesale abolition of statutory overtime protections, yet it prompted fears that hard-won reforms could be diluted.

The argument is sometimes presented as a simple choice between economic growth and workers' wellbeing. In practice, the relationship is much more complicated. Someone who wants extra shifts to improve their income may regard tighter restrictions as frustrating. Another employee, anxious about promotion or worried about letting colleagues down, may experience the same supposed freedom as pressure. In a labour ministry survey reported in March, 30% of respondents wanted fewer hours, compared with 10.5% who wanted to work more. Those figures complicate the claim that workers broadly welcome a longer working week.

Meanwhile, other aspects of Japanese working life are changing. The tradition of after-hours socialising with colleagues has weakened in some workplaces, and flexible schedules are gaining visibility. Tokyo's metropolitan administration has offered eligible employees a four-day week option, while some businesses are loosening rigid expectations around appearance and office conduct. These developments are uneven, and a four-day schedule does not necessarily mean a four-day workload. Still, they point towards a different understanding of professional commitment, one that leaves room for family responsibilities and a life outside employment.

Japan's dilemma is not unique. Ageing societies must find ways to sustain public services and economic activity without exhausting the people who deliver them. Asking workers to spend longer at their desks may appear to offer a quick answer, but fatigue, illness and disengagement carry costs of their own. A better workplace would make extra hours genuinely voluntary and reward the work done. People should be able to do a good job and still have time and energy for the rest of their lives.
