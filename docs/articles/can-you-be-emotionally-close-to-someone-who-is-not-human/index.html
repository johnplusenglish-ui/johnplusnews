Core version

Some people say they are in a relationship with an AI chatbot. For many of us, that sounds strange. Is it real closeness, or just a clever copy? In November 2026, researchers at the University of Michigan in the United States shared a study that looks at part of this question.

Lionel Robert and his colleagues Annette Masterson and Xin Ye asked 463 adults to fill in a survey. All of them were already in a relationship. There were 155 people with an AI chatbot, 153 in a long-distance relationship with another person, and 155 who saw their partner in person. The three groups were similar in age, gender, length of relationship and reported depression.

The survey asked about different kinds of closeness, such as sharing feelings and sharing ideas. It also asked about loneliness, happiness with the relationship and general wellbeing. Finally, it asked how much support people got from other people in their lives.

The first result may come as a surprise. People in all three groups gave similar scores for emotional and intellectual closeness. But loneliness told a different story. In both human groups, people who felt more emotionally close to their partner tended to feel less lonely. Among chatbot users, the pattern went the other way. More emotional closeness went with more loneliness, even though it also went with more satisfaction in the relationship.

Then came a twist. When the researchers took into account how much support people had from others, the link between closeness and loneliness in the chatbot group disappeared. This suggests that the people around us matter, and not only the relationship we are thinking about. Intellectual closeness, such as sharing ideas, also went with higher satisfaction and wellbeing for chatbot users.

The study has important limits. It was a survey, so it shows links, not causes. We cannot tell whether chatbots make people feel lonely, whether lonely people turn to chatbots, or whether something else explains both. All the people were adults in the United States, and they described their own lives. The results are a mixed bag. According to Robert, they were more complicated than expected.

So can you be close to someone who is not human? The study suggests that you can feel close to a chatbot and still feel lonely. That is food for thought: closeness and loneliness are not simple opposites.

Intermediate version

When someone says they have a close relationship with an AI chatbot, many of us raise an eyebrow. Is that real intimacy, or a clever imitation of it? A study from the University of Michigan, reported by Phys.org on 12 November 2026, takes the question seriously by comparing chatbot relationships with two kinds of human ones.

The research team, led by Lionel Robert with Annette Masterson and Xin Ye, asked 463 adults in the United States to fill in a survey. All of them were already in a relationship. There were 155 people with a chatbot partner, 153 in long-distance relationships with another person and 155 with a partner they saw in person. The long-distance group was included to check whether any pattern was specific to AI or simply came from being apart. The three groups were similar in age, gender, relationship length and reported depression.

Participants rated several kinds of closeness, including emotional, intellectual and social, and they also answered questions about loneliness, satisfaction, wellbeing and the support they received from other people. On emotional and intellectual closeness, the three groups scored about the same. That may come as a surprise to sceptics.

Loneliness told a different story. In both human groups, people who felt more emotionally close to their partner reported feeling less lonely. Among chatbot users, more emotional closeness was linked to more loneliness, even though it was also linked to greater satisfaction with the relationship. In other words, people could feel close to a chatbot and lonely at the same time.

There was a twist. When the researchers took into account how much social support people had, the chatbot link between emotional closeness and loneliness disappeared. The authors read this as a sign that a person's wider human relationships help to shape how an AI companion affects them. Intellectual closeness was also linked to higher satisfaction and wellbeing among chatbot users, and that link held after support was accounted for.

The limits matter. This was a survey of people's own reports, so it shows associations, not causes. It cannot tell us whether chatbots leave people lonelier, whether lonely people are more likely to turn to chatbots, or whether something else explains both. The sample was also limited to US adults, so the results may not apply elsewhere. According to Robert, the picture was more complicated than he expected.

For now, the study offers food for thought rather than a verdict. It suggests that closeness and loneliness are not simple opposites, and that the question may be less about whether a chatbot can feel close and more about what else is in a person's life. It also reminds us that one study of this size cannot settle the debate.

Advanced version

Debates about AI companions tend to run on instinct. Some people see a lifeline for the isolated, others a double-edged sword that may quietly replace human contact. A University of Michigan study, covered by Phys.org on 12 November 2026 and published in Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans, tries to anchor the argument in evidence by comparing people in relationships with chatbots against two kinds of human couples.

Lionel Robert, Annette Masterson and Xin Ye surveyed 463 adults in the United States, all of them already in relationships: 155 with a chatbot, 153 in long-distance relationships with another person and 155 with a partner they saw in person. The long-distance group serves as a useful comparison, helping to separate what is peculiar to AI from what simply comes with physical separation. The groups were comparable in age, gender, relationship length and reported depression.

Participants rated closeness along several dimensions, including sharing feelings, exchanging ideas, sexual closeness, shared social networks and joint activities. They also reported on loneliness, satisfaction, wellbeing and the social support they felt they had. Ratings of emotional and intellectual intimacy were broadly similar across the three groups, which undercuts any assumption that people in chatbot relationships describe thinner bonds.

The patterns diverged on loneliness, where the chatbot data tell a different story. In both human groups, greater emotional intimacy accompanied less loneliness. Among chatbot users it accompanied more loneliness, although also more satisfaction with the relationship. The paper singles out this apparent contradiction as its most noteworthy result. Once perceived social support entered the analysis, the chatbot link vanished, which the authors take as evidence that human relationships outside the chatbot shape its emotional effects.

Other associations were more specific. Sexual intimacy was linked with greater satisfaction in chatbot and long-distance relationships but not in in-person ones. Intellectual intimacy was linked with higher satisfaction and wellbeing among chatbot users and, among long-distance partners, with wellbeing alone. These links held after accounting for social support. Taken together, the results suggest that the kind of intimacy matters, and that a single label such as intimacy hides several different patterns.

Caution is essential. Every finding is an association, and none shows that chatbots cause loneliness or relieve it: lonely people might equally gravitate towards chatbots, or an unmeasured factor might drive both. The data rest on self-reports from US adults, and people who describe themselves as being in a relationship with a chatbot may differ from other users in ways the survey did not capture. Because the sample is limited to the United States, cultural differences remain untested.

Robert has said the results turned out more complicated than expected, and the study raises more questions than it answers. That may be the fairest summary. It is possible to feel close to something that is not human and to feel lonely at the same time, and what matters most may be how much human support surrounds the relationship. The work was supported by two University of Michigan institutes, according to the journal record.
