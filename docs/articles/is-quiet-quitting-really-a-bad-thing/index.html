Two Colleagues Leave at Six: Only One Has Finished

The label covers everything from sensible boundaries to genuine withdrawal, and it travels badly between workplaces. Gallup's engagement figures are not a count of quiet quitters.

B1 version

A worker arrives on time, finishes their tasks and goes home when the working day ends. They do not answer emails on Sunday or take on extra projects without pay. Are they a bad employee? Some people call this behaviour quiet quitting. The name is confusing because these people have not actually left their jobs. They are choosing to do what their contract requires, without making work the centre of their lives. For some, this is a way to protect their free time. For others, it may show they have lost interest in their work.

A 2026 Gallup report found that only 20% of employees around the world were engaged at work in 2025. Gallup uses engagement to measure how connected workers feel to their jobs and workplaces. This number does not mean that the other 80% were all quiet quitting.

Managers sometimes worry that employees who refuse extra tasks are not committed. They may believe a good worker should help out colleagues and show initiative. However, workers may feel that doing unpaid overtime every week is unfair, especially when salaries are not increasing.

There are important differences between setting limits and doing a job badly. Someone can work hard during paid hours and still keep their evenings free. At the same time, refusing to cooperate with colleagues can create problems for everyone.

The debate also depends on the kind of job someone has. An office worker may choose not to open work emails at night. A nurse, firefighter or restaurant worker may have different responsibilities when something unexpected happens during a shift. Even so, managers should not assume that every emergency can be solved by asking staff to work for free.

Clear rules help both sides understand what is expected. Some teams discuss busy periods in advance and give workers time off after extra hours. Others pay overtime. When people feel treated fairly, they may be happier to help. When rules constantly change, even motivated workers can end up feeling angry. Quiet quitting may sometimes be a sign that trust between employees and employers has already been damaged.

Perhaps the real question is what a reasonable working relationship looks like. Employers need reliable people, and workers need time to live. If both sides agree on clear expectations, leaving the office on time should not have to be treated as a problem.

B2 version

Imagine an employee who performs their duties carefully but refuses to check messages after hours. One manager might describe that person as dependable and sensible. Another might claim they are quiet quitting. The difference says a great deal about changing expectations in the workplace. Consider two colleagues who both leave at six. One has finished their work; the other has spent the afternoon avoiding it. Judging them only by their departure time misses the point. Despite the dramatic label, quiet quitting usually means remaining employed while rejecting the idea that success requires constant extra effort. It became a popular phrase as workers debated work-life balance, unpaid overtime and whether their careers deserved such a large share of their energy.

The discussion takes place against a wider problem of employee disengagement. Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace report found that 20% of employees worldwide were engaged in 2025. However, its categories of engagement are not the same as quiet quitting, and people can be committed to their work without being available every evening.

From an employer's perspective, initiative and flexibility matter. Businesses sometimes face unexpected problems, and colleagues depend on one another. But if absorbing additional responsibilities becomes a permanent requirement, workers may reasonably ask why those duties are not included in their pay or job description. The phrase also travels badly between workplaces. In a team where overtime is rare, doing your contracted hours sounds unremarkable. In another, the same behaviour might be treated as disloyalty.

There is also a difference between healthy boundaries and withdrawing effort. A person who completes agreed tasks well and finishes on time has not necessarily stopped caring. By contrast, someone who avoids responsibilities or leaves colleagues to solve every problem may damage both relationships and results.

The meaning of a reasonable boundary also varies dramatically between professions. An office employee may be able to switch off notifications at the end of the day, while a healthcare worker may face an emergency just before a shift finishes. That does not mean one group deserves unlimited protection and the other unlimited demands. Predictability, compensation and staffing levels help determine whether flexibility feels like cooperation or exploitation.

Employers who rely routinely on unpaid overtime may be using workers' goodwill to paper over problems that require proper investment. Equally, employees who join a team need to recognise that occasional unexpected responsibilities are difficult to eliminate entirely. A healthy workplace makes those trade-offs explicit rather than relying on guilt, vague expectations or assumptions about what committed workers should sacrifice.

The phrase quiet quitting turns a complicated negotiation into an argument about attitude. A more useful discussion might concern fair workloads, realistic expectations and whether employees have any influence over how their jobs are organised. Commitment should not have to mean being permanently on call.

C1-C2 version

The popularity of the phrase quiet quitting reveals how differently employers and employees understand professional commitment. To some managers, limiting effort to the responsibilities set out in a contract represents a troubling loss of ambition. To some workers, the same behaviour represents a long-overdue rejection of unpaid labour and constant availability. The label is provocative partly because it suggests that an employee has withdrawn something they previously owed. Yet the boundary between contractual obligation and voluntary goodwill is rarely made explicit. Despite its name, quiet quitting does not involve resignation. It is an imprecise label applied to behaviours ranging from sensible boundary-setting to genuine disengagement. That ambiguity matters, because a person who works conscientiously for their contracted hours may be unfairly grouped with somebody who has stopped taking their responsibilities seriously.

There is a broader context. Gallup's 2026 global workplace report estimated that just 20% of employees were engaged in 2025. Engagement measures employees' psychological connection to their work and organisation; it is not a direct count of quiet quitters. Treating the figures as interchangeable would create a misleading picture of modern employment. Managers who celebrate staff for 'going the extra mile' may genuinely appreciate dedication. Problems arise when exceptional effort quietly becomes the baseline against which everybody else is judged.

Employers make a reasonable point when they value initiative, generosity and adaptability. Teams rarely function well if everybody insists that unforeseen problems belong to somebody else. Yet the counterargument is equally significant: when exceptional effort becomes routine, organisations may be benefiting from an invisible extension of the working week.

A further complication is unequal bargaining power. Highly paid professionals may negotiate flexible hours, while staff in insecure or low-paid roles risk punishment for refusing extra work. Advice to 'set boundaries' is less useful when an employee cannot afford to lose their job. This is not an argument for rigidly refusing every unexpected request. It is an argument for making the costs of flexibility visible, including whose time is being given up.

There are important differences between sectors that the popular debate tends to obscure. In professional office work, boundaries may primarily concern unanswered evening emails. In transport, retail or emergency services, unexpected demands can arise while staff are physically present and customers need assistance. A fair policy should consider those operational realities without treating workers' personal time as an infinitely available resource.

Compensated overtime, reliable staffing and transparent arrangements for exceptional circumstances can distinguish legitimate flexibility from the normalisation of overwork. The concept of quiet quitting can otherwise become a convenient accusation levelled at employees who refuse to pick up the slack for organisational failures. Conversely, an employee's right to boundaries does not remove responsibilities towards colleagues or the people who depend on a service. The challenge is to negotiate commitments that are both humane and workable.

Perhaps the debate should move beyond deciding whether quiet quitting is virtuous or lazy. It raises a more practical question about the unwritten contract between organisations and their staff. Sustainable commitment depends not just on individual motivation but also on fairness, trust and an honest agreement about what a job actually requires.
