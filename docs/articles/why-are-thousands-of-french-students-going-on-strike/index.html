Why Are Thousands of French Students Going on Strike?

Thousands of teenagers across France are refusing to attend lessons and joining demonstrations instead. They say their schools are falling apart and the government isn't doing enough to help.

Before you read3 questions
  1. Have you ever taken part in a protest or a strike? What was it about?
  2. What are the biggest problems in schools in your country?
  3. Should students have a say in how their schools are run?

Intermediate version

When we think of school strikes, we usually imagine teachers refusing to work because of disagreements over pay or working conditions. But in France, something unusual is happening: the students themselves are leading the protests. Since September 2026, thousands of secondary school students have been organising demonstrations, blocking school entrances and demanding improvements to their education.

Their complaints include overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, broken facilities and schools that have not received enough investment. In some schools, students say lessons are regularly cancelled because there aren't enough teachers. Others complain about buildings that are too hot in summer, too cold in winter or in urgent need of repairs. The protests began after students at a school near Paris complained about serious staff shortages. Their frustration quickly spread to other schools across the country.

On 13 October, more than 160,000 people took part in demonstrations across France, including schoolchildren, university students and trade union members. The movement has also attracted international attention. Greta Thunberg, the Swedish activist who became famous for organising school strikes against climate change, visited students at a secondary school in Saint-Denis, near Paris. She expressed support for their campaign, arguing that young people's concerns about their future deserved to be heard.

The French government has promised to improve the situation. Education minister Édouard Geffray announced plans to send 3,000 additional supply teachers to schools experiencing serious staff shortages. However, many students believe the measures don't go far enough. They want better facilities, smaller classes and more investment in public education. Some teachers and parents support the protests, but others worry about the disruption to learning. When students miss lessons, they may fall behind with their studies, especially if they are preparing for important exams.

The demonstrations have also caused tension with the police. Although most marches have been peaceful, clashes have taken place in several cities, including Paris and Nantes. There have been reports of injuries, arrests and police using tear gas. The government has called for calm and further discussions, while students and human rights organisations have raised concerns about the treatment of young protesters.

Money is another important part of the debate. France faces pressure to reduce government spending and control its public debt. Improving school buildings and employing more staff would require significant investment. Nevertheless, students argue that education should be a national priority. The protests have also opened up a wider discussion about inequality. Children in poorer neighbourhoods may have fewer educational opportunities than those attending well-funded schools in wealthier areas.

For many young demonstrators, the issue is therefore not simply about uncomfortable classrooms or missing teachers. It is about having the same opportunities as everyone else. Whether the strikes lead to lasting changes remains uncertain, but one thing is clear: French students want a greater say in the decisions that shape their education. And this time, they are making themselves heard outside the classroom.

Advanced version

In a country where street protests have long been part of political life, the sight of demonstrators marching through French cities is hardly unusual. What makes the latest wave of unrest different is the age of those leading it. Since late September, thousands of secondary school pupils have walked out of classrooms, blocked school entrances and joined demonstrations demanding urgent improvements to France's education system.

Their grievances are both practical and deeply political. In some schools, lessons are routinely disrupted by staff shortages. Elsewhere, students complain of overcrowded classrooms, deteriorating buildings and facilities that have been neglected for years. What began as frustration at individual schools has developed into a nationwide movement questioning whether France's public education system is delivering the equal opportunities it promises.

On 13 October, more than 160,000 demonstrators took to the streets, including teenagers, university students and trade union members. Among those expressing solidarity was Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg, who visited students at Lycée Paul Éluard in Saint-Denis, a suburb north of Paris. Her appearance provided an international spotlight for a movement that has increasingly drawn comparisons with earlier youth-led campaigns.

Yet beneath the slogans lies a troubling question about educational inequality. France has traditionally regarded public education as a cornerstone of republican equality, promising children the opportunity to succeed regardless of their social background. Students argue that this principle becomes difficult to defend when some schools struggle to provide regular lessons while others enjoy considerably better resources.

The government has acknowledged the problem. Education minister Édouard Geffray announced an emergency programme to deploy 3,000 additional supply teachers to schools facing severe staffing shortages. But the response has done little to resolve the broader dispute.

Student organisations and teaching unions maintain that temporary staffing measures cannot compensate for years of underinvestment. They are calling for sustained improvements in recruitment, infrastructure and educational support. Meanwhile, the government faces a difficult financial balancing act. France is under considerable pressure to reduce its budget deficit and control public debt. Increasing education spending may require higher taxes, reductions elsewhere or further borrowing.

The protests have also raised uncomfortable questions about the policing of public demonstrations. While the majority of marches have remained peaceful, confrontations in Paris and other cities have resulted in injuries and arrests. Rights organisations have criticised what they regard as excessive police force, while authorities point to incidents of vandalism and violence that require a response. President Emmanuel Macron has called for restraint and dialogue, but tensions remain high.

There is a further contradiction at the heart of the movement. Students are protesting because they believe their education is being neglected, yet prolonged strikes may result in additional lost learning, potentially affecting those already facing disadvantages. Supporters counter that continuing to attend inadequately resourced schools without challenging the situation would do little to improve their long-term prospects.

The dispute therefore extends beyond the condition of individual classrooms. It touches on the distribution of public resources, the relationship between citizens and the state, and whether teenagers should be treated merely as recipients of education or as participants in decisions affecting their future. Whatever the immediate outcome, the movement has given thousands of young people their first experience of collective political action. For France's protesting students, the streets have become an unexpected classroom, offering a lesson in democracy that no textbook could fully reproduce.
