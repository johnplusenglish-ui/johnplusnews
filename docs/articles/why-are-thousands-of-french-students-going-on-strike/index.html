The Teenagers Who Walked Out of School

Secondary-school students across France are blocking their own gates. They say the buildings, the class sizes and the staffing have been ignored for years.

Core version

When students refuse to go to school, it is usually because they want a day off. But in France, thousands of teenagers have been missing lessons for a very different reason. They are protesting because they say their schools need more teachers, better buildings and more money. In September and October 2026, the protests spread to several parts of the country.

At some schools, students say lessons are cancelled because there are not enough staff. They also complain about crowded classrooms and buildings that need repairs. At a school in Saint-Denis, near Paris, students described poor conditions and demanded action. They blocked entrances and joined in demonstrations. Soon, other young people were organising their own protests. Students in other towns said they were putting up with the same problems.

On 13 October, large demonstrations took place across France. More than 160,000 people joined them, according to government figures, although the organisers gave a higher number. The Swedish climate campaigner Greta Thunberg visited students in Saint-Denis to show her support. The protests attracted attention far beyond France. Students argued that their education was being affected and that adults in power had not listened to them.

The French government said it would send in thousands of extra replacement teachers to schools that needed them. But many protesters believed this was not enough. They wanted a longer-term plan to improve classrooms, school buildings and teaching. Some parents and teachers supported the students, while others worried that staying away from lessons would make learning even harder. There were also clashes with police during some demonstrations, creating new arguments about how protests should be controlled.

There is no easy answer. Improving public schools costs money, and governments must decide how to spend limited budgets. But students in poorer areas may already have fewer opportunities than children in wealthier places. For the teenagers taking part, the protests are about more than missing a lesson. They want schools that give everyone a fair chance. Many of them say they are not asking for luxury, only for lessons that actually take place. Their biggest challenge is turning attention in the streets into real changes in the classroom.

Intermediate version

Since late September 2026, thousands of secondary-school students in France have walked out of lessons, blocked school gates and marched through city centres. Their complaint, as they put it, is straightforward: their schools are struggling, and the government is not doing enough to fix them.

Students describe overcrowded classrooms, buildings in poor condition and lessons cancelled because no teacher is available. The campaign gained attention in Saint-Denis, near Paris, where pupils at Lycée Paul Éluard protested against serious problems at their school. What began as anger over local conditions quickly grew into a wider movement. Young people in other cities recognised similar problems in their own schools and began to organise.

On 13 October, demonstrations brought more than 160,000 people onto French streets, according to official estimates. Organisers put the figure much higher. Greta Thunberg, who became internationally known through school strikes for climate action, visited students in Saint-Denis to support their demands. Her presence gave the campaign further publicity, although its main concerns remained practical: reliable lessons, decent buildings and enough teachers to cover the timetable.

The government promised to provide 3,000 additional replacement teachers in the worst-affected areas. Student groups and teaching unions welcomed attention to the problem but argued that temporary measures would not solve years of underinvestment. France also faces pressure to control public spending, making large improvements difficult to fund. A classroom may need repairs urgently, but money spent on schools cannot be spent elsewhere without changes to the budget. Unions argue that the shortage of teachers has been building up for years and will not be cleared up by short-term contracts.

The protests have not been entirely peaceful. Some marches led to clashes with police, injuries and arrests, prompting concerns about the treatment of young demonstrators. Authorities said they needed to respond to disorder, while students and campaigners accused police of using too much force. Meanwhile, some parents questioned whether missing weeks of school could damage the very education the protesters were trying to improve.

That contradiction is central to the debate. Continuing to attend poorly equipped schools may seem pointless to frustrated teenagers, yet prolonged disruption could leave them further behind. The movement also raises the question of educational inequality. If children in richer areas have more reliable lessons and better facilities, how equal are their opportunities? Whatever happens next, these students have spelled out what they expect: to have a say in decisions that shape their future.

Advanced version

The gates are blocked and the placards are handwritten. Many of the people demanding change are pupils who would normally be in class. Since late September, secondary-school students across France have protested about run-down buildings, staff shortages and what they say is too little money for public education.

At Lycée Paul Éluard in Saint-Denis, north of Paris, complaints about inadequate facilities became a focus for wider frustration. Students described disrupted timetables and an environment that fell short of the standards promised by the education system. Their grievances resonated well beyond the school gates. By 13 October, nationwide demonstrations had drawn more than 160,000 participants according to official figures, with organisers estimating substantially larger crowds. Greta Thunberg's visit to Saint-Denis brought international attention, though the campaign had already developed its own momentum.

The demonstrations expose a difficult tension in France's understanding of public education. Schools are supposed to embody republican equality, offering children opportunities that do not depend on their parents' income or postcode. Yet pupils argue that shortages and deteriorating infrastructure are not distributed evenly. When one school repeatedly cancels lessons and another is adequately staffed, the promise of equal opportunity becomes increasingly hard to defend. Pupils in Saint-Denis have been quick to point out that this gap is not an abstraction but their daily timetable.

The government's response has included a pledge to roll out 3,000 additional replacement teachers. That may alleviate immediate disruption, but campaigners insist that stopgap staffing arrangements cannot make up for sustained underinvestment.

Ministers face another political constraint: pressure to contain public expenditure and debt. Better recruitment, maintenance and classroom support require money, while competing priorities make additional spending contentious. The protests have laid bare the effects of years of underinvestment, so the dispute is as much about national choices as it is about local school conditions. Ministers who concede the principle while holding back the money are unlikely to satisfy anyone for long.

Policing has become a further source of controversy. Most demonstrations have been peaceful, but confrontations have produced injuries and arrests, and campaigners have accused officers of excessive force. Authorities point to incidents of violence and the need to maintain public order. These competing accounts underline the difficulty of policing a movement involving teenagers, many of whom are encountering organised political activism for the first time. The debate has widened from educational standards to the limits of protest and the state's responsibility towards young citizens. Those are questions that will not be settled by clearing a street.

There is an uncomfortable paradox at the heart of the strikes. By refusing to attend lessons, students risk losing more of the teaching they say they are being denied. Supporters respond that accepting the status quo carries its own long-term cost. Whether the movement secures lasting changes remains uncertain. What is already evident is a generation of pupils unwilling to be treated as passive recipients of government policy. In demanding better schools, they are also insisting on a voice in the institutions that shape their lives.
