What Is It Like to Live Next to a Data Centre?

Britain is building data centres to power artificial intelligence. In Newport and Cardiff, the people living beside them are asking who benefits.

Core version

Dale Johnston has a garden on the edge of Newport, in south Wales. There is a trampoline and a slide on the lawn for his grandchildren, and two chairs for him and his wife. On an autumn evening he should be watching the light fade out of the sky. Instead he looks at a wall of grey panels 23 metres high.

The building is a data centre. Inside are thousands of computers that store information and run online services. When you save a photo, stream a film or ask a chatbot a question, machines in a building like this one may be doing the work. Artificial intelligence needs a great deal of computing power, so companies are building more of these centres and making them much bigger.

Local politicians have welcomed the Newport centre as a source of jobs. Johnston is thinking about what he has lost. “Before, the sun used to come around here and it was lovely,” he told the Guardian. He says the way residents have been treated is “disgusting”, and that the politicians who backed the project will not come to his garden to look at it.

He is worried about noise as well as light. The centre has 36 sets of large coolers to stop the computer chips overheating, and 60 back-up generators that have to be tested regularly. He already keeps his bathroom window shut because of a hum from another building on the same site. His wife is a nurse who works night shifts, and she struggles to sleep.

Newport is not the only place having this argument. In Britain, 315 big data centres are waiting for a connection to the electricity network. Together they have asked for far more power than the country normally uses on a cold winter day. That number is a request, not a measurement. Nobody yet knows how much electricity each building will really use, because that depends on how it is built and run.

In Cardiff, people are fighting a plan to put a data centre on a car park used by hospital staff. More than 1,200 people have objected to it.

Johnston can talk about little else. The internet is often called a cloud, which sounds light and far away. His garden is a reminder that it is made of concrete, and that it has to stand somewhere.

Intermediate version

Dale Johnston's garden on the western edge of Newport has a trampoline, a slide and two chairs. It also has a view of 23 metres of grey prefabricated panelling, because a hyperscale data centre now stands behind his small enclave of 27 houses. “Before, the sun used to come around here and it was lovely,” he told the Guardian.

Data centres hold the computers that store and process information for the services most of us use every day. Artificial intelligence has made them far hungrier for space and electricity, and the industry argues that only larger and larger facilities can meet the demand. In Britain, 315 hyperscale centres are queuing to connect to the national grid. The power they have applied for adds up to considerably more than the country's typical peak demand on a cold winter day, although an application is not the same as a measurement of what will actually be consumed.

Johnston's objections are specific rather than abstract. The centre has 36 sets of air blast coolers to stop its chips overheating, and 60 diesel back-up generators that will be tested regularly to protect the flow of data. He expects a constant drone and worries about fumes. He already closes his bathroom window against the hum of another building on the same 46-acre campus, and his wife, a nurse on night shifts, can barely sleep. Labour politicians presented the project as a job creator. Johnston says he has invited councillors and MPs to stand in his garden, and that none of them has come.

In Cardiff, a similar argument is louder. The council wants to sell a park and ride used by hospital staff to a developer planning a data facility. More than 1,200 objections have been lodged, and the Welsh government has ordered the council to pause the application. Sue Elias, a retired librarian, has leafleted the car park every Wednesday for six weeks and collected 1,300 signatures. Nick Richards, who moved onto a new estate two months before the application appeared, lives 300 metres from a proposed energy centre with seven gas turbines. “I feel trapped,” he says. The developer says the scheme cannot proceed without full compliance with environmental and public health rules, and air quality assessments it commissioned estimate that emissions will be within safe levels.

The councillor who defended the Cardiff plan argued that some of the claims circulating were scaremongering, and noted that the centre would employ up to 125 people once built. Three weeks after a packed public meeting in a local boxing club, he submitted a formal objection of his own.

Advanced version

The word cloud was a marketing triumph. It made a global industrial estate sound like weather. For Dale Johnston, the part of the cloud nearest his house is 23 metres of grey prefabricated panelling, standing behind an enclave of 27 homes on the western fringe of Newport. He has a trampoline and a slide out for his grandchildren and, as of this year, very little sky.

Johnston's case against the hyperscale data centre beside him is not a case against computing. It is a list: 36 sets of air blast coolers that will most likely hum continuously, 60 diesel generators that must be tested so the flow of data is never interrupted, a bathroom window he already keeps shut against noise from another building on the same 46-acre campus, and a wife on night shifts who cannot sleep. The politicians who welcomed the development as a job creator have, he says, declined his invitation to come and see it.

The national figures explain why this conversation is spreading. Britain has 315 hyperscale data centres queuing for a grid connection, and their combined requests substantially exceed the country's typical peak winter demand. That comparison deserves care: it describes what developers have applied for, not electricity anyone has used, and real consumption will depend on how each facility is designed, cooled and operated. Globally, spending on new capacity has been estimated in the trillions of dollars by 2030, which indicates how much of this is a speculative bet on an AI-led economy rather than a response to demand already in evidence.

What makes the politics awkward is distribution. The services these buildings support are used almost everywhere, while the panels, turbines and generators stand in particular postcodes, often poorer ones. In Cardiff, a council wants to sell a park and ride relied on by hospital staff to a data centre developer. Residents have lodged more than 1,200 objections, a retired librarian has gathered 1,300 signatures, and a filmmaker who moved onto a new estate two months before the planning application arrived now lives 300 metres from seven proposed gas turbines. The developer says the scheme cannot proceed without full regulatory compliance, and the air quality assessments it commissioned put emissions within safe limits. Residents are being asked to find that reassuring.

There is a serious case on the other side, and it is not only about employment, since the Cardiff facility would itself employ up to 125 people. Publicly owned computing can do obvious good: a university supercomputer in Bristol supports dementia and heart disease research. Campaigners who have actually read the planning documents tend to argue for enforceable standards and public ownership rather than outright refusal.

The most instructive detail is what happened to the councillor who called the objections scaremongering. Three weeks after a boxing club filled up with his own constituents, he submitted a formal objection himself.
