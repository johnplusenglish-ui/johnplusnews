Core version

Imagine your feed is full of sad news and angry comments, and you feel worse after ten minutes. Many people call this doomscrolling, which means scrolling for a long time through bad news. Now there is a new word for the opposite habit: bloomscrolling.

Bloomscrolling means choosing a feed that is full of things that make you smile or feel calm, such as art, cute animals or kind messages. The word has recently become popular on TikTok. The way people use it today is linked to the writer Lucy Bloom. She deleted her Instagram account and started again, and this time she only followed accounts about art. The app slowly showed her more and more art. She said she felt calmer and more motivated than before. For her, it was a breath of fresh air.

How does it work? Apps learn from what you do. On TikTok, if you linger on a video, watch it again or like it, the app learns that you want more videos like it. If you scroll past quickly, it learns the opposite. TikTok says its system looks at videos you view, like or share. You can also press "Not interested", add words you want to filter out, or refresh your For You feed.

You can also mute comments or unfollow people who make you feel bad. Some people keep a different account for each mood.

How many people do this? A toy company called Hasbro paid for a survey, and it says that 93% of people aged 18 to 34 have changed their feeds to make them happier. That is a big number, but you should take it with a pinch of salt. The people in the survey are adults, not teenagers, and a company paid for the research, so it is not a neutral study.

Here is the most important point. Nobody has shown that a kinder feed makes people healthier or happier. A calm feed may feel pleasant, and that is fine. But a nice feed can also keep you scrolling for hours. At the end of the day, you are still the one who decides when to stop. If your phone often makes you feel bad, taking a break or talking to someone you trust may help more than any trick.

Advanced version

Most of us know the feeling of opening a phone for a quick look and surfacing an hour later, slightly worse off. People call it doomscrolling: a long session of reading distressing news. A new term has now appeared for the opposite habit, bloomscrolling, which describes shaping your feed so that it shows art, cute animals, kind messages and other pleasant material.

The word is not new. It has been around for a few years, and it recently took off on TikTok. Prof Ruha Benjamin popularised it with a different meaning, as a way of seeking out organisers, artists and educators whose work gives people reasons to believe that change is possible. The current, lighter sense is linked to the author Lucy Bloom, who deleted her Instagram account and started again with a feed made only of art accounts. Her feed soon filled with art, artist residencies and paint, and she described feeling calmer and more motivated. It is a vivid story, but it is one person's account of her own experience, and for her it was a breath of fresh air rather than a measured result.

The mechanism is straightforward. Recommendation systems respond to behaviour, so lingering on a video, letting it replay or liking it tends to bring more of the same, while scrolling past tells the system you are not interested. TikTok's transparency page says its system draws on videos you view, like or share, and it lists user controls: a "Not interested" option, keyword filters and a refresh of the For You feed. The same page says it tries to include a variety of content, so a feed will not always match your tastes exactly.

The most quoted statistic is that 93% of 18 to 34 year olds have curated their feeds to make them happier. It comes from research commissioned by the toy maker Hasbro, which, according to a news report, released it alongside a Play-Doh range for adults. The coverage we found did not give the sample size or the method, and the figure describes adults, not teenagers. Like any company-funded figure, it deserves to be taken with a pinch of salt, because it tells us that many young adults say they tidy their feeds, not that doing so is good for them.

That gap is the heart of the matter. Nobody has shown that a kinder feed improves mental health. A pleasant feed is not necessarily a healthy one, because a stream of uplifting clips can be just as hard to put down as a stream of bad news. The Guardian piece that explains the idea is a light, humorous column, and even its headline jokes that bloomscrolling is a new way to waste your own time.

None of this makes curation pointless. Muting accounts that upset you is a reasonable habit, and being more deliberate about what you see is better than drifting. But the sensible position is modest: it is a small tool, not a cure. Whether the benefit comes from the content, from the sense of control, or from nothing more than a pleasant mood is an open question, and if a phone regularly leaves you feeling low, talking to someone you trust is likely to help more than any trick for your feed.
