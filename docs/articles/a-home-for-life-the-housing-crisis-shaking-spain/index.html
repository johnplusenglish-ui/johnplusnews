It was where she had spent almost her whole life. But in September 2026, at the age of 87, she was forced to leave after a long disagreement over her rental contract.

The building had been bought by new owners, and a legal battle followed. Maricarmen had an old agreement that allowed her to pay a relatively low rent. Housing campaigners stood by her as she fought to stay. On 23 September, police arrived to carry out the eviction. Neighbours and supporters gathered outside, but she was removed from the building and taken to hospital on a stretcher.

Maricarmen died on 7 October, about two weeks later. She had already been in poor health. Her supporters said the dispute had caused her serious stress, but the eviction has not been shown to be the cause of her death. Although an agreement had been reached that might have allowed her to return, she never went back home. Her death led to demonstrations and renewed anger about Spain's housing crisis. Protesters said no one should have to face losing a lifelong home in old age.

Across Spain, many people struggle with high rents and the price of buying a home. Madrid, Barcelona and other popular cities have become particularly expensive. Young adults may work full time but still be unable to afford their own place. Some share crowded flats, and others put off moving out of the family home. Holiday rentals and property investment are also part of the debate because people worry that there are fewer homes available for residents.

There are different views about the best solution. Housing groups want stronger protection for tenants and more affordable homes. Property owners argue that they also have rights and that Spain needs to build more housing. Maricarmen's story has become a symbol because it shows what these arguments can mean for one person. Behind every discussion about rents and laws, there is someone who simply wants a secure place to live.

The case attracted attention because millions of Spaniards recognise the fear behind it. Rents and purchase prices have climbed in many cities, while earnings have often failed to keep pace. Young workers can find themselves priced out of neighbourhoods where they grew up. Some continue living with family well into adulthood; others spend a large share of their income on a room in a shared flat. Popular tourist areas face additional pressure from short-term holiday rentals.

The argument over who is responsible quickly becomes political. Campaigners criticise property investment firms and call for stronger tenant protections, rent controls and more public housing. Landlords and other critics warn that strict rules may discourage people from renting out homes and argue that too little new housing is being built. Both sides claim that their approach would make the system fairer, but neither can offer a quick solution to years of rising demand and limited supply.

Maricarmen's death prompted further demonstrations, with supporters demanding changes in the law. Her story should not be reduced to a slogan: she was a person with a long life, a family and a home she wanted to keep. But the public response illustrates why housing debates have become so emotional. For those worried about losing their home, the issue is not an investment or an economic statistic. It is whether the place they belong can suddenly become somewhere they can no longer afford to stay.

Advanced version

Maricarmen Abascal had lived in the same Madrid flat for more than 70 years. At 87, she knew its rooms and the streets around it as home. In September 2026, a long legal dispute ended in her eviction. The case brought a vast housing argument down to one woman's front door.

After the property changed hands, Abascal became embroiled in a battle over an older tenancy agreement that allowed her to remain on relatively favourable terms. Campaigners viewed the effort to force her out as an example of the pressure placed on long-standing residents in an increasingly lucrative housing market. On 23 September, despite a gathering of supporters outside the building, the eviction proceeded. Photographs of the frail woman being carried away on a stretcher became a stark illustration of the human stakes of a legal process.

Abascal died on 7 October. She had been in poor health, and campaigners said the prolonged conflict caused severe distress. The circumstances prompted widespread outrage, although attributing her death directly to the eviction would go beyond the available medical evidence. An agreement that could have enabled her return had reportedly been reached, but came too late for her to resume living in the flat.

Her case prompted protests in Madrid and elsewhere, turning a dispute over one tenancy into an argument about housing policy. Protesters said they wanted the law changed so that nobody in old age could lose a home they had lived in for decades.

The pressures are familiar in much of the country. In major cities and popular coastal destinations, demand for housing has risen amid limited supply, tourism and investor interest. Wage growth has not consistently matched the cost of renting or buying, leaving younger workers struggling to establish independent households. Holiday accommodation removes some properties from the longer-term rental market, although its impact varies by location. The cumulative result is a growing sense that employment no longer guarantees access to a secure home.

Competing explanations produce competing remedies. Tenant organisations call for greater security, stronger regulation and investment in social housing. Property owners and some economists warn that excessive restrictions may reduce the incentive to rent homes, arguing instead for more construction and a faster planning process. Neither position should obscure the structural complexity of the crisis. The market consists not only of investors seeking returns but also of residents whose social connections and daily routines can be destroyed by displacement.

That is why Abascal's story struck a nerve. The law may establish who has the right to occupy a particular flat, but it cannot fully express the significance of decades spent living there. A successful housing policy has to grapple with affordability, supply and legal certainty; it must also acknowledge that a home is not interchangeable with any other address. Behind the arguments about market efficiency lies a simple concern: whether people can remain in the communities to which they have devoted their lives.
