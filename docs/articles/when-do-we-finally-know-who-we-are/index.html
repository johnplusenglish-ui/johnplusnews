When Do We Finally Know Who We Are?

A Finnish study that followed the same people between the ages of 27 and 61 found that identity kept developing well into adulthood, and that stronger identity development went together with better mental wellbeing.

Core version

Many people think that the big question of "Who am I?" belongs to the teenage years. At sixteen, we try out different styles, groups and ideas. Then, the story goes, we grow up, settle down and stay the same for life. But is that really true? A long study from Finland suggests that it is not.

Researchers at the University of Jyväskylä used information from an unusually long project. It has followed the same people for more than 50 years. For this study, the team looked at how people described their identity at the ages of 27, 36, 42, 50 and 61. They also measured mental wellbeing at 36, 42, 50 and 61.

In this study, identity means your view of who you are and what matters to you in different areas of life. The researchers did not only ask what people believed. They asked how firmly people held their views, and whether those views came from their own thinking or were taken from other people or from society. A well-developed identity has clear views that a person has chosen after exploring.

On average, identity became stronger in this way between the ages of 27 and 61. Most of the change happened between 27 and 42. So people did not stop developing at the end of their twenties. In their thirties and early forties, the group was still working out who they were.

The researchers also found a link with mental wellbeing. They looked at psychological wellbeing, such as having a sense of purpose, and social wellbeing, such as feeling connected to your community. They also measured life satisfaction, anxiety and depression. People whose identity developed in a more positive way tended to have better psychological and social wellbeing and fewer feelings of anxiety.

But a link is not proof that one thing causes the other. The study did not test which comes first. A person with a clear identity may feel better, but feeling better may also help a person build a clear identity. It is a chicken and egg question. The reports we read also do not say how many people took part.

Still, the finding is reassuring. If you do not yet know your own mind at thirty or forty, you are not failing. Identity may stay a work in progress for a long time. One study cannot tell you what will happen to you.

Intermediate version

If you had to pick the age when people ask themselves who they are, you would probably say the teenage years. Identity has often been treated as a task for adolescence, partly because very few studies have followed people for long enough to see what happens afterwards. A research team in Finland has now done exactly that, and their findings suggest that we are still working on ourselves long after we have left school.

The data come from the Jyväskylä Longitudinal Study of Personality and Social Development, which has followed the same individuals for more than 50 years. Professor Lea Pulkkinen led it from 1968 to 2012, and Katja Kokko has led it since 2013. It is mainly funded by the Research Council of Finland. In a paper in the Journal of Adult Development, doctoral researcher Liinu Marin and colleagues looked at identity when the participants were 27, 36, 42, 50 and 61, and at mental wellbeing at 36, 42, 50 and 61.

The researchers define identity as a person's view of who they are and what they consider important in different areas of life. It gives a sense of continuity over time. To measure how it develops, they looked at how firmly people were committed to their views, and whether those views had been formed through personal exploration or adopted from significant others or society. A well-developed identity, in this sense, rests on clear views that someone has explored for themselves.

On average, identity developed in a positive direction between the ages of 27 and 61, and most of the change took place between 27 and 42. Marin says this shows that identity can continue to develop throughout adulthood. In other words, a typical person in their thirties was still doing a good deal of work on the question of who they are.

The second part of the study looked at mental wellbeing, measured through psychological and social wellbeing, life satisfaction, and feelings of anxiety and depression. Psychological wellbeing covers things such as autonomy and a sense of purpose, and social wellbeing covers feeling connected to communities and society. A more positively developed identity was associated with better mental wellbeing, for example better psychological and social wellbeing and fewer feelings of anxiety.

It would be easy to read too much into that. The study did not examine the causal direction of the association. Marin points out that mental wellbeing may also support or challenge how identity forms, so the effect may cut both ways. These are also average trends, and the reports we read do not give the number of participants. The version on Phys.org appears to be based on the university's own announcement, so the two accounts are not independent.

What can we take from it? For a start, the idea that we should have everything worked out by thirty looks like a myth. A bit of searching in your thirties or forties may be a normal part of adult life.

Advanced version

Popular accounts often place the search for identity in adolescence. The teenager experiments, the adult settles, and by the time you are paying a mortgage the question of who you are is supposed to have been answered. One reason this picture has survived is that few studies have followed people through adult life, so there has been little evidence to challenge it.

A team at the University of Jyväskylä has drawn on one of the rare datasets that can. The Jyväskylä Longitudinal Study of Personality and Social Development has followed the same individuals for more than 50 years. It was led by Professor Lea Pulkkinen from 1968 to 2012 and by Katja Kokko since 2013, and it is primarily funded by the Research Council of Finland. In a paper in the Journal of Adult Development, Liinu Marin and colleagues examined identity at ages 27, 36, 42, 50 and 61, and mental wellbeing at 36, 42, 50 and 61.

Identity here is defined as a person's view of who they are and what they consider important in different areas of life, supplying a sense of continuity. The researchers assessed its development through two questions: how firmly people were committed to their views, and whether those views were reached through personal exploration or taken over from significant others or society at large. A well-developed identity is one with clear views a person has examined and chosen.

On average, identity developed positively from 27 to 61, with most of the movement between 27 and 42. Marin concludes that identity can continue to develop throughout adulthood. That is a modest but useful correction to the assumption that adulthood is mainly a time for living out an identity formed earlier.

The association with wellbeing is the more intriguing result. Wellbeing was measured broadly, covering psychological wellbeing such as autonomy and purpose in life, social wellbeing such as connectedness to communities, life satisfaction, and feelings of anxiety and depression. More positive identity development was associated with better mental wellbeing, in particular better psychological and social wellbeing and less anxiety.

The researchers are careful here, and readers should be too. The study did not examine causal direction, and Marin explicitly notes that mental wellbeing may support or challenge the formation of identity. This is a classic chicken and egg problem: a clearer sense of self may protect against anxiety, but a calmer mind may also make it easier to explore and commit to a sense of self. The accounts we have do not report the sample size, and results are averages, so they say nothing certain about any one person. The Phys.org article appears to repeat the university's own press announcement, so it is not independent confirmation.

The practical lesson should therefore be drawn with care. It would be going too far to claim that working on your identity will improve your mental health. But the findings do challenge the notion that being unsure of yourself in your thirties or forties is a sign of failure. It may simply mean that, like many people, you are still a work in progress.
