Core version

Look at a picture of a street. There are no names, no maps and no help. Can you say which country it is? What would you look at first?

A game called GeoGuessr asks exactly this question. The game shows you a picture taken on a street somewhere in the world. You look for clues, and then you click on a map to guess the place.

In September 2026, the best 24 players in the world met in Berlin, Germany, for the world championship. About 2,000 fans came to watch, and the matches were also shown live online.

The players use small clues. They look at road signs and the writing on them, for example Hindi, Japanese Kanji or Russian letters. They look at plants and buildings. They know special things about each country, like the shape of a road post. Some players even know small things about the car that took the picture.

The final was exciting. A player from Hungary, known as Debre, played against Blinky from France. Blinky was the winner in 2024, and he led 2-0 early. But Debre came back and won 3-2. In the last round, the players saw only one picture. They could not move, turn or zoom. Debre made a brave guess in northern Namibia, and he was right. Blinky chose Argentina.

A good player does not only guess. A good player explains why. A picture can give you clues, but a clue is not proof. The same plant may grow in many places. So players think about what is likely and what is not.

Next time you look at a photo, play the game in your head. What are the three clues you see? And what ordinary detail of your own country do you think a visitor would notice?

Intermediate version

Imagine someone shows you a photo of an ordinary road, with no names, no map and no captions. You have one minute to decide which country it is in. What would you look at first?

That is the idea behind GeoGuessr, a game in which you are placed in a street-view image somewhere in the world, look for clues, and then place a guess on a map. In September 2026, the game's world championship took place in Berlin, as part of a four-day fan festival called GeoFest. According to the organisers, 24 of the world's best players took part, and every match was streamed live on Twitch and YouTube.

The German newspaper taz, which reported on the final, says that about 2,000 fans came to the Tempodrom arena. The matches were best-of-five, with different rules in different games. In some, players can move along the street. In others, they can only turn and zoom. In the last, they see one image and cannot move, turn or zoom at all. Each guess has a 60-second limit.

What do players look at? According to taz, they use street signs and the scripts on them, such as Hindi, Japanese Kanji and Cyrillic, as well as plants, buildings, telephone codes and regions. Some even recognise features of the camera car that took the picture, such as an antenna in Russia or a snorkel-like device in Kenya. Some clues are reliable, such as a script, and others are much weaker.

The final was between Szabolcs Attila, known as Debre, from Hungary, and Blinky from France, the 2024 champion. Blinky led 2-0. Debre won the next three games and took the match 3-2. In the decisive round, the players saw one image with no moving, turning or zooming. Debre made what taz calls a bold hedge guess in northern Namibia, and it was correct. Blinky guessed Argentina.

It is worth remembering that a landscape does not prove a country. A plant or a building may appear in many places, so good players weigh their clues and accept uncertainty. Explaining a good guess can be more interesting than guessing correctly by luck.

Advanced version

Imagine you are dropped on a road somewhere on Earth. There is no map, no label and no name. You have sixty seconds. What do you look at first? Geography helps, but so does noticing what other people overlook: a road sign, a plant or the shape of a roadside post.

That is the idea behind GeoGuessr, in which players are shown a street-view image, look for clues and place a guess on a map. The 2026 world championship was held in Berlin from 2 to 5 September, as part of a four-day fan festival called GeoFest. According to the organisers, 24 of the world's best players took part, and every match was streamed live. The German newspaper taz reported that about 2,000 fans came to the final in the Tempodrom arena.

Matches were best-of-five, and the rules changed from game to game. In some, players can move along the street. In others, they can only turn and zoom, and in the decisive fifth game they see a single image and cannot move, turn or zoom at all. The clues players use, according to taz, include street signs and their scripts, plants, buildings, telephone codes and regional features, and even details of the camera car, such as an antenna in Russia or a snorkel-like device in Kenya.

The final shows how that works under pressure. Szabolcs Attila, known as Debre, from Hungary, who had lost the 2025 final, faced Blinky of France, the 2024 champion. Blinky led 2-0, but Debre won the next three games and took the match 3-2. In the final round, with a single image and no movement, Debre made what taz calls a bold hedge guess in northern Namibia. A hedge is a guess placed to be reasonably close to several possible answers. It turned out to be correct. Blinky guessed Argentina.

Debre's guess shows how a hedge works: it is placed where it would still be reasonably close if other clues pointed elsewhere. Good players weigh evidence against assumption, accept that they may be wrong and choose the guess that costs least if they are.

There is also a caution. A landscape or a building does not prove a nationality, and a plant that grows in one country may also grow in others. The game rewards probabilities, not certainties, because a lucky guess, however impressive it looks, is not the same thing as a good explanation.

The most interesting part may be what the game teaches you to see at home. Ordinary details, such as the shape of a road post, the colour of a bus stop or the way a sign is written, become invisible to the people who live with them every day. A visitor notices them at once.

If you had to describe the place where you live using only three ordinary details, which would you choose?

Try it

In pairs, write three invented descriptions of places (not real streets or homes). Give each description two useful clues and one distracting detail, for example: "a bus stop with a red roof, a shop sign in a curly script, and a very large dog". Swap with another pair and ask them to make a tentative guess and explain which clues they used and which they ignored. No live maps, personal locations or game accounts are needed, and nobody should describe where they live.
