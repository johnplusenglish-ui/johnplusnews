A footbridge, a famous view and a crumbling cliff. Civita draws visitors all summer, but keeping people there through the winter is the harder problem.

Core version

The bridge to Civita di Bagnoregio hangs high above a valley. Visitors walk across it towards stone houses, narrow streets and a view that looks almost unreal. Then they look down. Rain and wind are slowly wearing away the soft rock beneath the village. Parts of the hill have already disappeared. Civita is beautiful, famous and in physical danger, which is why local people have long called it the Dying Town.

The village was founded more than 2,500 years ago, but only a small number of people now live permanently in its old centre. Tourists turn up in large numbers at busy times, then leave again. A crowded afternoon can hide the harder question: who will live and work there all year? A real community needs safe buildings, services and jobs, not only visitors taking photographs.

In October 2026, Italy's culture minister announced that Civita and the nearby town of Bolsena had won the title of Italian Capital of Contemporary Art for 2028. Their joint project will receive one million euros for cultural activities. The plan includes art projects, events and spaces where creative people can work. Organisers also hope to involve young people and schools. These are plans for the years ahead, not results yet.

The announcement is good news for the area. Exhibitions and cultural events could bring visitors at different times of the year and encourage people to stay longer. Local cafés, guesthouses and small businesses may benefit. Art could also give young people more reasons to study and work nearby instead of moving to a bigger city. But attracting tourists is not the same as building a community where people want to live all year.

There is also a problem that art cannot solve on its own. Civita's hill is still slowly changing, and protecting its buildings needs engineering work and money. The new cultural title offers a chance to look beyond the village's famous views and think about its future. Whether it succeeds will depend on what happens after the visitors have gone home. A village needs more than attention to stay alive.

Intermediate version

Halfway across the footbridge to Civita di Bagnoregio, the village seems to float above the valley. Its medieval buildings attract cameras from every direction, but the dramatic view is also a warning. Erosion has been eating into the soft rock for centuries. The hilltop is not merely picturesque; it is unstable. Civita's nickname, the Dying Town, describes both the land falling away and the tiny community trying to remain on it.

The settlement dates back more than 2,500 years, yet only a handful of people now live permanently in its old centre. Tourism keeps businesses going, but it does not automatically restore the services, employment and ordinary routines that make year-round life possible. In summer, the bridge can be full. In winter, the difference between a successful attraction and a living village becomes much harder to ignore.

In October 2026, Civita and the nearby lakeside town of Bolsena received an unexpected opportunity. The two places were chosen together as Italy's Capital of Contemporary Art for 2028, beating other finalists including Capri, Padua and Pozzuoli. The winning project, called "Arte che viene, arte che va", will receive one million euros in public funding. Its organisers hope to develop exhibitions, creative spaces and programmes involving schools and young artists.

The decision is more ambitious than simply organising a festival for visitors. Supporters want contemporary art to become part of local life, connecting old buildings and landscapes with new ideas. If the programme attracts artists, students and cultural organisations, it could generate work and help businesses outside the main tourist season. Small communities can sometimes benefit more from steady activity than from occasional crowds.

There are reasons to be cautious, however. Tourism does not automatically reverse population decline. A village can become a popular place to visit while becoming too expensive or inconvenient for local people to live in. Civita also faces an unusually serious physical threat. The erosion of its hilltop requires engineering expertise, careful planning and long-term investment. Art cannot hold the ground together.

Still, the award changes the conversation. Instead of being known only as a beautiful place that may eventually disappear, Civita has a chance to become a centre for new cultural activity. The true test will come after 2028, when the exhibitions have ended and the publicity has died down. If the project helps people work, create and remain in the area, it may offer the village something more valuable than another successful tourist season.

Advanced version

The long footbridge to Civita di Bagnoregio creates the perfect approach to a beautiful place, and exposes the reason for its fragility. Medieval houses cling to a shrinking fragment of hilltop above the eroded valleys of northern Lazio. Visitors pause to photograph the spectacle; beneath them, rain and wind continue working on the soft volcanic material. "La Città che Muore", the Dying Town, is not simply an evocative nickname. The ground beneath the village is slowly wearing away.

Civita has survived since Etruscan times, but permanent settlement has dwindled to a tiny community. Tourism sustains cafés and guesthouses without solving the familiar problems of rural depopulation: limited employment, fragile services and the departure of younger residents. The contrast between a crowded visitor route and a nearly empty village raises an awkward question. Can a place remain culturally alive if it functions mainly as scenery? The question is hardly unique to Civita, but few places in Italy pose it quite so starkly.

An answer, or at least a new experiment, came along in October 2026 when Civita and nearby Bolsena were jointly designated Italy's Capital of Contemporary Art for 2028. Their proposal, "Arte che viene, arte che va", won against entries from Capri, Padua and Pozzuoli, securing a million euros of public funding. The scheme envisages more than temporary exhibitions. Its ambitions include creative infrastructure, collaboration with educational institutions and opportunities for younger artists to engage with the region.

The choice reflects an increasingly influential view of culture as a tool for regional development. A well-designed programme can bring in investment, new partnerships and year-round activity to places otherwise known largely for their heritage. Contemporary art may also challenge the tendency to treat historic villages as finished objects, picturesque surroundings to be consumed rather than communities capable of change.

Yet cultural projects often struggle to convert short-term attention into durable economic opportunities. Funding cycles come to an end, visiting artists move on, and the institutions left behind are frequently too thinly staffed to shore up what the programme began. Whether Civita and Bolsena can avoid that familiar pattern is, for the moment, an open question.

Civita's particular vulnerability makes this tension sharper. Art can stimulate discussion and attract resources, but it cannot remedy the erosion of the volcanic material supporting the settlement. Conservation and engineering remain indispensable, as does investment in the practical conditions that enable permanent residents to stay. There is a risk, too, that an increasingly celebrated village becomes more attractive to visitors and property investors than to people seeking an ordinary home.

The significance of the 2028 programme will therefore depend on what survives it. Temporary installations and a crowded calendar may bring welcome publicity, but they will not in themselves secure a future for Civita or Bolsena. More meaningful success would involve permanent spaces for creative work, stronger local institutions and reasons for younger generations to remain connected to the area. The Dying Town does not need art to disguise its fragility. It needs a cultural project capable of working alongside the less glamorous business of keeping a community alive.
