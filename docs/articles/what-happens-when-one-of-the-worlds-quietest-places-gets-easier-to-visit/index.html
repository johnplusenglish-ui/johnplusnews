What Happens When One of the World's Quietest Places Gets Easier to Visit?

Greenland now has three new airports and more visitors than ever, but tourism is still a small part of its economy. Some residents worry about what a rush of visitors could do to a quiet place with a fragile environment.

Core version

Greenland is the biggest island in the world, and one of the quietest places. Only about 56,000 people live there, and almost 80% of the land is covered in ice. There are no roads between the towns, so people travel by boat or plane. It has always been a remote place, and for many visitors getting there was too much trouble.

That is changing. Greenland has opened three new airports: in the capital, Nuuk, in 2024, in Qaqortoq in April 2026, and in Ilulissat. On 29 October 2026 the first direct flight from Copenhagen landed in Ilulissat, a town of about 5,000 people which is famous for its huge icebergs. The flight took four and a half hours, and the prime minister was one of the passengers. Visitors can also fly there directly from Iceland.

Why does Greenland want more visitors? Tourism brings in money and jobs. The Guardian reports that nearly 180,000 visitors came in 2025, more than three times the number of people who live there. The country has long depended on fishing, and it does not want all its eggs in one basket. Its leaders also hope to become independent from Denmark one day, and they say that needs a strong economy.

Still, tourism is small. In 2024 it made up 4.9% of the economy, and about 1,800 people worked in it. Those figures come from Greenland's first official tourism accounts, published by Visit Greenland, the organisation that promotes tourism there.

Not everyone is happy. In parts of Spain, Italy and Portugal, local people have protested about too many tourists, and some people in Greenland worry about the same thing. A former regional mayor asked how many visitors Ilulissat can take in before its environment, its people and its services are overwhelmed. A researcher says some American travellers were put off by political tension over the island, and the land itself is fragile.

So tourism is a mixed blessing. It can create work and income, but it can also change a quiet community. Greenland's challenge is to make sure local people feel the benefit, and that a place far off the beaten track stays special.

Intermediate version

Ilulissat is a town of about 5,000 people on Greenland's west coast, known as the city of icebergs. Until recently, travellers arrived by cruise ship, took a small plane from Iceland, or flew to another Greenlandic airport and changed to a smaller aircraft. On 29 October 2026 that changed when the first direct flight from Copenhagen touched down on a new 2,200 metre runway. The prime minister, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, was one of 169 passengers.

Ilulissat's airport is the third in a quick series. Nuuk, the capital, opened an international airport in late 2024, and Qaqortoq followed in April 2026. For an island of about 56,000 people where almost 80% of the land is ice and there are no roads between towns, that is a big change. The Guardian reports that Greenland welcomed nearly 180,000 visitors in 2025, its highest figure yet, and that this year looks set to beat it. Counting methods differ between sources, though. The Associated Press put international visitors at almost 150,000 in 2024, so numbers from different reports do not always line up.

The government's reasoning is mostly economic. Greenland has depended on fishing for generations, and the Guardian argues that melting polar ice puts that industry under serious threat. The territory also hopes for independence from Denmark, and Mr Nielsen has said that a strong economic base is needed first. Tourism looks like one way to spread the risk, since leaders do not want to put all their eggs in one basket.

The numbers show how far there is to go. Greenland's first official tourism accounts, published by Visit Greenland and prepared by a consultancy, say that in 2024 tourism directly produced 4.9% of GDP and about 1,800 jobs, over 6% of all jobs. The Associated Press notes that the expected boom has not fully arrived. An Arctic tourism researcher it quoted, Carina Ren, adds that some American visitors were put off by recent political tension over the island.

Tourism is a double-edged sword, though. Local people in parts of Spain, Portugal, Italy and France have protested about mass tourism, and the worry is present in Greenland too. A cruise ship was blocked from entering Ilulissat, where the Guardian says locals feel they see little benefit from foreign operators. A former regional mayor told the Associated Press that the environment, population and infrastructure could be overwhelmed, and asked how many people the town can invite.

It is worth knowing who is speaking. The Associated Press reporter flew on a trip organised by Visit Greenland, which the agency stated openly, and Visit Greenland is government owned and wants more visitors. That does not make the facts wrong, but it is a reason to read with care. The real test is whether Greenland can grow without losing the calm that attracts people in the first place. A place can be off the beaten track one year and crowded the next.

Advanced version

By the standards of mass tourism, Greenland barely registers. Tourism supplied 4.9% of its GDP in 2024, according to the territory's first official tourism satellite account, and about 1,800 people worked in the sector directly, a little over 6% of all jobs. Yet the way the island is being reorganised around visitors is striking. Nuuk opened an international airport in late 2024, Qaqortoq followed in April 2026, and on 29 October the first direct flight from Copenhagen landed at a new Ilulissat airport with a 2,200 metre runway long enough for larger aircraft.

The motives are plainly economic and, increasingly, political. Greenland, with about 56,000 people, has long relied on fishing, and the Guardian argues that melting polar ice makes that industry's future precarious. The government wants independence from Denmark eventually, and Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen told the Associated Press that independence needs a firm economic footing. The American threats to acquire the territory, which the Guardian says filled residents with anger and apprehension, have sharpened that urgency. Tourism is attractive because it offers growth without giving up control to anyone else, and it spares the economy from keeping all its eggs in one basket.

Whether the boom is real is harder to say than the headlines suggest. The Guardian reports nearly 180,000 visitors in 2025 and expects a higher figure this year. The Associated Press gives almost 150,000 international visitors in 2024 and says the expected surge has yet to arrive. These figures do not quite add up, though they need not conflict, since the sources may count different kinds of visitor, perhaps including cruise passengers, and that is a good reason to treat any single figure with caution.

The tension that interests most observers is local. People in Spanish, Portuguese, Italian and French hotspots have protested against the strain tourism puts on daily life, and Greenland is not immune. The Guardian notes concern around the Nuuk airport about the effect on a historically isolated community and a pristine landscape, and a cruise ship was blocked from entering Ilulissat, where residents feel they see little return from foreign operators. A former regional mayor told the Associated Press that the town's environment and infrastructure could be overwhelmed. His question, how many can we invite, deserves a better answer than anyone has offered yet.

It matters, too, who is telling the story. The Associated Press reporter travelled on a trip organised by Visit Greenland, which the agency disclosed, and Visit Greenland, wholly owned by the government, commissioned the tourism accounts from a consultancy. Neither fact undermines the numbers, but both remind us that the loudest voices on tourism often have an axe to grind.

The sensible reading is cautious. Better access is a double-edged sword: it can bring jobs, income and a wider economic base, and it can also hasten the very changes that make a remote place worth visiting. If local people are consulted early and the benefits are shared beyond a handful of operators, Greenland may avoid the worst problems seen elsewhere. If not, a land that sells silence could find it has traded its greatest asset for a short-lived boom. Nobody can say yet how it will turn out.
