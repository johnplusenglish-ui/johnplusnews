Core version

Imagine missing work because of a new video game. Would you feel embarrassed, or would you think that taking a day off for something you love is perfectly normal? When Grand Theft Auto VI arrived in November 2026, some fans were ready to do almost anything to play it straight away. They stayed up all night and went to launch events.

The game was eagerly awaited. Fans had waited thirteen years for a new main game in the Grand Theft Auto series. The Guardian reported on 19 November that players around the world had skipped work or school and stayed up through the night to play the new game. It takes place in a made-up American state called Leonida and follows a crime story about two main characters. The game is rated 18, so it is made for adults.

The excitement was not just about people posting online. A company called Remote said that requests for time off work around the world rose by 32% on launch day. On 20 November, the company behind the game said its estimated first-day sales in shops and online were $3.79 billion. That is an enormous amount of money for a single day. However, it was a figure from the company itself, and it was an estimate. Other estimates of the game's sales were also being talked about.

Why would people take time off for a game? For some, gaming is their favourite hobby, just as sport, books or music are important to other people. A big release can feel like a festival that friends enjoy together.

Others think taking time off to play is going too far. Staying awake all night can leave people tired and unable to concentrate the next day. Someone who misses important responsibilities may disappoint family members or colleagues. But taking an agreed holiday from work for a hobby is different from pretending to be ill. People use their free time for many activities that others may not understand.

Perhaps the interesting question is why we judge some hobbies more seriously than others. Nobody is surprised when people travel to watch a football final or queue for a concert. For dedicated fans, a major game release can feel just as important. The challenge is enjoying the experience without letting it take over everything else. Would you use a day of your holiday to play a new game, or would that be a waste of time?

Intermediate version

Imagine booking a day off work, not to travel or attend a family event, but to spend twelve uninterrupted hours playing a video game. Some people would consider that a perfect use of their holiday. Others might think it was childish or irresponsible. The release of Grand Theft Auto VI in November 2026 brought this disagreement into the spotlight, as fans across the world stayed awake all night, attended launch events and made room in their schedules for an eagerly awaited game.

The Guardian reported on 19 November that enthusiasts had skipped work or school and organised overnight gaming sessions to celebrate the arrival of the new title. Players had waited thirteen years for another main instalment in the Grand Theft Auto series. The new adventure is set in the fictional American state of Leonida and follows two central characters through a crime story. The game is rated 18, so it is meant for adults.

The numbers were extraordinary too. The human resources platform Remote said there was a 32% increase in leave requests worldwide on launch day. On 20 November, Rockstar Games reported estimated first-day retail sales of $3.79 billion. That amount is the company's own estimate of what players spent, so it should be attributed rather than treated as an independently checked total. Before the launch, the research firm Omdia had estimated that the game could earn $3.2 billion in sales by the end of the year, so the estimates differ.

For many gamers, the appeal goes beyond the screen. A launch gives friends a shared experience, something to talk about at school or work and a chance to discover surprises before they are spoiled online. People may even arrange time off in advance, much as music fans plan for a festival or sports supporters travel for an important match. From that perspective, using a holiday for gaming is no stranger than spending it on another favourite hobby.

However, there is a difference between choosing how to spend your free time and neglecting your responsibilities. Missing an important shift without permission affects other people, while staying awake all night can damage concentration and mood the next day. Cost matters too. A streamer told the BBC that consoles are very expensive at the moment, so some fans can only enjoy the story through other people's streams. Excitement can easily get out of hand when everyone online seems to be talking about the same release.

The debate may reveal more about our attitudes towards hobbies than about gaming itself. People rarely question someone who takes a day off for a football final or an expensive concert, yet they may judge a person who does the same for a video game. Perhaps leisure is valuable because we enjoy it, not because other people approve of it. Would you consider a day spent gaming a well-earned break, or would you rather use your free time for something else?

Advanced version

Would you take annual leave specifically to play a video game? For some people, the idea sounds like the perfect reward after a demanding week. For others, it suggests a hobby getting completely out of hand. The arrival of Grand Theft Auto VI in November 2026 turned that question into an international talking point. After thirteen years without a new main instalment, fans organised overnight sessions, rearranged commitments and immersed themselves in a fictional world they had been anticipating for more than a decade.

Reporting on 19 November, the Guardian described enthusiasts around the world skipping work or school, attending launch parties and staying awake through the night. The latest instalment of the enormously successful Grand Theft Auto franchise, which is rated 18 and so aimed at adults, takes place in Leonida, a fictional American state, and follows a crime-focused storyline involving two central characters. The intensity of the response highlights how gaming releases can function as cultural events rather than ordinary entertainment purchases, with fans eager to share the experience while avoiding spoilers.

The commercial numbers reinforced that impression. The human resources platform Remote reported a 32% increase in leave requests worldwide on launch day. On 20 November, Rockstar Games announced estimated first-day retail sales of $3.79 billion, a record, according to the company, and a figure that reflects what players paid rather than what the publisher keeps. That figure requires careful attribution because it was based on company estimates rather than an independent audit. The Guardian reported a different forecast from the analytics firm Omdia, which expected the game to reach $3.2 billion in sales by the end of the year. Even allowing for uncertainty, the attention surrounding the game illustrates the extraordinary economic and cultural power of major interactive franchises.

What makes someone willing to take time off for a digital world? Anticipation is part of the answer, but the social element matters too. Games can provide shared stories, challenges and communities, especially when millions of people are discovering a new experience at roughly the same time. In that respect, a major launch resembles a concert, sporting final or long-awaited film premiere. There is something satisfying about being present when a cultural moment unfolds rather than hearing about it later.

Of course, enthusiasm has limits. Someone who takes an agreed holiday is exercising a reasonable choice about leisure, whereas an employee who disappears without warning may leave colleagues covering their responsibilities. Sleep deprivation and prolonged gaming sessions can also have practical consequences. The pressure to participate immediately, amplified by social media and marketing, may make an enjoyable hobby feel like an obligation. Fans can end up chasing the event rather than deciding how they genuinely want to spend their time.

The more revealing question may be why society grants some pastimes greater legitimacy than others. A person travelling across the country for sport is often described as passionate; someone arranging leave for a game may be dismissed as immature. Such judgements depend heavily on cultural expectations, not on a consistent measure of how worthwhile an activity is. Leisure need not be productive to have value, but it should leave room for ordinary responsibilities. If you had waited thirteen years for something you loved, how much of your working week would you be willing to give up for it?
