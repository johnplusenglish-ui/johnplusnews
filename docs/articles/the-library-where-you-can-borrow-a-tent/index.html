The Library Where You Can Borrow a Tent

America's first public nature library lets visitors borrow tents, binoculars and wildlife tools. Could it get more people outdoors?

Before you read3 questions

Core version

What can you borrow from a library? Books, of course. Perhaps films or music. But at a new library in Colorado, in the United States, you can also borrow camping equipment, binoculars and tools for exploring nature. The Anythink Nature Library opened in August 2026 with an unusual aim: helping people spend more time outdoors, even if they do not own expensive equipment.

The library is in Thornton, near Denver. It has thousands of books and around 850 sets of equipment that visitors can borrow. Some contain tools for watching birds or studying insects. Others help people go hiking, look at stars or try camping. The idea is simple. If you want to learn about nature, you should not have to buy everything before getting started.

The building itself is part of the experience. It stands beside open land and has gardens, paths and places for children to explore. There are rocks to climb, areas where children can play in mud and plants that attract birds and insects. The library also uses solar panels and an energy-efficient heating and cooling system. Staff organise activities to help visitors learn new skills.

Families who visited soon after it opened described how useful the service was. One mother borrowed a camera that helped her young son photograph insects and flowers during a walk. Another family borrowed books about birds and planned to try a kit for looking at stars. Some visitors said that camping equipment would normally be too expensive to buy for a single weekend.

The library's director, Mark Fink, believes that people do not need to travel far into the mountains to enjoy nature. A local garden, a bird outside the window or a short walk can be interesting too. For children growing up in cities, having the chance to touch plants, climb rocks and explore outside may be especially valuable.

The nature library is still a public library, so people can read, study and borrow ordinary books. What makes it different is the idea that learning can happen outside as well as inside a building. A tent, a camera or a pair of binoculars might encourage somebody to try something new. Perhaps the library's most useful gift is not the equipment itself, but the confidence to go outdoors and discover what is there.

Intermediate version

Most libraries lend books. A new public library in Colorado will also lend you a tent, a wildlife camera or a pair of night-vision binoculars. The Anythink Nature Library, which opened in Thornton in August 2026, aims to remove some of the barriers that prevent people from spending time outdoors. Instead of asking visitors simply to read about nature, it offers them the equipment and space to experience it for themselves.

The idea is particularly attractive to families who would like to try camping or birdwatching but cannot justify buying expensive equipment. Alongside its large book collection, the library offers about 850 sets of nature-related equipment, including microscopes, gardening tools, hiking supplies and cameras. Visitors can borrow a kit, learn how to use it and decide whether the activity interests them before spending any money of their own.

The building stands on a 15-acre site next to a much larger area of open land. There are walking paths, gardens designed for different plants and animals, and places where children can climb on rocks or play in mud. The library also uses solar panels and geothermal heating and cooling. It is designed not only to provide information about the natural world but to become part of the outdoor environment it encourages people to explore.

Early visitors have already described the benefits. One family borrowed an outdoor camera and a navigation device before taking their seven-year-old son on a hike. Another borrowed a gardening kit to plan a space that would attract bees and other pollinators. For people unfamiliar with camping or wildlife, access to the right equipment can make an unfamiliar activity feel far less intimidating.

There is a wider social argument behind the project. Outdoor activities can involve costs that are easy to overlook: transport, suitable clothing, specialist tools and time away from work. Those barriers mean that green spaces, even when they are technically open to everyone, may not feel equally accessible. The library's director, Mark Fink, argues that experiencing nature need not involve an ambitious expedition. Noticing birds in a local neighbourhood can be a meaningful starting point.

The project also asks us to think again about what a public library is for. Libraries have traditionally provided shared access to things individuals might not be able to afford, especially books and information. Lending outdoor equipment follows the same principle, only with a wider understanding of learning. A visitor might leave with a novel, a tent or a new interest in the natural world. The point is that curiosity should not depend on the size of someone's bank account.

Advanced version

A public library has traditionally offered a modest but radical proposition: knowledge should be available whether or not you can afford to buy the book. In Thornton, Colorado, that principle has been extended to the equipment required to explore the natural world. The Anythink Nature Library, opened in August 2026, lends not only books but also tents, wildlife cameras, binoculars, microscopes and a range of other outdoor equipment. Its ambition is to make access to nature less dependent on disposable income, specialist knowledge or the confidence to set off into unfamiliar surroundings.

Alongside a conventional collection of tens of thousands of items, the library provides approximately 850 nature-focused equipment kits. The building stands on a 15-acre site next to open land, with gardens, walking trails and spaces designed for practical exploration. Children can scramble over boulders or play in mud, while adults can learn about drought-resistant gardening, wildlife photography or stargazing. Solar panels and a geothermal heating and cooling system reinforce the environmental purpose of a public building that treats the outdoors as an extension of its reading rooms.

The strongest argument for the project emerges in the experiences of its visitors. One mother borrowed an outdoor camera and navigation equipment, allowing her young son to explore and photograph insects and flowers. Another family used gardening materials to plan a garden more welcoming to pollinators. Parents who felt intimidated by camping described how borrowing equipment lowered the financial and psychological stakes of trying something new. The library does not promise to turn every visitor into an accomplished naturalist. It offers a manageable first encounter with activities that might otherwise remain out of reach.

That distinction matters. Access to public green space is often discussed as though the presence of a park or trail were sufficient. In practice, enjoying the outdoors can require money, transport, time, appropriate clothing and a sense of belonging. Families working long hours, older people with limited mobility and city residents unfamiliar with rural environments may encounter very different obstacles. Anythink's director, Mark Fink, has emphasised that nature need not be synonymous with remote mountains or lengthy camping trips. It can begin with learning the names of birds visiting a garden.

There is an environmental argument, too, although it deserves a little caution. Direct experience of wildlife may encourage people to value conservation more deeply, and borrowing shared equipment can reduce the need for individual purchases. Yet the relationship between environmental knowledge and behaviour is not automatic. Owning fewer objects is useful only if the lending system is accessible, maintained and well used. The library's success will depend not merely on an impressive building and an inventive catalogue, but on whether residents continue to return and discover practical value in the service.

At a time when public libraries are frequently required to justify their existence, the Colorado experiment offers a more expansive definition of what they provide. Its central innovation is not the tent itself but the removal of a threshold. A person can try camping without buying equipment, learn to recognise local birds without first becoming an expert, or explore a garden without owning one. The institution remains recognisably a library: a place where resources are shared so that opportunities need not belong exclusively to those who can afford them.
