Has Streaming Beaten the Cinema?

Streaming has changed an ordinary film night. Cinemas now need to offer an experience worth leaving home for.

Core version

It is Friday evening and there is a new film you want to see. You can put on a coat, travel across town and buy a ticket. Or you can stay where you are, make something to eat and stream it on the screen in the corner of the room. Millions of people make that choice every week, and more and more of them stay at home.

An American poll gives a sense of the scale. AP-NORC asked 1,182 adults in August 2025 about their film habits. Three quarters of them had watched a recently released film at home instead of going to the cinema at least once during the previous year. Only 16% said they go to a cinema at least once a month.

The reasons are not hard to guess. At home you can pause the film, eat your own food and watch in comfortable clothes. One monthly payment covers the whole household, while cinema tickets are bought one at a time. For a family, the difference in price is large.

The cinema has not disappeared, though. In Europe, about 796 million cinema tickets were sold in 2025. That is fewer than the year before, but it is still an enormous number of people choosing to leave the house for a film.

It is also not a straight choice between the two. Plenty of people stream most weeks and still book tickets a few times a year. The habit has changed, not the taste.

What do they get for the journey? A screen far bigger than any television. Sound you can feel. And a room full of strangers reacting at the same moment, which is something a sofa cannot provide. Research by the British Film Institute in 2025 found that younger adults valued the cinema partly because it is a place where they actually watch, without checking a phone.

That may be the real change. The cinema used to be the normal way to see a new film. Now it is a decision, and people save it for the films they think deserve it. The question is no longer whether you watch at home. It is which film is worth the trip.

Intermediate version

Friday night presents the same small decision in most households. There is a film worth seeing, and there are two ways to see it: buy tickets and travel, or wait a few weeks until it arrives on a service you already pay for. Convenience usually wins.

The evidence from the United States is striking. In a poll of 1,182 adults conducted in August 2025, AP-NORC found that three quarters had streamed a recently released film at home rather than watching it in a cinema at least once in the previous year. Around a third streamed a new release at home every month, while only 16% visited a cinema that often.

Those figures describe American viewers, and they describe frequency rather than loyalty. The same poll suggests many people do both. What has changed is which option feels automatic, and the automatic option is now the one that requires no coat, no timetable and no second ticket.

Money matters too. A single subscription covers a household for a month, and nobody has to agree on a start time. Cinema prices are paid per person, per visit, and the cost rises with the size of the group. For a family of four, an ordinary evening out can cost more than a month of watching at home.

Who goes also depends on what they earn. In the same American poll, 72% of adults earning $100,000 or more had been to a cinema, against 54% of those earning under $50,000. When a night out costs four tickets, the sofa is not only more convenient; for some households it is the only realistic option.

Cinemas have responded by selling the atmosphere a living room cannot reproduce. The British Film Institute's audience research, carried out in March 2025, found that young adults in the UK valued the cinema as a focused and social occasion. Only 14% wanted cinemas to relax their rules about talking or phone use, which suggests that the attention is part of the attraction rather than an inconvenience.

The industry figures show survival rather than collapse. European admissions fell by around 5.5% in 2025 to roughly 796 million, yet box office revenue stayed broadly level. Worldwide, takings rose about 5% that year while remaining some 30% below the average before the pandemic. Different markets are moving in different directions, and no single number captures all of them.

So the honest answer is that streaming has won the ordinary evening and the cinema has kept the occasion. An audience that chooses to be there, for a film that rewards the journey, is a smaller business than the one cinemas used to run. It is not obviously a dying one.

Advanced version

The interesting thing about the decline of cinema is how reliably it is announced and how stubbornly it fails to finish. Audiences did not abandon the building; they stopped treating it as the default. A film at home now requires no decision at all, which is precisely the problem for everybody who sells tickets.

The numbers describe a shift in habit rather than a verdict. AP-NORC surveyed 1,182 American adults in August 2025 and found three quarters had watched a recently released film at home instead of going out for it at least once in the preceding year. Roughly a third streamed a new release monthly, against 16% who went to a cinema that often. The poll is American and measures frequency, so it cannot be stretched into a global judgement, but the direction is not in doubt.

Price explains a good deal of it. A subscription is a fixed household cost that does not grow with the number of people watching, while a cinema charges per seat and per visit. Once the same film is available both ways, the building has to compete on something other than access, and access was the thing it used to own outright.

What remains is distinctive rather than convenient. Scale, sound and the absence of a pause button amount to an argument for attention, and audiences appear to understand it as such. The British Film Institute's audience tracker, fielded in March 2025, found that young adults in the UK prized the cinema as a focused and sociable outing, with only 14% wanting venues to be more relaxed about talking or phones. They are not asking for the living room with a larger screen.

The trade figures suggest an industry finding a smaller equilibrium rather than falling off a cliff. European attendance dropped about 5.5% in 2025, to somewhere near 796 million admissions, while box office revenue held almost steady. Global takings grew roughly 5% in the same year and still sat about 30% below their pre-pandemic average. Recovery has been uneven, and in several Asian markets it has been driven by national films rather than Hollywood releases.

The growth is also not where a Hollywood-shaped account would predict. The Observatory credits Asia's largest markets with most of the 2025 increase, and national films led the box office in several of them. A global figure assembled from very different national recoveries should be handled carefully.

A cinema that survives on occasions rather than habits is a different business, and the change carries a risk worth naming. If only spectacle justifies the journey, the quieter films that once filled a Tuesday screening may lose their route to an audience. That would be a loss of range, not merely of revenue.

Still, the shift is not simply a defeat. Something is being sold that streaming genuinely cannot supply: two hours in which the film is the only thing happening, in a room of people who all agreed to turn up. Scarcity has a way of making that feel more valuable, not less.
