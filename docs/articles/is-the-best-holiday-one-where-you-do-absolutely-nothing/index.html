The Trip You Need a Holiday to Recover From

Slow travel means fewer places and more unplanned hours. It is not automatically cheaper or greener, and it is hard to manage on limited annual leave.

Core version

Imagine going on holiday and waking up without any plans. You have no museum tickets, no early train and no list of places you must visit. You could sit in a café for two hours or take a walk and see where you end up. Would that sound relaxing or boring?

Many holidays are full of activities. People want to visit famous places, eat at popular restaurants and take beautiful photographs. When they have only a week away, they may feel that every minute needs to count. But a busy holiday can leave people feeling tired.

A 2025 survey by the Marist Poll found that 45% of Americans planning a summer holiday said their main aim was rest and relaxation. Other people wanted adventure, cultural experiences or time with family and friends. The results remind us that travellers do not all want the same thing.

A slower holiday might mean staying in one place rather than changing hotels every night. You could explore local streets, go swimming, read a book or simply enjoy a long lunch. There may be fewer exciting photographs, but also less pressure to be somewhere at a particular time.

Of course, doing nothing is not everybody's dream. Some people have saved money for years to see famous sights. Others love climbing mountains or visiting museums.

Travelling slowly can also change how we spend money. Instead of paying for many tickets and long journeys, someone might choose a simple apartment and cook a few meals. Staying in one place may reduce transport costs, but longer holidays still need time away from work. Others enjoy organised trips because they do not have to think about every detail themselves.

A family with young children may want an easy beach holiday, while a person who loves history may happily spend each day in museums. What matters is giving yourself enough time to enjoy your choices. Even a short weekend can feel restful if you do not try to fit a week's activities into two days.

Perhaps the best holiday is not the busiest or the quietest one. It is the one that gives you what you actually need. Before planning every hour, it may be worth asking: am I going away to see everything, or to enjoy being somewhere different?

Intermediate version

A week in a new country can sometimes feel like an unusually demanding job. There are famous landmarks to visit, restaurants to book and photographs to take before moving on to the next destination. Travellers return home with hundreds of pictures and a strange feeling that they need another holiday to recover.

A packed itinerary can be enjoyable, especially on a first visit. But there is little room for surprises when every meal, museum and train journey has been booked months in advance.

Against this approach is the appeal of slow travel: spending more time in fewer places, allowing ordinary moments to become part of the experience. Instead of rushing between attractions, someone might spend a morning in a neighbourhood café, wander without a plan or return to the same beach several days in a row.

The desire for rest is not imaginary. In a July 2025 Marist Poll, 45% of Americans who planned a summer holiday said rest and relaxation were their main goal. The survey also found travellers who preferred seeing family, exploring new places or experiencing culture. It does not prove that slow travel is becoming more popular everywhere, but it illustrates that relaxation is a major reason for travelling.

Slow holidays may also offer a different relationship with a destination. Staying longer can create opportunities to discover local routines rather than concentrating only on the most famous attractions. At the same time, visitors should be careful not to romanticise ordinary communities as entertainment created for tourists. Slow travel also means accepting that you will miss things. Instead of treating that as a failure, travellers can enjoy getting to know a smaller area properly.

There are practical disadvantages. Some travellers cannot afford extended time away, while others reasonably want to see as much as possible when visiting a distant country. And for people who enjoy adventure, an empty itinerary might feel like a missed opportunity rather than freedom.

Slower travel can also influence spending and the local economy. Staying in one area may mean fewer expensive transfers and more opportunities to use neighbourhood shops and restaurants. But its environmental impact depends on much more than how long a visitor remains. The journey to the destination, the type of accommodation and the activities chosen all matter. Nor is a longer stay automatically affordable or sustainable.

A couple with limited annual leave may get more out of a carefully planned weekend than an unstructured two-week holiday they cannot realistically take. The idea of travelling slowly is best understood as a possible approach rather than a new set of rules. It encourages people to leave room for unexpected pleasures.

Perhaps the important choice is not between doing everything and doing nothing. It is recognising that a successful holiday does not need to be measured by the number of places visited. Sometimes one memorable afternoon can matter more than a long checklist of sights.

Advanced version

The modern holiday is often organised around a fear of missing out. A break can turn into a rush between bookings, directions and photographs. Someone can come home with plenty to show and little sense of having rested.

An alternative is to travel more slowly: choosing fewer destinations, leaving unstructured time and allowing a place to become familiar rather than treating it as a collection of attractions. The appeal is easy to understand. A long lunch, an aimless walk or a morning spent reading beside the sea may provide a sense of freedom that a tightly managed itinerary cannot.

A July 2025 Marist Poll offers useful context. Among Americans planning a summer holiday, 45% identified rest and relaxation as their principal aim, while others prioritised visiting loved ones, adventure or cultural experiences. The survey did not directly measure slow travel, nor should its US results be presented as a global trend. It does, however, show that recovery is a major motivation for taking time away.

There is a subtle difference between travelling slowly and simply staying still. A visitor can explore a place attentively without rushing, while another may shut themselves away indoors for a fortnight and see very little. Slow travel can sometimes encourage more sustained engagement with local life, particularly when visitors move beyond the most photographed landmarks. Yet there is a danger in assuming that lingering somewhere automatically makes tourism more responsible. Transport choices, accommodation, visitor numbers and the needs of residents also matter.

The freedom to travel slowly is unequally distributed. People with limited annual leave or costly long-distance journeys may understandably prefer ambitious itineraries. For someone visiting a country once in a lifetime, seeing several remarkable places is not necessarily a sign of superficial travel. Perhaps the most liberating decision is to leave some hours unclaimed. A recommendation from a local resident or an unexpected change in weather can then become part of the experience.

The economics of leisure also influence how holidays are planned. International travel can require substantial expenditure and limited annual leave, making an ambitious itinerary a rational choice for somebody who may never return to the destination. Remaining in one area might reduce internal transport and encourage spending at local businesses, but a longer trip is not automatically cheaper or environmentally responsible.

Those outcomes depend on travel distances, accommodation and the wider tourism model. Slow travel is therefore most useful as a challenge to the assumption that value is proportional to the number of attractions completed. It invites travellers to consider what they genuinely want from a journey, whether that means time with family, a particular cultural experience or simple recovery from everyday pressures.

Perhaps the real problem is the expectation that holidays must be optimised. Restful and adventurous travel can both be rewarding when they reflect genuine preferences rather than pressure to tick off experiences. The most successful trip may be the one that leaves a person feeling they actually spent time somewhere, rather than merely passed through it.

Source: Marist Poll
