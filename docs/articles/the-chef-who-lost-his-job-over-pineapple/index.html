Davide Tamburella would not put pineapple on a pizza, and it cost him his job. A food historian argues that the tradition he protected keeps moving.

Core version

Pizza brings people together. Then somebody mentions pineapple, and the table divides. Some people love sweet fruit against salty cheese. Others say it has no place on a pizza at all.

An Italian chef called Davide Tamburella felt so strongly about it that it cost him his job. He was working at a restaurant in Brisbane, Australia, and he refused to put pineapple on a pizza. He told his customers and his boss that the combination was an insult to Italian culture. The restaurant sacked him. There is a small joke hidden in the story. Queensland, the state where Brisbane is, grows almost all of Australia's pineapples.

He is not the first cook to lose a job this way. In 2017 a chef from Bologna was sacked by a restaurant near Lyon, in France. He had refused to cook the pasta for longer, and he would not serve spaghetti bolognese, because that dish does not exist in Bologna.

Luca Cesari is a food historian who has written a history of pizza. In an opinion article for The Guardian, he explains why Italians react so strongly. Food fashions are changing quickly, he says, and there are now more chefs on television than grandmothers cooking at home. People can feel that something they love is in danger.

But Cesari also points out a problem. Many of the rules Italians defend are not very old at all. Pasta was eaten soft for hundreds of years. Cream in carbonara was introduced by a few cooks in Rome in the 1960s. The pizza of a hundred years ago was nothing like the pizza we eat today.

Not every Italian is against pineapple either. Gino Sorbillo, a famous pizza maker, allows it on a white pizza with no tomato. Franco Pepe, who many people call the best in the world, invented a pizza cone filled with pineapple, ham and cheese.

So who decides what is traditional? A recipe can connect us to our family and our home. It can also change, as it always has. Perhaps the best table is one where the old version and the new one sit side by side, and the argument is part of the meal.

Intermediate version

It is one of the most reliable ways to start an argument at dinner: ask whether pineapple belongs on a pizza. For some people, sweet fruit and salty cheese are a small, cheerful act of rebellion. For others, the combination ruins the entire evening. One Italian chef felt strongly enough that the disagreement ended his employment.

Davide Tamburella was working at a restaurant in Brisbane, Australia, when he refused to put pineapple on a pizza. He would not give way to his customers or to his boss, insisting that the combination was an insult to Italian culture. The restaurant sacked him. The detail that makes the story funnier is geographical: Queensland grows almost all of Australia's pineapples.

He has company. In 2017 Maurizio Landi, a chef from Bologna, was sacked by a bistro near Lyon after resisting pressure to cook his pasta for longer and to serve spaghetti bolognese. In Bologna, ragù is eaten with tagliatelle and nothing else, so the request was close to an insult.

Writing in The Guardian, the food historian Luca Cesari offers an explanation that is more sympathetic than it first appears. Italians can sense that their cooking is under pressure, he suggests. Food fashions move faster than ever, and there are now more chefs on television than grandmothers cooking at home. Defending a recipe is a way of defending something larger.

The difficulty is that the recipes keep moving too. Cesari notes that several of the rules modern Italians enforce would have puzzled their ancestors. Pasta was eaten soft for centuries, and macaroni with cheese was served beside meat from the Middle Ages onwards. A spoonful of cream in a carbonara is now enough to cause an argument, yet a few Roman cooks introduced it in the 1960s and kept it up for forty years.

Even the pineapple is less settled than it sounds. Gino Sorbillo, one of the most respected pizza makers in the world, has allowed it on a white pizza with no tomato, which would otherwise make it too acidic. Franco Pepe created a filled pizza cone he called the AnaNascosta, the hidden pineapple. Being Italian, it turns out, does not require a single opinion about every topping.

Cesari's list of inconsistencies goes on. Grated cheese on fish offends many Italians, who will nonetheless order the thoroughly traditional mussels with pecorino. A cappuccino after dinner attracts horrified looks, while a caffè macchiato, much the same drink in a smaller cup, passes without any comment at all.

None of that obliges anybody to enjoy it. You can dislike pineapple on pizza for the ordinary reason that you dislike the taste. The interesting question is why a small yellow fruit makes so many of us ready to defend everything standing behind it.

Advanced version

Some disagreements can divide a table before anyone has eaten. Pineapple on pizza is the reigning example. Its defenders enjoy the sweetness against the salt, and a little pleasure in breaking a rule. Its opponents regard the whole arrangement as evidence of a civilisation in decline.

Davide Tamburella went further than an opinion. Working at a restaurant in Brisbane, he refused to put pineapple on a pizza, would not yield to his customers or his employer, and was sacked for it, insisting throughout that the combination insulted Italian culture. Queensland, as it happens, grows almost all of Australia's pineapples, which gives the stand a certain splendid futility.

He joins a small tradition. In 2017 Maurizio Landi, from Bologna, lost a job near Lyon for refusing to overcook pasta and to produce spaghetti bolognese, a dish his home city does not recognise. There, ragù meets tagliatelle and nothing else.

Writing in The Guardian, the food historian Luca Cesari treats this with affection rather than mockery, and then quietly removes the ground from under it. Italians can feel their cuisine slipping, he argues, with food fashions accelerating and more chefs on television than grandmothers at the stove. The instinct to guard it is understandable. The trouble is the thing being guarded.

Cesari's list is quietly devastating. Pasta was eaten soft for centuries. Macaroni with cheese sat beside meat dishes from the Middle Ages. Cream in a carbonara, now close to a criminal offence, was introduced by Roman cooks in the 1960s and persisted for forty years before everyone agreed to forget it. Grated cheese on fish provokes outrage from people who will happily eat the thoroughly traditional mussels with pecorino. A cappuccino after dinner horrifies waiters, while a caffè macchiato, which is much the same drink in miniature, passes without comment.

Nor is the profession united. Gino Sorbillo has permitted pineapple on a white pizza, where no tomato makes it too acidic. Franco Pepe built the AnaNascosta, a filled cone of pineapple, San Daniele ham and Grana Padano. A London chain spent the summer selling a spiced pineapple sauce specifically to annoy the prejudice out of existence. Italian pizzerias, meanwhile, sell frankfurters with chips, speck with mascarpone and tuna with onion, and the traditional pizza of Pesaro arrives as a margherita under hard-boiled eggs and mayonnaise.

The longest view belongs to a newspaper. In December 1860 the Morning Post described Neapolitan pizza and reported that the highest authorities dismissed anchovies, sausage and mushrooms as modern innovations unworthy of the name. The toppings change; the authorities are permanent.

Cesari's conclusion is the sharpest thing in the piece. Italians, he suggests, do not need pineapple pizza in order to eat. They need it in order to have something to despise, because despising it places you on the right side of a line. That is worth holding on to when the next argument starts, because the heat rarely comes from the ingredient. It comes from what we have decided the ingredient stands for.
