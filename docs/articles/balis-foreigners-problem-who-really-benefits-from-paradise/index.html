Core version

On a phone screen, Bali can look like a perfect office: a laptop beside a swimming pool, warm weather and the beach after work. Now imagine seeing the same picture from the other side. New villas and cafés can bring jobs, but they can also change neighbourhoods and make land more expensive. Bali's argument is not simply about whether foreign visitors are welcome. It is about who benefits when a home becomes a global lifestyle.

Bali welcomed almost seven million international visitors in 2025, according to figures reported by Business Insider. Tourism supports hotels, restaurants, guides and thousands of other jobs. Many foreign visitors respect the island's customs and spend money with local businesses. Others, however, have been accused of breaking rules or behaving disrespectfully, especially at sacred sites that matter deeply in Balinese religious life.

One issue is work. Some foreigners enter Indonesia on tourist visas and then carry out activities that require a different permit. In August 2026, Channel NewsAsia reported that Bali's immigration authorities were checking more closely whether foreign social-media creators were making money or promoting businesses without the right permission. Indonesian officials said 342 foreign nationals had been deported from Bali during the first six months of 2026 for various immigration and legal violations. The figure does not mean they were all influencers.

There are also concerns about housing and business. In popular areas, new villas, shops and cafés have changed neighbourhoods and pushed up the value of land. People who earn salaries from other countries may be able to pay far more for accommodation than local workers. Bali has restricted new foreign investment applications in some small business categories, including certain tourism and food businesses, to protect opportunities for local people.

The situation is not as simple as locals against foreigners. Some international residents have lived in Bali for decades, built friendships and created businesses that employ local people. Tourism experts also say videos made by travellers can help small restaurants reach customers around the world. Even content creators who disagree with the current rules often say that people who earn money from local companies should follow Indonesian law. They want clearer information about which activities need permission.

Bali faces a difficult choice. Tourism brings in money and opportunities, but it can also change the places people love. The island is not just a beautiful background for holiday videos. It is home to communities with their own traditions, jobs and everyday problems. Visitors may be welcome, but being welcomed does not mean they can ignore the people who live there.

Intermediate version

On social media, Bali appears almost frictionless: private pools, laptop-friendly cafés and a beach waiting after work. The same success looks more complicated from a neighbourhood where land values are rising and new businesses cater to people earning foreign salaries. Tourism creates jobs and opportunities, but it also changes who can afford to live and trade in popular areas. The real argument is not whether Bali should welcome visitors; it is how the rewards and costs are shared.

Bali's tourism industry remains a powerful source of employment and investment. Nearly seven million international visitors arrived in 2025, according to figures cited by Business Insider. Yet the popularity that supports local businesses can also put pressure on neighbourhoods. In some areas, expensive villas and foreign-run services have multiplied. Some residents worry about rising land values. Others point to competition from people whose income is much higher than local wages. The result is a growing argument about who gains from Bali's success and who pays the price.

The authorities are taking a tougher approach to foreigners who work without the right permits. In August 2026, Channel NewsAsia reported that immigration officers were paying closer attention to influencers and content creators. A tourist who accepts a hotel stay or meals in exchange for promotional posts may in effect be taking on commercial work, even if no cash changes hands. Indonesian officials reported 342 foreign nationals deported during the first half of 2026 for a range of immigration and legal offences. The number should not be mistaken for a count of influencers alone.

The crackdown goes beyond social media. Bali has restricted new foreign investment applications in 18 business categories, including certain cafés and motorcycle-rental businesses. Officials say the aim is to protect opportunities for local entrepreneurs. Supporters argue that outsiders should not be able to use their spending power to take over businesses that local people have traditionally operated. Others warn that unclear or inconsistently enforced regulations could discourage legitimate investment that creates jobs.

There is also a cultural dimension. Bali's Hindu traditions are central to daily life, not simply attractions for visitors to photograph. A small number of widely reported incidents involving disrespectful behaviour at religious sites have caused considerable anger. Nevertheless, it would be unfair to judge every foreign resident by the actions of a few. Many long-term newcomers learn local customs, support community projects and have built their lives around relationships with Balinese neighbours.

What Bali needs may be less a choice between welcoming foreigners and keeping them out than a clearer set of expectations. Visitors should understand the laws and traditions of the place they enter, while officials need rules that distinguish an ordinary travel video from paid commercial activity. The island's future cannot be judged solely by how attractive it looks on Instagram. A successful destination should also be a place where the people who live there can afford a home, run a business and feel respected.

Advanced version

The digital-nomad version of Bali is almost frictionless: a laptop beside an infinity pool, inexpensive services and sunset after work. Turn the camera around and the picture becomes more complicated. The same international demand can raise land values, reshape neighbourhoods and give outsiders spending power unavailable to many residents. Bali's controversy is therefore not best understood as locals against foreigners. The argument is about who gets a say when international demand changes local housing, work and streets.

Bali's extraordinary appeal is not in dispute. The island welcomed nearly seven million international visitors in 2025, according to figures reported by Business Insider, and tourism provides income for countless local families. Foreign creators have helped restaurants and guesthouses reach international customers. Yet an influx of well-paid remote workers and investors can also alter the character of neighbourhoods, raise land values and intensify competition over commercial opportunities. The controversy is therefore not simply about misbehaving holidaymakers. It concerns the distribution of tourism's rewards and the ability of local residents to shape their own communities.

Indonesia's immigration authorities have become increasingly assertive. As Channel NewsAsia reported in August 2026, officials were scrutinising foreigners suspected of conducting commercial activities on visas that do not permit them. Even an arrangement involving a complimentary hotel stay in exchange for promotional posts can raise questions about unauthorised work.

Authorities said 342 foreign nationals were deported from Bali in the first half of the year for various immigration and legal breaches. It would be misleading to present them all as influencers, or to suggest that ordinary tourists are forbidden from posting photographs of their holidays. The unresolved issue is how clearly the boundary between personal expression and commercial activity is defined.

Economic regulation has become another flashpoint. Bali restricted new foreign investment applications in 18 categories of smaller businesses, including certain cafés, vehicle rentals and tourism services. The aim, according to officials and local reporting, is to prevent foreign capital from dominating activities that Indonesian entrepreneurs can provide. Critics contend that the rules risk discouraging legitimate businesses and may be applied inconsistently. Supporters counter that allowing wealthy newcomers to compete on terms unavailable to locals can hollow out an economy even while its headline visitor numbers remain impressive.

Cultural tensions complicate the debate further. Bali's religious traditions are not decorative additions to an international lifestyle destination; they are living practices around which communities organise their lives. High-profile incidents involving foreign visitors who have disregarded local customs have stirred up resentment. But collective blame is a poor substitute for thoughtful policy. Many foreign residents have longstanding relationships with their neighbours, create employment and contribute to Balinese life. Indonesian content creators, meanwhile, have argued that the central issue is fair competition rather than hostility towards outsiders.

For Bali, the challenge is to move beyond the false choice between unrestricted tourism and an island closed to foreigners. Transparent visa rules, fair business enforcement, responsible development and stronger opportunities for local entrepreneurs could preserve the benefits of international interest without treating residents as background scenery. The island has successfully sold the world an image of freedom. The next test is whether that freedom can coexist with the rights, livelihoods and cultural identity of the people who were there long before the hashtags.

9Role-play: a spare room for tourists

Work in pairs. Read your card, then have the conversation. Swap roles and do it again.

Student A · the neighbour who rents out a room

You rent a spare room to tourists and earn extra money. Your neighbour is unhappy about it. Explain why it helps you and what you will do to keep things calm.

Student B · the worried neighbour

Tourists are noisy and rents on your street are rising. Explain your worries, ask questions and try to agree on some rules.

Useful phrases

  • I'd like to talk to you about the guests.
  • It's not easy for me to pay my bills.

  • We're not trying to cause trouble.
  • Would you be willing to set some quiet hours?
  • Let's try it for a month and see.

Afterwards, tell the class: did you agree on any rules, and are they fair to both neighbours?
