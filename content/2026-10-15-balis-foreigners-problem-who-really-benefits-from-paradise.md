=== Core ===

title: Bali's Visitors: When Does a Holiday Become a Problem?
summary: Bali is popular with tourists and remote workers. Some local people say it is changing too fast. What is the right balance?

For many people, Bali seems like the perfect place to live. It has beaches, warm weather, cafés and places where people can work online. Photos of laptops beside swimming pools have helped turn the Indonesian island into a favourite destination for tourists and remote workers. But some local people are becoming worried about the way parts of Bali are changing.

Bali welcomed almost seven million international visitors in 2025, according to figures reported by Business Insider. Tourism supports hotels, restaurants, guides and thousands of other jobs. Many foreign visitors respect the island's customs and spend money with local businesses. Others, however, have been accused of breaking rules or behaving disrespectfully, especially in places that are important to Balinese religious life.

One issue is work. Some foreigners enter Indonesia on tourist visas and then carry out activities that require a different permit. In August 2026, Channel NewsAsia reported that Bali's immigration authorities were checking more closely whether foreign social-media creators were making money or promoting businesses without the right permission. Indonesian officials said 342 foreign nationals had been deported from Bali during the first six months of 2026 for various immigration and legal violations. The figure does not mean they were all influencers.

There are also concerns about housing and business. In popular areas, new villas, shops and cafés have changed neighbourhoods and increased the value of land. People who earn salaries from other countries may be able to pay far more for accommodation than local workers. Bali has restricted new foreign investment applications in some small business categories, including certain tourism and food businesses, to protect opportunities for local people.

The situation is not as simple as locals against foreigners. Some international residents have lived in Bali for decades, built friendships and created businesses that employ local people. Tourism experts also say videos made by travellers can help small restaurants reach customers around the world. Even content creators who disagree with the current rules often say that people who earn money from local companies should follow Indonesian law. They want clearer information about which activities need permission.

Bali faces a difficult choice. Tourism brings money and opportunities, but it can also change the places people love. The island is not just a beautiful background for holiday videos. It is home to communities with their own traditions, jobs and everyday problems. Visitors may be welcome, but being welcomed does not mean they can ignore the people who live there.

=== Intermediate ===

Scroll through social media and Bali can look like a place where ordinary rules no longer apply. There are villas with private pools, cafés designed for laptop workers and beaches that seem made for an endless holiday. For digital nomads earning money abroad, the Indonesian island offers an attractive lifestyle. But the picture looks rather different to some of the people who have lived there all their lives.

Bali's tourism industry remains a powerful source of employment and investment. Nearly seven million international visitors arrived in 2025, according to figures cited by Business Insider. Yet the popularity that supports local businesses can also put pressure on neighbourhoods. In areas where expensive villas and foreign-run services have multiplied, residents worry about rising land values and competition with people whose income is much higher than local wages. The result is a growing argument about who gains from Bali's success and who pays the price.

The authorities are taking a tougher approach to foreigners who work without the right permits. In August 2026, Channel NewsAsia reported that immigration officers were paying closer attention to influencers and content creators. A tourist who accepts a hotel stay or meals in exchange for promotional posts may be carrying out commercial work, even if no cash changes hands. Indonesian officials reported 342 foreign nationals deported during the first half of 2026 for a range of immigration and legal offences. The number should not be mistaken for a count of influencers alone.

The crackdown goes beyond social media. Bali has restricted new foreign investment applications in 18 business categories, including certain cafés and motorcycle-rental businesses. Officials say the aim is to protect opportunities for local entrepreneurs. Supporters argue that outsiders should not be able to use their spending power to take over businesses that local people have traditionally operated. Others warn that unclear or inconsistently enforced regulations could discourage legitimate investment that creates jobs.

There is also a cultural dimension. Bali's Hindu traditions are central to daily life, not simply attractions for visitors to photograph. A small number of widely reported incidents involving disrespectful behaviour at religious sites have caused considerable anger. Nevertheless, it would be unfair to judge every foreign resident by the actions of a few. Many long-term newcomers learn local customs, support community projects and have built their lives around relationships with Balinese neighbours.

What Bali needs may be less a choice between welcoming foreigners and keeping them out than a clearer set of expectations. Visitors should understand the laws and traditions of the place they enter, while officials need rules that distinguish an ordinary travel video from paid commercial activity. The island's future cannot be judged solely by how attractive it looks on Instagram. A successful destination should also be a place where the people who live there can afford a home, run a business and feel respected.

=== Advanced ===

title: Paradise, Profit and Pushback: Bali's Uneasy Relationship with Foreign Residents
summary: Remote workers, influencers and investors are reshaping Bali. As the authorities tighten the rules, who gets to shape the island's future?

In the carefully edited world of the digital nomad, Bali is an almost frictionless paradise. A laptop rests beside an infinity pool, the weather is reliably warm and a day's work can be followed by sunset on the beach. For a growing number of foreigners, this image has become the promise of an enviable lifestyle. For some Balinese residents, however, it raises a less photogenic question: what happens when a place increasingly caters to people with far greater spending power than those who call it home?

Bali's extraordinary appeal is not in dispute. The island welcomed nearly seven million international visitors in 2025, according to figures reported by Business Insider, and tourism provides income for countless local families. Foreign creators have helped restaurants and guesthouses reach international customers. Yet an influx of well-paid remote workers and investors can also alter the character of neighbourhoods, raise land values and intensify competition over commercial opportunities. The controversy is therefore not simply about misbehaving holidaymakers. It concerns the distribution of tourism's rewards and the ability of local residents to shape their own communities.

Indonesia's immigration authorities have become increasingly assertive. As Channel NewsAsia reported in August 2026, officials were scrutinising foreigners suspected of conducting commercial activities on visas that do not permit them. Even an arrangement involving a complimentary hotel stay in exchange for promotional posts can raise questions about unauthorised work. Authorities said 342 foreign nationals were deported from Bali in the first half of the year for various immigration and legal breaches. It would be misleading to present them all as influencers, or to suggest that ordinary tourists are forbidden from posting photographs of their holidays. The unresolved issue is how clearly the boundary between personal expression and commercial activity is defined.

Economic regulation has become another flashpoint. Bali restricted new foreign investment applications in 18 categories of smaller businesses, including certain cafés, vehicle rentals and tourism services. The aim, according to officials and local reporting, is to prevent foreign capital from dominating activities that Indonesian entrepreneurs can provide. Critics contend that the rules risk discouraging legitimate businesses and may be applied inconsistently. Supporters counter that allowing wealthy newcomers to compete on terms unavailable to locals can hollow out an economy even while its headline visitor numbers remain impressive.

Cultural tensions complicate the debate further. Bali's religious traditions are not decorative additions to an international lifestyle destination; they are living practices around which communities organise their lives. High-profile incidents involving foreign visitors who have disregarded local customs have sharpened resentment. But collective blame is a poor substitute for thoughtful policy. Many foreign residents have longstanding relationships with their neighbours, create employment and contribute to Balinese life. Indonesian content creators, meanwhile, have argued that the central issue is fair competition rather than hostility towards outsiders.

For Bali, the challenge is to move beyond the false choice between unrestricted tourism and an island closed to foreigners. Transparent visa rules, fair business enforcement, responsible development and stronger opportunities for local entrepreneurs could preserve the benefits of international interest without treating residents as background scenery. The island has successfully sold the world an image of freedom. The next test is whether that freedom can coexist with the rights, livelihoods and cultural identity of the people who were there long before the hashtags.
