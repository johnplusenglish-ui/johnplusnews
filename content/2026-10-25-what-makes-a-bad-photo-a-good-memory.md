title: Does a Good Photo Have to Be Perfect?
summary: A BBC reporter tried an old digital camera at a DJ set. What can a messy photo show that a perfect one can't?

Imagine two photos of the same birthday party. In the first, everyone looks at the camera, the light is perfect and every face is clear. In the second, the picture is a little blurry. Your friend is laughing so hard that her eyes are closed, and someone's hand is in front of the lens. Which photo would you keep?

In August 2026, the BBC reporter Annabel Rackham tried something different. She took a digital camera to a DJ set with her best friend. It was the camera she last used 15 years ago, on a family holiday. She nearly forgot the battery. People looked at it and said they hadn't seen one for years.

Rackham says that older technology is "having a moment". On social media, some people are going back to digital cameras, wired headphones and old music players.

Lily Redman, a 26-year-old content creator from South Wales, says her digital camera gives a look her phone can't copy. She thinks phone flash can look grainy and too sharp. She says people want something that feels "a bit more real".

Redman also likes the surprise. With a digital camera, you see the photos later, when you put the memory card into a computer. You don't know exactly what you got. On a phone, you can check at once and try again.

Remember who is in this story. The people are adults in their twenties and thirties, not school students. It's one article with a few voices, so it doesn't prove that most young people prefer old cameras. It's also about digital cameras, not film cameras, and it doesn't say that an old camera makes anyone happier.

So back to the two photos. A perfect picture can show how a party looked. A blurry one might show how it felt. If you could keep only one photo from your last trip, which would it be, and why?
