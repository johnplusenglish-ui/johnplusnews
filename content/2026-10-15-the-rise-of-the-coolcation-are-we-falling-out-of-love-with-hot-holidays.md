title: Coolcations: Why More People Want a Holiday Without the Heat
summary: Some travellers now want cool weather instead of hot sunshine. This is called a coolcation. Is it a real change in how we travel?

For years, the perfect summer holiday meant blue skies, a hot beach and plenty of sunshine. Now some travellers are looking for something different. They want cool air, a good night's sleep and weather that makes it possible to go outside all day. This is a "coolcation": travelling north to escape the heat instead of flying south to find sunshine.

The change became particularly noticeable during the European summer of 2026. Parts of southern Europe faced long periods of very hot weather, and some visitors found that sightseeing in high temperatures was exhausting. A holiday in a beautiful city is less enjoyable when you have to spend the hottest hours indoors. Europe's Copernicus climate service reported that summer 2026 was the continent's third warmest on record.

Scotland is one place benefiting from the new interest in cooler holidays. In August, the BBC met holiday-cottage owners near Glencoe who said visitors were choosing the area partly because of its weather. Some guests were even pleased when it rained. They could walk, kayak or sleep comfortably without needing air conditioning. Sweden, Norway, Finland and Iceland have also attracted travellers looking for milder summer temperatures and beautiful natural scenery.

The travel industry has noticed. Research reported by Euronews in July found growing interest in northern destinations. Norway's tourism organisation said about one in five foreign visitors chose the country partly for its cooler weather. But experts warn that it is too early to say everyone is giving up Mediterranean holidays. Spain, Italy and Greece remain extremely popular, and people choose places for many reasons, including price, food, culture and beaches.

Cooler destinations also have their own problems. Norway's Lofoten Islands, for example, have experienced crowded roads, rubbish and pressure on local communities as tourism has grown. A peaceful-looking destination can become crowded when everybody has the same idea. And northern countries are not protected from climate change. Scotland experienced unusual heat and wildfires during the same summer that visitors were travelling there to cool down.

Perhaps the biggest change will be **when** people travel, rather than **where**. A family might still choose Greece, but visit in May or October instead of August. Others might spend summer in the mountains or by a northern lake. Sunshine will always be part of the holiday dream for many people. But as summers grow hotter, a little rain and a comfortable night's sleep are starting to sound surprisingly attractive.

=== Intermediate ===

For generations, British holidaymakers have packed their bags and headed south in search of guaranteed sunshine. A week of cloudless skies was considered a success, while rain could ruin the mood before breakfast. Yet after another summer of intense European heat, a growing number of travellers are beginning to see the appeal of a very different forecast. Their ideal holiday involves cool evenings, fresh air and the freedom to spend the afternoon outdoors without hunting for shade.

The trend has acquired an awkward but memorable name: the "coolcation". Instead of the traditional Mediterranean escape, travellers choose places such as Scotland, Norway or southern Sweden, where summer temperatures are generally more comfortable. There are obvious attractions beyond the weather. Norwegian fjords, Scottish lochs and long Nordic evenings offer a different kind of holiday, with walking, kayaking and exploring taking the place of sunbathing. For visitors tired of sweltering city streets, the change can feel refreshing in more ways than one.

The weather has helped push this idea into the mainstream. Europe's Copernicus climate service reported that summer 2026 was the continent's third warmest on record, with western Europe recording its hottest summer. In August, the BBC visited a holiday business near Glencoe whose owners said customers were increasingly choosing Scotland because of its cooler climate. Some guests welcomed the rain rather than complaining about it. The kind of weather tourism advertisements once tried to play down had become a selling point.

There are signs that this goes beyond a few unusual travellers. Euronews reported growing interest in Scandinavian and northern European destinations, while Norway's tourism organisation said roughly one in five overseas visitors regarded cooler summer temperatures as a reason for going there. However, tourism researchers are cautious about declaring the end of the beach holiday. Southern Europe is still enormously popular, and climate is only one factor alongside cost, transport links, food and personal preference. An internet search does not always turn into a booking.

Nor is heading north a perfect solution. In Norway's Lofoten Islands, growing visitor numbers have put pressure on roads, parking and local facilities. Moving crowds from one part of Europe to another could simply create new problems. Northern destinations are also experiencing climate change themselves. Scotland suffered a major wildfire in the Cairngorms during the summer, a reminder that cooler does not mean risk-free. And taking a longer flight to escape the heat can add to the emissions that contribute to global warming.

A more lasting change may be a shift in the holiday calendar. People who love Spain or Italy might still visit, but choose spring or autumn instead of the height of summer. Others may discover that their happiest holiday involves a mountain path rather than a sunlounger. The appetite for sunshine has hardly disappeared. But when a holiday starts to feel like an endurance test, it's understandable that a grey sky can suddenly look rather inviting.

=== Advanced ===

title: The Coolcation Paradox: When Bad Weather Becomes a Holiday Selling Point
summary: As summers grow hotter, cooler destinations are gaining admirers. But moving the crowds north may simply move the problems too.

The traditional European summer holiday was built on a straightforward promise: escape the drizzle, head south and return home with a suntan. For decades, destinations competed to advertise ever more reliable sunshine. Now, after a succession of punishing summers, some travellers are choosing their holidays on almost the opposite principle. They want lower temperatures, breathable afternoons and the unremarkable luxury of sleeping comfortably. Even Scottish rain, once an occupational hazard for the tourism industry, has taken on a certain commercial appeal.

The term "coolcation" may sound like an invention from a marketing meeting, but the pressures behind it are real. Europe's Copernicus climate service reported that summer 2026 was the continent's third warmest on record, while western Europe endured its hottest. By the end of the season, 52% of Europe had experienced at least very strong heat stress, a measure of how difficult and potentially dangerous outdoor conditions can become. That helps explain why some visitors are reconsidering an August city break in the Mediterranean.

In Scotland, businesses are already turning their traditionally unpredictable weather into an asset. BBC reporting from Glencoe in August described holidaymakers seeking relief from heat further south and cottage owners surprised to find that customers actually welcomed a downpour. Farther east, travellers have been exploring Sweden's coast and Norway's fjords for similar reasons. Euronews, reporting with AFP, cited tourism research showing growing interest in northern regions and noted that roughly one in five international visitors to Norway considered cooler summer weather among their reasons for travelling there. The appeal is also being able to explore without organising every hour around the temperature.

Nevertheless, the evidence calls for a measured interpretation. A spike in online searches may reflect curiosity rather than a lasting change in behaviour, and Europe's Mediterranean destinations continue to draw enormous crowds. Decisions depend on affordability, food, culture and flight connections, not the thermometer alone. Scandinavia can also be expensive, while a rainy week in the Highlands is not everyone's idea of relaxation. Rather than abandoning southern Europe, travellers may increasingly visit it in spring or autumn, leaving the hottest months for mountain regions or northern coasts.

There is an uncomfortable irony in the enthusiasm for cooler destinations. In parts of Norway's Lofoten archipelago, rising tourist numbers have already brought about congested roads, overflowing parking areas and arguments about who should pay for local infrastructure. Tourism pressure does not become sustainable merely because it moves north. Cooler regions are not insulated from the climate crisis: Scotland experienced a substantial Cairngorms wildfire that summer. Long-distance flights, meanwhile, remain part of tourism's environmental footprint, whatever the weather at the destination.

The more consequential shift may therefore concern how holidays are organised, rather than the geographical direction of travel. If tourists spread their trips across different months and destinations, some overcrowded regions could gain breathing space while less-visited communities benefit economically. But that requires planning, infrastructure and an honest account of environmental costs, not simply a new label attached to another fashionable escape. The coolcation captures a changing mood in European travel: sunshine is still desirable, but having enough of it may no longer be the same as having a good holiday.
