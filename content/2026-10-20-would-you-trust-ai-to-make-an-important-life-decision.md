=== Core ===

You have two job offers and cannot decide which one to accept. One pays more, but the other sounds more interesting. You ask an AI chatbot for help. It quickly gives you a list of advantages and disadvantages. Should you follow its advice?

AI tools can explain difficult information, compare options and help us think of questions. But giving information is different from making a decision for someone. A computer may not understand your family, personality or feelings as well as a close friend does.

In a 2025 survey, Pew Research Center asked Americans about different uses of AI. Many people supported using it for tasks such as weather forecasting and medical research. They were much less comfortable with AI taking a role in very personal decisions, including deciding whether two people should be together.

There are practical risks too. AI can make mistakes or sound confident when its information is wrong. If someone asks about a serious health problem or an important financial choice, acting on an incorrect answer could cause harm.

This does not mean we should avoid AI completely. It might help us prepare for a conversation with a doctor, compare questions to ask an employer or understand the meaning of a document. We can use its ideas and still check important facts with qualified people.

The way we ask AI questions can make a big difference. Suppose you are thinking of moving to another country. Instead of asking, 'Should I move?', you could ask, 'What costs should I think about before moving?' You could then check the answers on official websites and talk to people who have made a similar move. This keeps the tool in a helpful role. The same idea works for money and health questions: ask for explanations or a list of questions to take to a professional, not an answer you must follow. We should also remember that chatbots may not know when our information is incomplete. A friend might stop and ask why we want to make a change. A chatbot may give a detailed answer before it understands the whole situation.

Perhaps the best role for AI is not to tell us what to do. It is to help us understand our choices more clearly. The final responsibility still belongs to us, especially when the decision could change our lives.

=== Intermediate ===

Imagine asking an AI chatbot whether to move abroad, end a relationship or accept a new job. Within seconds it produces a convincing answer, carefully organised into advantages and disadvantages. The advice may be useful, but how much authority should we give it when the consequences are personal?

Pew Research Center explored public attitudes in a September 2025 survey of more than 5,000 US adults. Respondents were more willing to accept AI involvement in analysing complex information, such as forecasting weather or developing medicines, than in intimate areas of life. A clear majority opposed AI playing a role in judging whether two people could fall in love.

The distinction makes sense. AI can process large quantities of text and identify patterns, but it does not have human experience or direct knowledge of someone's circumstances. It may also provide inaccurate information in an extremely confident tone. An answer that sounds compassionate is not necessarily reliable.

Nevertheless, avoiding AI altogether would mean losing some genuinely useful support. Someone facing a difficult career decision could ask a chatbot to list considerations they had overlooked. A patient could use it to understand medical terminology before speaking to a doctor. In both cases, the tool can improve preparation without replacing professional judgement.

There are questions about privacy as well. Sharing intimate relationship problems, financial details or health information with a digital service may expose sensitive material. Users should understand how their data may be handled before treating a chatbot like a confidential adviser.

One way to reduce the risks is to improve the questions we ask. A person considering a move abroad could request a checklist of financial, legal and practical issues rather than asking a chatbot to decide whether they should leave. Someone facing a complicated medical appointment could ask for an explanation of unfamiliar terms and then confirm it with their clinician. These uses treat AI as a tool for preparing decisions, not as a substitute for the people responsible for them. Users should also check official sources, question unsupported claims and avoid supplying unnecessary personal information. A fluent answer can hide important gaps: the chatbot may not know local regulations, family circumstances or recent changes. The ability to challenge its output is therefore part of using it intelligently.

The central issue is not whether AI is intelligent enough to have an opinion. It is whether we understand its limitations. A sensible approach may be to ask AI better questions rather than allow it to make our decisions for us.

=== Advanced ===

A persuasive answer can be mistaken for a trustworthy one, particularly when it arrives instantly and is expressed with apparent confidence. This is one reason the growing use of AI for personal advice deserves scrutiny. The technology can organise complicated information with remarkable speed, but the ability to produce a convincing recommendation is not the same as possessing sound judgement.

A September 2025 Pew Research Center survey of 5,023 US adults revealed an important distinction in public attitudes. Respondents were generally more receptive to AI being used for analytical tasks, including weather forecasting and scientific research, than for intimate personal matters. Roughly two-thirds believed AI should have no role in judging whether two people might fall in love. Those findings describe American opinion, not a universal rule about what AI can achieve.

Practical value and decision-making authority should be separated. AI might help a prospective employee identify questions about a contract, or help a patient understand unfamiliar medical language. Such uses are different from trusting a chatbot to recommend a particular treatment, relationship decision or risky investment without qualified human oversight.

The technology can be inaccurate, may lack crucial context and sometimes presents uncertainty poorly. An additional concern is privacy: people may disclose information to a chatbot that they would hesitate to share with almost anybody else. The convenience of an apparently attentive conversational partner can obscure how the service handles sensitive data.

There is a counterargument. Human advisers can be biased, impatient, expensive or unavailable. For people with limited access to expertise, a digital tool that helps them explore possibilities may be valuable. The solution is therefore unlikely to be a blanket rejection of AI assistance.

Effective use of AI depends partly on the distinction between generating options and selecting among them. A chatbot might identify practical considerations for a career change, explain legal terminology or help a patient prepare questions for a consultation. These tasks can improve the quality of subsequent decisions without requiring the system to make them. The greater danger arises when its polished presentation conceals missing context, uncertain evidence or errors in factual assumptions. People may mistake a coherent argument for one grounded in their specific circumstances. A robust approach therefore combines independent verification, appropriate expert advice and careful handling of private information. It also requires resisting the temptation to regard apparent empathy as evidence that the system understands personal consequences in the way a human adviser might.

What matters is maintaining a distinction between support and delegation. A useful system should make its limitations visible, encourage verification and strengthen the user's judgement. The more consequential the decision, the greater the need for reliable evidence, relevant professionals and personal responsibility. Outsourcing thinking is convenient; outsourcing accountability is considerably more dangerous.
