title: A Dance Where Nobody Is Watching You
summary: At a pub in south London, young and old are learning the same dances together. You do not need a partner, or any skill at all.

A ceilidh is a traditional dance from Scotland and Ireland. The word comes from Gaelic and means something like a visit or a gathering, and it is said “KAY-lee”. A band plays, someone explains the steps, and everybody dances in lines and circles. You do not need a partner. You do not need to be good.

Last Friday, a ceilidh filled the Ivy House pub in Nunhead, in south-east London. The pub is better known for rock and indie music, but that night the dancefloor belonged to a folk band and a crowd of all ages. Many of the dancers were young, and many of them had never been to a ceilidh before.

The numbers suggest this is not only happening in one pub. According to the ticket platform Eventbrite, ticket sales for ceilidh events in the UK grew by 16% from 2024 to 2025. Across the world, they grew by 15%. A spokesperson for the platform said young people are looking for events that are “communal and a lot less polished”.

The dancers gave simple reasons. “We spend a lot of time looking at screens,” said Matt, who liked the chance to spend an evening offline. George, 35, was at his first proper ceilidh. “I just like how silly it can be,” he said. “It just makes you feel alive.” His partner Louise, 32, liked that nobody was judging how well anyone danced. Jamie, 19, has been to many and says they are becoming popular with his friends.

Part of the appeal seems to be that you cannot look cool while doing it. You get hot, you get the steps wrong and you laugh at the same moments as everyone else. Ed McCabe, who founded the London Ceilidh Club, said the scene has boomed since Covid. In his words, people simply want to be in a room together.

It is not perfect for everyone. The music is loud, the room is crowded and some people would rather not hold hands with a stranger. But for a first-timer who arrives alone, that may be the point. Within one dance, you have already met six people.

The Ivy House in Nunhead, south-east London, is a pub with a loud musical history. Ian Dury and Elvis Costello played there in the 1970s, and contemporary indie bands still pull in crowds. Last Friday, though, the thing filling the dancefloor was not a gig. It was a ceilidh: a traditional Scottish and Irish dance, pronounced “KAY-lee”, from a Gaelic word meaning a visit or a gathering.

Reporters and organisers have started calling it a ceilidh renaissance, and there are figures behind the phrase. The ticketing platform Eventbrite says sales for ceilidh events in the UK rose 16% between 2024 and 2025, with 15% growth worldwide. Page Dudley, a spokesperson for the platform, said young people were seeking events that felt “communal and a lot less polished”. A touring show, Ceilidh the Musical, opened at Shoreditch town hall in east London last week.

What draws people is easier to hear from the dancers than from the statistics. “We spend a lot of time looking at screens,” said Matt, who treated the evening as a refreshing way to be offline. George, 35, at his first proper ceilidh, said he liked how silly it could be: “It just makes you feel alive.” His partner Louise, 32, valued the absence of judgment. “When you strip away the ego, everyone just becomes their childlike selves,” she said. Jamie, 19, has been to plenty, and says the surprise is how often he now meets schoolfriends at them.

The format does a lot of the work. A caller explains each dance, the steps are simple enough to copy and complicated enough to go wrong, and partners change constantly, so nobody is left standing at the side. Ed McCabe, founder of the London Ceilidh Club, says the scene has boomed since Covid, and that ceilidhs have low barriers to entry. His point is partly about dignity: you cannot look cool while doing it, which removes a great deal of pressure.

The musicians make a related point about who feels welcome. Richard Jones, who plays accordion in the South East London Folk Orchestra, notes that the dances are not organised around men and women. His bandmate Neil McLean put it more plainly: “We don't really do men and women, we do ones and twos.” Jones adds that people know they can come without a group, and that the room becomes its own community very quickly.

That may explain why an anti-loneliness charity was involved. Link Age Southwark raised money from ticket sales at the Ivy House, and Anna Spree, who works there, said the ceilidh revival had allowed the charity to reach new people. An evening out that happens to introduce you to thirty strangers is an unusual kind of fundraiser.

=== Advanced ===

title: What a Crowded Dancefloor Offers That an App Cannot
summary: The ceilidh revival is being read as a verdict on dating apps and screens. The more interesting explanation is in the design of the dance itself.

A ceilidh is not an obvious candidate for a youth trend. The music is acoustic, the dances are centuries old, the instructions are shouted by someone with a microphone, and at no point does anybody look good. Yet last Friday the Ivy House in Nunhead, a south London pub with a history of raucous gigs, was full of people in their twenties and thirties learning to strip the willow.

The commentary around this tends to reach immediately for a diagnosis: a lonely generation, exhausted by dating apps, seeking something analogue. There is evidence that something is happening. Eventbrite reports that UK ticket sales for ceilidh events grew 16% between 2024 and 2025, and 15% globally, and a spokesperson describes demand for occasions that are communal and less polished. A touring musical has arrived to meet the moment.

The explanation worth taking seriously, though, is structural rather than generational. A ceilidh is unusually well designed for strangers. A caller teaches each dance in advance, which means nobody is expected to arrive competent. Partners rotate by the logic of the figures, so joining alone carries no social cost and standing at the edge is not really an option. The steps are, as one charity worker put it, accessible enough that anyone can join in but complex enough to be fun, which is a precise description of a good game.

The dancers' own accounts point the same way. George, 35, said he liked how silly it was and that it made him feel alive. Louise, 32, valued that nobody was assessing anyone's ability: strip away the ego, she said, and people become their childlike selves. Ed McCabe of the London Ceilidh Club argues that the inability to look cool is a feature, since you are sweaty and smiling and therefore unguarded. Musicians in the band note that the dances assign roles as ones and twos rather than men and women, which quietly removes a set of assumptions nobody has to discuss.

Put together, these are not nostalgic virtues. They are the things most social occasions are bad at. A party requires you to already know someone. A dating app requires you to perform a version of yourself and then meet the consequences alone. A ceilidh gives you a structure, a task, a reason to touch hands with a stranger and an excuse to laugh when it goes wrong, and it ends at a fixed time.

Some caution is warranted before declaring a movement. Growth figures from one ticketing platform describe a market, not a culture, and a crowded pub in Nunhead is one evening in one city. Scottish and Irish communities have been holding ceilidhs continuously for well over a century, so the renaissance belongs to the people discovering them, not to the form.

Still, the appeal is not mysterious. A local anti-loneliness charity took the ticket money and said the revival had helped it reach new people. Whatever else is going on, a room where nobody can look cool turns out to be an efficient way to meet somebody.
