title: Could You Put Your Phone Away for Dinner?
summary: Some restaurants in Sardinia are asking families to put their screens away. Instead, they want parents and children to talk and play together.

You are at a restaurant with your family. The food has not arrived yet, and everyone is looking at a phone. Nobody is arguing, but nobody is talking either. Does that sound peaceful, or a little sad? A group of restaurants in Sardinia, an Italian island, wants families to try something different.

Fifteen restaurants have agreed to take part in a one-month trial called “At the table without screens”. Families eating at those restaurants will be asked to put their devices away. The idea is to give parents and children more time to talk during their meal. The restaurants chose to join the scheme, so it is not a rule for every restaurant on the island.

After families hand in their devices, staff will offer them a “reunion kit”. It contains games and drawings designed to help children and parents spend time together. The games have time limits, so they fit around a restaurant meal. Families can also use the time to share stories about their day.

The kit also holds a short leaflet for parents. It explains the risks of too much time online and gives advice about social media at home.

Anna Cau, Sardinia's children's ombudsman, is leading the project. An ombudsman is an official whose job is to protect people's rights. She wants public places to help families connect, rather than leaving parents to deal with screens alone. A local restaurant association is supporting the trial too. Its island president said that lively chatter is often replaced by silence, and that children are sometimes handed a device to keep them quiet.

Of course, a phone can be useful at a restaurant. A parent might be waiting for an important message, and a game can help a tired child wait for food. People may also disagree about whether a restaurant should make rules for a family's meal. A quiet table does not always mean an unhappy one.

Still, the idea raises an interesting question. If the screens disappeared for an hour, what would happen next? Some families might enjoy the break. Others might discover that starting a conversation takes a little more effort than picking up a phone. Either way, the trial invites them to find out.

summary: Fifteen Sardinian restaurants are trying a new approach to family meals: fewer screens, more games and, they hope, more conversation.

Look around a restaurant while people are waiting for their food and you may notice a familiar scene: a family sitting together, each person absorbed in a separate screen. It can be a moment of welcome peace, particularly for tired parents. But a group of restaurants in Sardinia is asking whether those quiet minutes could be used differently.

Fifteen establishments on the Italian island have volunteered for a one-month pilot called “At the table without screens”. During the trial, parents and children dining at participating restaurants will be asked to put their devices away. The aim is not simply to remove phones, but to make room for the kind of conversation that can disappear when everybody is scrolling.

Families will be offered a “reunion kit” containing games and drawings. Designed with the Italian federation of paediatricians, the activities are intended to help children and parents interact, perhaps by sharing stories about their day. The kit also includes a leaflet about the risks of too much screen time and tips for managing social media at home.

The initiative is being led by Anna Cau, Sardinia's children's ombudsman and a former juvenile magistrate, with support from a local catering and tourism association. Cau argues that the burden should not fall entirely on parents, and that public settings ought to support them. The association's island president, Emanuele Frongia, said the fifteen restaurants had readily agreed to take part. He has noticed lively chatter giving way to stretches of silence as diners look at their devices, and said children are often handed one to keep them quiet.

Cau, who worked on the scheme with the Sardinian wing of the catering association Fipe-Confcommercio, describes it as a small step in a wider campaign to reduce excessive screen use among children. The games, she said, are deliberately given time limits so that they fit the length of a lunch or dinner.

It is easy to understand the appeal of the idea. A meal out can bring people together without anyone having to cook or wash up. Removing the usual distractions might make it easier to notice what someone else has to say. A game could also help families who find that the question “How was your day?” gets the same answer every evening.

Yet phones are not used only to escape conversation. They can help parents manage long waits, keep in touch with someone at home or deal with an unexpected problem. Some diners may feel that putting a screen away should be a personal choice rather than part of the restaurant's rules. Others may welcome having that decision made for them.

For now, the scheme is a limited experiment, not a ban across Sardinia, and the organisers plan to expand it only on a voluntary basis. The question for diners is simple: if everyone put their phone away, would dinner feel more enjoyable, or just a little longer?

=== Advanced ===

title: Would a Phone-Free Dinner Bring Families Closer?
summary: A group of Sardinian restaurants is trying to bring conversation back to family meals. But when does a helpful nudge become an unwelcome rule?

There is a particular kind of silence that can settle over a restaurant table before the food arrives. Nobody is upset. Nobody has necessarily run out of things to say. They are simply elsewhere: checking a message, watching a video or giving a child a screen to make the wait easier. In Sardinia, fifteen restaurants have volunteered to see what happens when that familiar option is removed.

The one-month pilot, called “At the table without screens”, asks parents and children eating at participating establishments to put their devices away. In exchange, families receive a “reunion kit” of games and drawings, designed with the Italian federation of paediatricians to encourage conversation. The activities have time limits so they can sit comfortably within a lunch or dinner, rather than turning the whole meal into an organised event.

Anna Cau, the island's children's ombudsman and a former juvenile magistrate, is leading the initiative with support from a local hospitality association. Her argument is that this should not be treated as a task for parents alone: the broader environment can help create the conditions for children to interact with others. Emanuele Frongia, who represents the association in Sardinia, says restaurants increasingly see lively chatter replaced by diners absorbed in their devices.

It is an appealing idea, partly because it addresses a problem many people recognise without requiring an elaborate solution. A shared meal can be one of the few times a family stops moving in different directions. A game might ease the awkwardness of beginning a conversation, especially if asking about school or work tends to produce a one-word answer. The restaurant provides both the opportunity and a reason to take it.

It is also part of something larger. Cau frames the pilot as one step in a campaign against excessive screen use among children, and Frongia says the plan is to extend it to other restaurants, voluntarily, if the month goes well.

The case for phones is less romantic, but it is practical. A parent may be expecting an important call. A child may struggle with a long, noisy wait. A family might be talking to someone who cannot join them in person. And a silence at the table is not automatically a failure: people can enjoy each other's company without filling every minute with words.

The trial also raises a question about choice. Restaurants already set rules about reservations, menus and behaviour, so a screen-free table is not an entirely new kind of condition. Even so, some customers may welcome a reason to put their phones away while others resent being told how to spend time with their own families. That the restaurants volunteered does not mean every diner will feel the same way.

The scheme was announced before the pilot began, so nothing is yet known about whether it improves anything. Its value lies in the experiment itself. For an hour, a device that usually solves boredom is out of reach. What replaces it, whether a game, an argument, a conversation or comfortable silence, will depend on the people at the table.
