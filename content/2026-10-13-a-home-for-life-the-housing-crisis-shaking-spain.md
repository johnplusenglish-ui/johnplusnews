=== Intermediate ===

For more than 70 years, Maricarmen Abascal lived in the same flat in Madrid. It was the place she called home, full of memories from almost her entire life. But in September 2026, at the age of 87, she was forced to leave.

Her story has shocked Spain and brought thousands of people onto the streets to protest against rising rents and the difficulty of finding an affordable home.

Maricarmen had lived in her flat in Madrid's Retiro neighbourhood since she was a teenager. However, after a property company bought the building, a long legal battle began over her right to remain there.

The company wanted to end her old rental agreement, which had allowed her to pay a relatively low rent. Maricarmen fought the decision for years, supported by neighbours and housing campaigners.

On 23 September, police arrived to carry out the eviction. Outside, hundreds of people gathered to support her. Despite their protests, Maricarmen was taken from her home on a stretcher and transported to hospital.

Two weeks later, on 7 October, she died at the age of 87. Her health had already been poor, and the stress of the long legal battle had reportedly affected her badly. Although an agreement had been reached that might have allowed her to return home, she never made it back.

Her death led to even larger demonstrations. Thousands marched through Madrid carrying signs and demanding stronger protection for people at risk of losing their homes.

But Maricarmen's story is part of a much bigger problem.

Across Spain, house prices and rents have increased sharply in recent years, particularly in cities such as Madrid, Barcelona and Málaga. Meanwhile, many salaries have failed to keep up with the rising cost of living.

Young adults are among those most affected. Even people with full-time jobs often struggle to afford a flat of their own, forcing them to share accommodation or continue living with their parents.

Tourism is another part of the debate. In popular destinations, some landlords prefer renting properties to visitors for short stays because they can earn more money. Critics argue that this reduces the number of homes available for local residents.

Property investors have also come under pressure. Housing campaigners believe that large companies buying residential buildings can push up rents and make life more difficult for ordinary tenants.

Others argue that landlords have rights too, and that Spain needs to build more homes rather than simply introduce stricter rental rules.

The government has promised measures to improve housing affordability, but political disagreements have made progress difficult.

For many Spaniards, Maricarmen has become a symbol of a housing system they believe is failing ordinary people.

Her story raises a simple but uncomfortable question: if someone can lose their home after living there for seven decades, how secure is anyone's future?

title: Priced out: How one woman's eviction exposed Spain's housing crisis
summary: The death of an 87-year-old pensioner, just two weeks after being forced from her lifelong home, has become a rallying cry for a generation increasingly priced out of the housing market.

For seven decades, María del Carmen Abascal's modest Madrid flat was more than a roof over her head. It was a lifetime of memories, a place of familiarity and security in a city transformed beyond recognition. Yet on 23 September, the 87-year-old was carried out on a stretcher as police enforced an eviction order. Two weeks later, she was dead.

Her passing on 7 October has sent shockwaves through Spain, transforming an already bitter dispute over housing into a nationwide movement demanding fundamental change.

Known affectionately as Maricarmen, Abascal had lived in her apartment in Madrid's Retiro district since her teenage years. Her family benefited from an old rental agreement that provided protection against steep rent increases. When a property company acquired the flat, however, a lengthy legal battle followed over whether she could remain.

The dispute dragged on for years, taking an enormous toll on her health. Despite widespread public support and repeated efforts by campaigners to prevent her eviction, the courts ultimately ruled in favour of the property owner.

In a particularly cruel twist, an agreement allowing her to return to the flat was reached while she was in hospital. She died before she could go home.

Her case has struck a nerve in a country where housing has become one of the most divisive political issues.

Spain's property market has undergone a dramatic transformation. In major cities and popular tourist destinations, soaring rents have outpaced wage growth, leaving increasing numbers of people struggling to find affordable accommodation.

For younger generations, home ownership has become an increasingly distant prospect. Even university graduates with stable employment can find themselves spending a substantial proportion of their income on rent, sharing overcrowded flats or remaining in the family home well into adulthood.

The rapid expansion of short-term holiday rentals has added fuel to the fire. In neighbourhoods once dominated by local residents, apartments have increasingly been converted into tourist accommodation, prompting accusations that entire communities are being hollowed out in pursuit of profit.

Meanwhile, the growing influence of property investment firms has intensified concerns about housing becoming a financial asset rather than a basic human necessity.

Campaigners argue that allowing large investors to accumulate residential properties drives up prices and undermines tenants' security. Property owners and economists who favour market-based solutions counter that excessive regulation risks discouraging investment and worsening an already serious shortage of housing.

At the heart of the dispute lies a fundamental tension between private property rights and the public need for affordable homes.

Maricarmen's death has brought that conflict into sharp focus. Around 20,000 people marched through Madrid on 8 October, while demonstrations and vigils took place elsewhere in Spain. The political fallout has been considerable, with emergency housing measures, parliamentary disagreements and an early general election scheduled for 29 November.

Yet beyond the political arguments lies a more uncomfortable question about what constitutes a home.

For investors, a flat may represent an asset whose value rises and falls. For tenants, it represents stability, identity and belonging. For Maricarmen, it was the place where she had spent almost her entire life.

Her death cannot be reduced to a single political argument, nor can Spain's housing crisis be solved overnight. But her story has given a human face to a problem too often discussed in terms of market figures and economic forecasts.

Whether the protests lead to meaningful reform remains to be seen. What is already clear is that, for thousands of Spaniards, the right to a secure home has become a cause worth fighting for.
