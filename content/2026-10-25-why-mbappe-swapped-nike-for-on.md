summary: The footballer Kylian Mbappé left a famous sports brand to help a newer company build something.

Imagine you have been in the same group for twenty years. Everyone knows it, and it is very successful. Then a small, new group asks you to help build something. Would you stay, or would you change?

On 18 September 2026, the footballer Kylian Mbappé made a choice like this. Since 2006, the sports company Nike had made his boots and clothes. Now he has left Nike and joined On, a Swiss sportswear company that wants to grow beyond running and tennis into football.

This is a sponsorship deal. It is not a move to a new football team. A sponsor pays a player, and the player wears and promotes its products. Mbappé will be a global ambassador for On. He will also work with the company's product teams to develop and test future boots and clothes.

The money is a secret. On said the deal includes cash and equity, which means a small share in the company, but it did not say how much. Nike said it was proud of what they achieved together. Mbappé wrote online that this is a big change in his life. He said he is now working with people who want to try new ideas.

On says its first football boots are planned for 2027. It also named the former France star Thierry Henry as its director of football. The company says top players will test its products from the start. Remember, these are the company's own plans. We do not know yet if the boots will be better than other boots.

So, would you rather join a successful team or help a new team get started? A famous place can give you experience and support. A new project can give you more freedom to try your own ideas. Neither choice is always right. It depends on what you want to learn.

title: Why Mbappé Chose a New Sportswear Partner
summary: A footballer's switch from Nike to a Swiss newcomer is not a transfer. It is a bet on a different kind of career, and it raises a question many of us face.

When a footballer changes teams, everyone notices. When one changes boots, fewer people do, yet that kind of move can say just as much about how athletes think about their careers. On 18 September 2026, the Swiss company On announced its entry into football with Kylian Mbappé as a global ambassador, ending something that had lasted for roughly twenty years.

Reuters reported that Mbappé's relationship with Nike began in 2006, when he was nine. It is important to be precise about what changed. This is a sponsorship agreement, not a transfer: he has not joined a new club. He has changed the company that makes and promotes his equipment, and according to On he will also work directly with its product teams on developing and testing future footwear and clothing.

The most interesting detail is what has not been said. On said the agreement includes cash and equity, but no amount was disclosed. Equity, in general, means owning a small share of a company, so an owner benefits if the company grows. That would make this more than a fee for appearing in advertisements, because the athlete has a stake in whether the project succeeds. How large that stake is remains unknown.

Both sides offered reasons, and each deserves to be read as what it is: a statement from someone with an interest. Mbappé wrote that football had taken him through a big change and that he was now surrounded by innovators who share his dreams. David Allemann, On's co-CEO, said football needs new ideas rather than more of the same. Nike said it was proud of what it had achieved with him on and off the pitch.

Several of the claims in On's announcement are promotional. The company says its development will be athlete-led, with elite players involved from the start, and it describes its future products in glowing terms. It has named the former France star Thierry Henry as director of football and said that the Swiss international Sydney Schertenleib will help test products, including some for the women's game. Reuters reports that the first boots are planned for 2027. Until players wear them in matches, nobody can say whether they are better than anything else on the market.

What makes the story relevant beyond football is the choice underneath it. Staying with a famous partner offers experience, stability and a proven way of working. Joining a newcomer offers less certainty, but a bigger voice in what gets built. Neither is automatically the braver or the wiser option. Someone who walks away from a successful group might be chasing influence, or might simply be bored. Someone who stays might be loyal, or might simply be comfortable.

Perhaps the best test is a practical one. Think about a group you belong to, or a project you have joined. If somebody offered you more say but fewer guarantees, which would you want to know first: what you could lose, or what you could shape?
