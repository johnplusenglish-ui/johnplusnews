=== Core ===

title: Why Do New Words Spread So Fast?
summary: A word can travel from one group chat to the other side of the world. The reason people pass it on is much older than the internet.

Imagine opening your group chat after school. Somebody has posted a photo. Somebody else has replied with a word you have never seen, and everybody else seems to find it funny. You could look it up. It is probably faster to ask. Ten minutes later you are using it yourself.

Slang means informal words used by a particular group. Some words are brand new. Others are ordinary words that have been given a different job. A word can also change because somebody used it as a joke and the joke stuck. The same word can feel friendly between two friends and completely confusing to anybody else.

Young people have always done this. Your parents had words that annoyed their parents, and so did their parents. What has changed is the speed. A song, a game or a short video can carry a word out of one town and into a hundred countries in a few days. Somebody hears it, repeats it, and gives it a new audience.

Words like this do a real job. They make people feel close. A group of friends might have a joke that makes no sense at all to anyone outside it, and using it reminds everybody of the day it started. Slang can also be quicker than ordinary language. There are feelings that take a sentence to explain and one word to share.

The same thing works in reverse. A word that includes your friends can leave somebody else standing outside the conversation, wondering what everyone is laughing about. That is not always unkind, but it is worth noticing.

If you are learning English, this can be exciting and annoying at once. A dictionary can tell you what a word means. It cannot always tell you whether it belongs in a message to a friend or in an email to a teacher. That depends on the context, which means the situation and the people involved, and working it out is a skill rather than a mistake.

You do not have to use every new word you hear. Some will last for years and most will be gone by the summer. What lasts is the habit underneath: people changing language to make jokes, to be understood, and to feel like part of something.

=== Intermediate ===

summary: A word can go from one group chat to the whole world in a week. Who invented it is a harder question than it looks.

For most of a year, saying the numbers six and seven in the right tone of voice was enough to make a classroom laugh. Adults had no idea what was happening. The phrase came out of a rap song, moved into short videos, arrived in schools, and in October 2025 Dictionary.com made it the word of the year, admitting more or less openly that nobody could define it. The impulse behind it, though, is much older than any app.

Slang is informal language tied to particular groups and situations. It can make a conversation faster, funnier or more exact. It can also quietly tell everybody who is in and who is out. Psychologists writing for the American Psychological Association describe language as one of the main ways people recognise who belongs with them, and slang is that process running at high speed.

What the internet changed is the route a word can take. An expression used in one neighbourhood can appear in a song, get repeated by somebody with a large audience and reach viewers in countries where nobody knows the original joke. A phrase from a game becomes a caption, then turns up in a conversation at a bus stop. Along the way, the meaning often shifts. A word that started as a compliment can end up sarcastic.

That journey is usually invisible, and this is where people get it wrong. When an expression becomes huge on one platform, that platform is very rarely where it began. Plenty of English slang travelled out of Black communities long before it reached a wider audience, and the person who made a word famous is almost never the person who made it. Giving credit properly is difficult when the trail has gone cold, but assuming the loudest voice invented it is simply wrong.

For anyone learning English, slang can look like a list to memorise. Knowing the definition is only the start. A phrase that sounds natural in a message to a friend can sound strange in a job application, and fluent speakers move between those registers constantly without thinking about it. That switching is a skill, not evidence that one version of your English is fake.

There is also plenty of room to get it wrong. A word used warmly inside one group can land as an insult outside it. Asking what somebody meant is not embarrassing. It is usually the fastest way out of an awkward moment, and people generally enjoy explaining.

Not every expression will survive, and not everybody your age wants to use the newest one anyway. The bigger story is not a war between proper English and a generation of strange words. It is people using language to experiment, to belong and to be understood, exactly as every generation before them did.

=== Advanced ===

title: Who Gets to Decide What Counts as Real English?
summary: Slang connects friends, crosses borders and baffles adults. The more interesting question is not what the words mean but who carries them.

For roughly a year, two numbers were enough to start a joke. Saying six and seven in a particular way set off entire classrooms while adults asked, with increasing irritation, what it was supposed to mean. The phrase came out of a rap song, spread through short videos and into schools, and Dictionary.com made it the 2025 word of the year while effectively conceding that it had no stable definition. The appeal was never the meaning. It was the pleasure of being in on something.

Slang gives people a way to be quick, playful or precise about feelings their group already recognises. It signals belonging: you get the reference because you share a friendship, an interest or a history. Psychologists writing for the American Psychological Association make the point that the same language which creates that connection also draws a line around everybody who does not have it. Both things happen at once, and usually nobody decides to do either.

The internet alters the scale rather than the mechanism. A phrase can move from a community into a song, a stream or a fifteen second video and reach millions who will never meet its original speakers. It can be repeated sincerely, borrowed for a joke, translated, and adapted again into something its first users would not recognise. Visibility is not authorship. The platform where you found a word is almost never the place it was made.

That distinction matters whenever people talk about Gen Z slang as though a whole generation had sat down and agreed a vocabulary. Expressions have roots in specific places and communities, and a great deal of English slang has come out of Black communities before being repackaged for a general audience. Sometimes the origin is genuinely hard to establish. Sometimes it is simply left out, which is a different problem. Admitting the uncertainty is more honest than crediting whoever happened to go viral.

There is a practical lesson here too. A dictionary entry tells you what a word can mean; context tells you what the speaker is doing with it. Are they being affectionate, sarcastic or genuinely unkind? Are they talking to a close friend or to somebody who has power over their grades? Fluent speakers move between registers without noticing, and learning to make that choice deliberately is worth more than forcing the newest expression into every sentence.

Adults often hear unfamiliar slang as proof that the language is deteriorating. Young speakers often hear formal rules as an instruction to sound like somebody else. Both readings miss how much range people already have. A group chat and a university application have different purposes, and being able to handle both widens your options instead of asking you to give one up.

Most of these expressions will fade, and a few will become so ordinary that nobody remembers thinking them strange. The joke may not outlast the school year. The habit that produced it will. Language changes because people keep finding new ways to describe what happened to them, and the most useful response to a word you do not know is curiosity about who is using it and why.
