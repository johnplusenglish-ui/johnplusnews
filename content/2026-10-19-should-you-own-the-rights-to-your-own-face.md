=== Core ===

Imagine opening your phone and watching a video of yourself saying something terrible. You know you never said it, and the video was never recorded. Someone used artificial intelligence to make a fake version of your face and voice. What could you do?

This kind of video is called a deepfake. AI can now create realistic pictures, sound recordings and moving images. Some are made for jokes or films, but others are used to embarrass, deceive or hurt people. The problem is that viewers may believe the video is real.

In October 2026, Denmark announced plans for a law to protect people against certain AI copies of their appearance and voice. The proposal would make it easier to challenge the unauthorised sharing of realistic digital imitations. It was a proposed law, not something that had already solved the problem.

Supporters say people should be able to control how others use their faces. A fake video could damage someone's reputation, create trouble at work or make friends and family believe something untrue. Ordinary people may be affected, not only celebrities and politicians.

However, the issue is complicated. Films, jokes and political cartoons sometimes copy famous people. It is important to protect artistic freedom and public discussion while stopping harmful fakes. Laws also have to work when videos are made or shared in other countries.

The internet has made it easy to copy photographs. AI now makes it possible to invent actions and speech as well. Denmark's proposal asks a simple but difficult question: if somebody can create a convincing digital version of you, who should decide what that version is allowed to do?

=== Intermediate ===

Your face used to be difficult to copy convincingly. A skilled actor, make-up artist or video editor might imitate you, but the result was rarely perfect. Artificial intelligence has changed that. It is increasingly possible to produce realistic footage or audio of a person saying things they never said, raising concerns about reputation, consent and personal identity.

In October 2026, Denmark announced plans to introduce legislation addressing the unauthorised sharing of AI-generated copies of people's appearance and voices. The proposed rules would strengthen individuals' ability to challenge convincing digital impersonations. At the time of the announcement, the legislation was still a proposal rather than an established legal solution.

Deepfakes can be used for entertainment, but the technology also creates serious risks. A fabricated video may spread false statements, humiliate a private individual or damage trust in genuine evidence. Once an image is shared widely, removing every copy becomes extremely difficult.

There are challenges in deciding where protection should begin and end. A realistic fake designed to deceive is different from an obvious cartoon or a clearly labelled comedy sketch. Journalists, artists and campaigners may have legitimate reasons to criticise public figures using parody. Laws must be precise enough to protect personal dignity without blocking ordinary expression.

Enforcement presents another difficulty. Images can be generated cheaply and shared across national borders, while some platforms respond more quickly to complaints than others. Even identifying the person responsible for a harmful fake can be difficult.

Denmark's proposal reflects a wider change in how people think about privacy. The concern is no longer only who may photograph you, but who may invent a convincing version of you. In a world of increasingly powerful AI tools, control over personal identity may become as important as control over personal information.

=== Advanced ===

For much of photographic history, the credibility of an image rested on the assumption that some recognisable event had taken place in front of a camera. Generative artificial intelligence has weakened that assumption. It can produce plausible representations of people performing actions or making statements that never occurred, creating new difficulties for consent, reputation and the public's ability to evaluate evidence.

Denmark responded in October 2026 by announcing proposed legislation aimed at limiting the unauthorised distribution of realistic AI-generated reproductions of individuals' faces and voices. Its significance lies in treating a person's digital likeness as something deserving specific protection. The proposal should not, however, be described as a law already in force.

The potential harms extend beyond embarrassing online jokes. Fabricated recordings can be used to impersonate people, distribute humiliating material, damage professional reputations or manipulate public debate. Even after the falsehood is exposed, the original impression may survive in screenshots, forwarded messages and private groups.

A workable legal framework must nevertheless distinguish harmful impersonation from legitimate expression. Satire, journalism, artistic adaptation and clearly identifiable fictional performances may all involve portraying real individuals without their direct participation. An excessively broad restriction could become a tool for suppressing criticism, particularly when powerful public figures are involved.

Practical enforcement is equally challenging. Online content crosses borders almost instantly, while technical systems designed to identify manipulated media remain imperfect. Removing a video from one platform does little if copies continue circulating elsewhere. Legal remedies may therefore need to be combined with better platform policies and public understanding of digital evidence.

The Danish initiative illustrates a shift in the meaning of privacy. It is not merely a question of keeping existing personal information secret. It is also about preventing others from manufacturing a persuasive identity on someone's behalf. As synthetic media becomes commonplace, societies must decide how much control individuals should have over their own digital doubles.
