=== Core ===

title: Is That Product Really Green?
summary: Many adverts say a product is good for the environment. How can you tell if the claim is true?

You are choosing shampoo in a supermarket. One bottle says it is good for the planet, and another has pictures of leaves and trees on it. Which one would you buy? Many people want to shop in a more environmentally friendly way, so companies know that green messages can help sell products. But these messages are not always clear or honest.

When a company makes something seem better for the environment than it really is, this is called greenwashing. A product might use some recycled plastic but still create a lot of waste. An airline might offer customers the chance to pay towards environmental projects when they book a flight. That payment may support useful work, but it does not stop the aircraft from producing pollution.

In July 2026, the UK's advertising regulator criticised advertisements from Eurowings and Qatar Airways. The companies had made claims about paying to compensate for flight emissions. The regulator decided that the adverts gave customers a misleading impression of how much environmental damage could be cancelled out. The airlines were told not to use those claims in the same way again.

The European Union has also introduced stronger rules against unclear environmental advertising. Since September 2026, businesses have faced restrictions on general green claims that do not have proper evidence. Labels cannot simply promise that a product is environmentally friendly without showing what that means. These rules aim to make it easier for customers to compare products fairly.

For shoppers, the best question is often a simple one: what exactly has improved? If packaging says it is recycled, find out how much recycled material it contains. If a company says it has reduced pollution, ask what figures support the claim. Not every business using green advertising is dishonest, and small improvements are worth making. But a leaf on a label is not proof that a product is good for the planet. Real progress needs more than a nice picture.

=== Intermediate ===

A flight that claims to be climate-conscious, a T-shirt advertised as sustainable and a bottle of cleaning liquid covered in green leaves can all make us feel better about spending money. Companies know that shoppers increasingly care about the environment, and many have changed the way they advertise their products. Some claims describe genuine progress. Others are much harder to believe, especially when important information is left out.

The practice is called greenwashing: making a business or product appear more environmentally responsible than it really is. Sometimes a claim is clearly false. More often, it concentrates on one positive detail while ignoring a larger problem. A brand might use recycled packaging but produce enormous amounts of waste. A retailer might advertise a small sustainable clothing collection while encouraging customers to buy far more clothes than they need.

Air travel offers a useful example. In July 2026, Britain's Advertising Standards Authority upheld complaints about environmental claims made by Eurowings and Qatar Airways. Their advertisements gave the impression that customers could pay towards projects that fully compensated for the emissions from flights. The regulator found that the companies had not provided enough evidence to support that impression. Environmental projects may do valuable work, but they do not automatically cancel out the impact of a particular journey.

Regulators have become increasingly concerned about vague promises. New European Union consumer rules, which began applying in September 2026, restrict general environmental claims that companies cannot properly support. The legislation also addresses unreliable sustainability labels and certain claims of climate neutrality based on offsetting. The aim is to stop companies gaining an advantage simply by choosing attractive green language.

However, understanding environmental impact is not always straightforward. A product may use less plastic but require more energy to make. A delivery company may reduce emissions on one route while expanding its overall business. Consumers cannot be expected to become experts in climate science every time they shop, which is why accurate information and independent checks matter.

There are practical ways to be more careful. Look for specific figures rather than broad promises, and check whether a claim has been independently verified. Ask whether the advertised improvement concerns the whole product or just one part of it. Companies deserve recognition for genuine progress, but they also need to acknowledge what has not improved. Buying something with a green label should not require an act of faith. If a business makes an environmental promise, it should be able to back it up.

=== Advanced ===

title: The Greenwashing Problem: When Sustainability Becomes a Sales Pitch
summary: When sustainability becomes a sales pitch, regulators are stepping in. How can shoppers tell genuine progress from clever marketing?

A company rarely needs to demonstrate environmental virtue in detail to benefit from its appearance. A muted green label, a reassuring phrase about sustainability or an invitation to compensate for emissions can transform an ordinary transaction into something that feels almost ethical. The appeal is understandable: consumers want to make responsible choices without examining the full environmental history of every purchase. That convenience, however, has created fertile ground for greenwashing.

The term describes claims that misrepresent or exaggerate environmental performance, though the most effective examples are not always straightforward falsehoods. A manufacturer may accurately advertise recycled packaging while avoiding discussion of the resources consumed by the product itself. An airline may offer a contribution to climate projects without making clear how uncertain the resulting benefits can be. The selective presentation of favourable information can create an impression that is technically supported in part but misleading as a whole.

In July 2026, Britain's Advertising Standards Authority upheld rulings against environmental advertisements from Eurowings and Qatar Airways. The regulator found that claims concerning carbon offsetting risked persuading customers that they could pay to compensate fully for the emissions associated with particular flights, without adequate evidence. Offsetting schemes may finance useful initiatives, but assessing their impact requires answering difficult questions about additionality, permanence and measurement. A promise to compensate for emissions is not equivalent to preventing the emissions in the first place.

Regulators are attempting to narrow the gap between marketing language and substantiated performance. Since September 2026, EU consumer rules have restricted vague environmental claims and unreliable sustainability labels, while addressing certain claims of climate neutrality based on offsetting. The shift places greater responsibility on businesses to explain their environmental credentials rather than relying on broad, emotionally appealing phrases. It also recognises that consumers rarely possess the information required to challenge complicated scientific assertions on their own.

The challenge is that environmental performance involves trade-offs that resist simple labels. A reusable product may have a higher manufacturing footprint but a lower impact over time. Recycled materials may reduce pressure on natural resources without eliminating energy consumption or pollution. An advertising system that reduces such questions to a cheerful green symbol risks creating false confidence even when genuine improvements have occurred.

There is a danger, too, that fear of regulatory action encourages companies to say nothing about worthwhile initiatives. The solution is not to ban environmental communication but to make it more precise. Claims should identify the specific benefit, the evidence supporting it and the limitations of the improvement. Consumers are entitled to know whether a company has substantially reduced harm or merely found a flattering way to describe a modest change. Sustainability cannot become credible while remaining principally a matter of choosing the right adjectives.
