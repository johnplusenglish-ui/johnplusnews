title: Would You Like School to Start Later?
summary: A Swiss school let students choose when to start. Almost all of them chose later, and they slept longer.

Imagine your school gives you a choice. You can arrive at 7:30, or you can wait and arrive at 8:30. Would you stay in bed for the extra hour?

Many people would say yes straight away. But there is a catch. A later start might mean a later finish, and that could change your bus, your training and your evening. The easy choice isn't always the best one.

A school in Gossau, in Switzerland, tried something like this. Before the change, classes there began at 7:20. Then the school introduced flexible hours. Students could arrive at 7:30, or they could wait until 8:30, when classes officially began.

Researchers from the University of Zurich asked students about their sleep and health before the change, and again a year later. In total, they collected 754 completed surveys. The students were about 14 years old.

Almost all of them chose the later start: 95 out of 100. They woke up later, but they went to bed at about the same time. On average, they slept 45 minutes more on school days. They also said they had fewer problems falling asleep.

This doesn't prove that a later start is perfect for every school. It was a survey, so it shows what happened, not exactly why. Some students travel a long way. Some have training or family jobs after school. A later finish could be hard for them.

So here is the real question. Would you choose more sleep or a free evening? If your school offered the choice tomorrow, which timetable would you pick?

title: Would You Choose a Later Start to School?
summary: A Swiss school offered students a choice between an early start and a later one. Researchers asked what happened to their sleep.

At 6:45 on a dark winter morning, an extra hour in bed sounds almost too good to be true. But would you still want it if it meant a later finish in the afternoon?

Researchers in Switzerland wanted to know what happens when students are actually given a choice. At a secondary school in Gossau, classes used to begin at 7:20. Then the school introduced flexible hours. Students could arrive at 7:30 or wait until 8:30, when classes officially began, and some lessons were arranged at midday or in the afternoon.

A team from the University of Zurich and the University Children's Hospital Zurich asked students about their sleep and health before the change and again a year later. The study appeared in the Journal of Adolescent Health in February 2026. They collected 754 responses in total, from students whose median age was 14. Some students answered both surveys and others answered only one, so the results describe groups of students as well as individuals.

When students were allowed to choose, almost all of them did. In fact, 95% used the later option, and they started about 38 minutes later. Most woke up later but went to bed at about the same time, so they slept about 45 minutes more on school days, on average. They also reported fewer problems falling asleep, and fewer students had a low score for health-related quality of life.

The researchers also reported higher scores in maths and English. That sounds like a clear win, but a survey like this cannot prove that the later start caused every change. Students were not sorted into different timetables by chance, so other things that changed during the year could also play a part.

There are everyday questions that a sleep study cannot answer. A student who depends on a bus from a village has to follow the bus timetable. Someone with football training or a younger brother to collect may find a later finish harder than an early start. A flexible system works best when the school and families can plan around it.

So, if your school offered the choice for a month, which would you pick: an extra hour in the morning or an extra hour in the afternoon?

=== Advanced ===

title: School Starts Later. What Changes?
summary: Swiss students who could start school later mostly did, and slept longer. The study did not test a later finish, so that half of the deal is a question for you.

It looks like a simple trade: an hour more in bed in exchange for a school day that ends an hour later. Ask yourself at seven in the morning and again at four in the afternoon, and you may well give two different answers. The Swiss study behind this story did not actually make students accept a later finish. It asked something narrower and more practical, namely what happens to teenagers' sleep when they are allowed to start later.

In Gossau, in north-eastern Switzerland, classes at one secondary school used to begin at 7:20. The school then introduced a flexible model, in which students could arrive at 7:30 or wait until 8:30, when classes officially began, and some lessons were moved to midday or the afternoon. Researchers from the University of Zurich surveyed students in spring 2022, before the change, and again in spring 2023, once it was in place.

The study appeared in the Journal of Adolescent Health in February 2026. It covers two secondary schools in Switzerland, although the university's summary concentrates on Gossau. In all, 351 students answered the first survey and 403 the second, and 212 answered both times. The median age was 14. That is why the researchers describe 754 responses rather than 754 students.

When offered the choice, 95% of students took the later option, and the median start moved about 38 minutes later. Wake-up times shifted while bedtimes stayed roughly the same, so students gained an average of 45 minutes of sleep on school days. They also reported fewer problems falling asleep, fewer of them had a clinically low health-related quality of life, and their scores in maths and English were higher.

None of this should be read as a guarantee. This was a before-and-after survey, not a randomised experiment: nobody was assigned to a timetable by chance, and a year is long enough for other things to change. The authors conclude only that flexible schedules may offer an effective way to reduce adolescent sleep deficits, a considerably more modest claim than saying that a later start improves grades or health for every student.

Flexibility is the model's main attraction, and also its main puzzle. A student who lives close by or badly needs sleep can arrive later, whereas one who relies on an early bus can still come at 7:30. But a survey about sleep cannot capture what a later arrival does to everything around it: after-school training, caring responsibilities, transport and the school's own staffing, since someone has to supervise and teach in the early slot.

So the question in the headline remains open, and it is a personal one. Would you take the extra hour in bed if it cost you an hour in the afternoon? And what would you want your school to ask before it changed anything?
