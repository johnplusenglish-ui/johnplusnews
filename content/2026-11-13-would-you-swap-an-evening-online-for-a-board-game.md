title: Board Games Are Back on the Table
summary: A games company says more young people want to play together in person. But can we trust its numbers?

Imagine it is a Saturday evening. You could log on to an online game, or you could set up a board on the table and play with friends. A games company says that more young people are choosing the table.

The company is Asmodee, a big games company. It sells board games such as Catan, Ticket to Ride and Carcassonne. It also sells trading card games. On 13 November 2026, its boss, Thomas Koegler, told the news agency Reuters that demand for tabletop games is strong. He said that people want to spend time together in person.

Asmodee has numbers to support this. In the three months from July to September 2026, its board game sales grew by 18.4 per cent, and its trading card sales grew by 51.6 per cent, compared with the same period a year before. These are the company's own figures, so it is wise to take them with a pinch of salt.

Mr Koegler also said that board games have become more popular with people aged 16 to 25, who now put them at the heart of their social life. He mentioned a survey made by Asmodee and a research company called Kantar. It found that 74 per cent of young people called Gen Z think that real-life experiences are more important than digital ones. Again, this comes from research linked to a games company, not from an independent study. A big games event in the Netherlands also had 33,000 visitors on 7 and 8 November.

What do these facts really tell us? Sales show how many games people bought, but not who played them, or how often. A parent might buy a game for the whole family. A survey shows what people say matters to them, which is not always the same as what they do on a Saturday night.

Even so, the idea is interesting. A board game can break the ice, because everyone sits in the same room and talks, laughs and argues about the rules. That is a far cry from playing with strangers online. Mr Koegler expects game nights to stay popular on Saturdays, especially around Christmas, when the weather is dark and cold. Maybe the best plan is to put your phone down for one evening and see what happens.

Think about the last time your group of friends could not decide what to do. According to the boss of one of the world's biggest tabletop games companies, more and more young people are picking the table.

Asmodee is a games company that publishes and distributes board games such as Catan, Ticket to Ride and Carcassonne. It also distributes trading card games made by other publishers, including Pokémon, Magic: The Gathering and One Piece. He expects game nights to stay a popular Saturday plan, particularly at Christmas, when the dark weather makes games more appealing.

The company's latest results back this up, at least on paper. In the quarter from July to September 2026, Asmodee's board game sales rose by 18.4 per cent and its trading card game sales by 51.6 per cent, compared with the same quarter a year earlier. These are the company's own figures, and a firm that sells games has every reason to sound upbeat, so they should be taken with a pinch of salt.

Mr Koegler said that board games have gained popularity among 16 to 25 year olds, who are now putting them at the heart of their social life. To support this, he pointed to a barometer compiled by Asmodee and the research company Kantar in 2026, which found that 74 per cent of the so-called Gen Z believed in-person experiences were more important than digital ones. It is worth remembering that this is commercial research, not an independent social science study, and it measures opinions rather than behaviour. Saying that real life matters more is easy. Spending a Friday night without your phone is harder.

He also pointed to events. Attendance is rising at trade shows such as Spiel Essen and Gen Con, he said. The largest board game event in the Netherlands, Spellenspektakel, reported 33,000 visitors at its 2026 edition on 7 and 8 November, and its first day sold out.

So what can we really conclude? Sales figures show how many games were sold, but not who is playing them or how much time those people spend on screens. A parent might buy a game for a whole family. A rise in sales is therefore a clue, not proof, that teenagers are swapping screens for tabletops. Many people probably do both.

Even so, the idea is appealing. A game around a table is a breath of fresh air because it is hard to hide behind a phone: you have to talk, bluff, negotiate and sometimes lose gracefully in front of your friends. Whether you would give up an evening online for that is a question only you can answer.

title: Are Teenagers Really Turning Back to Tabletop Games?
summary: A leading games company says young people want shared, offline experiences. Its sales are up, but what do the figures actually prove?

It is easy to picture the scene: a group of teenagers around a table, a board between them and phones face down. Whether that scene is becoming more common is harder to say, but one of the biggest names in tabletop gaming believes it is. In an interview with Reuters on 13 November 2026, Asmodee's chief executive, Thomas Koegler, argued that demand for board games and trading card games was holding firm because people are looking for ways to spend time together in person.

Asmodee, a major games company, publishes and distributes board games including Catan, Ticket to Ride and Carcassonne, and it distributes trading card games from other publishers, among them Pokémon, Magic: The Gathering and One Piece. In the quarter from July to September 2026, sales in its board game category rose by 18.4 per cent and trading card game sales by 51.6 per cent, while total net sales climbed by 36.8 per cent, although part of that came from acquisitions.

The figures that grab the attention, however, concern people rather than products. Mr Koegler said that board games have gained popularity among 16 to 25 year olds, who are now putting them at the heart of their social life. He cited a barometer compiled by Asmodee and the research company Kantar in 2026, according to which 74 per cent of the so-called Gen Z considered in-person experiences more important than digital ones. The Netherlands' largest board game event, Spellenspektakel, reported 33,000 visitors on 7 and 8 November, with its first day sold out.

It would be a mistake to treat any of this as settled evidence about teenage habits. The sales figures are the company's own, and the 74 per cent comes from commercial research produced partly by a business that sells games, not from an independent social science study. A survey question about what matters more is also a statement of opinion, and opinions can be a long way from behaviour: plenty of people say that real-life time matters most while still reaching for their phones.

Sales data raise their own difficulties. A rise in sales says how many boxes and card packs were bought, not who opened them, how often they were played or whether anyone's screen time fell as a result. Parents and collectors buy plenty of this material, and a trading card can sit in a binder without ever being played.

Even so, the story touches on something genuine. Playing a board game removes many of the escape routes that a screen offers. You have to read faces, bargain, bluff and lose gracefully, all in real time and in the same room, which is a far cry from the anonymity of many online games.

Perhaps the most sensible reading of the evidence is a modest one. Tabletop games are selling well, and a company that sells them believes that young people want shared, offline experiences. The jury is still out on whether that means a real change in how teenagers spend their evenings, and no sales chart can tell you whether you would choose a board game over a night online.

=== Activity ===

Work in pairs. Choose a board or card game that you both know, or invent a simple one using paper and a pen. One of you has one minute to persuade a group of teenagers to play it instead of going online. The other asks one question and then says whether they are convinced, and why. Swap roles and try again. Nobody needs to share personal information, and no phones are needed.
