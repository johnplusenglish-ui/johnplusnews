In March 2025, an American adventurer set off across the Bay of Bengal in a small inflatable boat. He was carrying a camera, a coconut and a can of Diet Coke. His destination was North Sentinel Island, home to one of the world's most isolated Indigenous communities. Mykhailo Polyakov hoped to capture footage that nobody else had managed to obtain. Instead, his expedition ended in arrest, a prison sentence and widespread criticism.

The Sentinelese have lived on their island for generations, resisting contact with outsiders. Very little is known about their language, customs or population. What is clear is their determination to remain isolated. Indian law prohibits anyone from travelling within five kilometres of the island, partly to protect its inhabitants from diseases introduced by visitors. Polyakov ignored the restrictions. He reached the shore, attempted to attract attention by blowing a whistle and eventually left without encountering anyone. He later received a 25-day prison sentence and a fine.

His actions have become part of a wider debate about a new type of adventurer: social media creators seeking encounters with communities that have deliberately chosen to remain separate from the outside world. According to Survival International, an organisation that campaigns for Indigenous rights, there are approximately 196 uncontacted Indigenous communities worldwide. Many live in remote parts of South America, particularly the Amazon rainforest. Their isolation is not necessarily the result of ignorance about modern civilisation. In many cases, it reflects a conscious decision shaped by previous encounters with violence, exploitation and disease.

The risks are considerable. Communities with limited exposure to common infectious diseases may have little immunity to them. Even an apparently harmless visit could introduce an illness with devastating consequences. Campaigners are increasingly concerned that the prospect of viral fame may encourage people to take those risks. Footage of a supposedly untouched civilisation can attract millions of views, bringing advertising revenue, sponsorship deals and international recognition. In an industry where attention is everything, exclusivity has become a valuable commodity.

Not every creator filming Indigenous communities is acting irresponsibly. Some work with established communities, obtain permission and contribute financially to local projects. Tourism and documentary-making can provide income and help raise awareness of threats to Indigenous land. But there is an important distinction between filming people who have agreed to participate and pursuing those who have repeatedly rejected contact. Critics argue that the latter treats human beings as material for entertainment rather than as people entitled to privacy and self-determination.

Polyakov has defended his actions, arguing that documenting isolated communities serves the public interest. He also disputes claims that his visit posed a serious danger. For Indigenous rights campaigners, however, the fundamental issue is consent. The fact that outsiders possess the technology to reach and film a community does not automatically give them the right to do so. The internet has made remote places more visible and more accessible than ever before. But as the competition for extraordinary content intensifies, an uncomfortable question remains: does our curiosity about other people's lives outweigh their right to be left alone?
