=== Core ===

summary: A radio signal travelled for more than ten billion years before it reached Earth. What can it tell us about the universe?

A signal from space reached Earth after travelling for more than ten billion years. It lasted only a tiny part of a second, but astronomers were able to detect it with a radio telescope in South Africa. The signal came from a distant galaxy that existed when the universe was much younger. Scientists reported the discovery in October 2026, describing it as the most distant fast radio burst whose home galaxy had been identified.

A fast radio burst is an extremely powerful flash of radio waves. Unlike ordinary radio programmes, these signals come from natural events in space. They last for just a few milliseconds, so telescopes must be ready to record them immediately. Astronomers have detected many such bursts, but it is difficult to find exactly where each one came from.

This burst is known as FRB 20240304B. Scientists first detected it using the MeerKAT telescope system in South Africa. They then used other observations, including images from the James Webb Space Telescope, to identify the distant galaxy linked to the signal. The galaxy was small, contained many young stars and existed when the universe was only about three billion years old.

Researchers still do not know exactly what produces every fast radio burst. One possible explanation involves magnetars, which are the remains of dead stars with extremely powerful magnetic fields. These objects can release enormous amounts of energy. But it is important to remember that scientists have not proved that a magnetar caused this particular signal.

The discovery matters for another reason. As radio waves travel through space, they pass through gas and other matter that can change the signal. By studying those changes, astronomers can learn about material between galaxies, including matter that is difficult to see directly. A very distant burst may therefore provide information about much of the universe's history.

The signal is not a message sent by intelligent life. Nobody was trying to contact us, as far as researchers know. It is a natural flash of energy that began its journey billions of years before Earth looked anything like it does today. Yet that may be even more remarkable. With the right instruments, humans can study an event that happened long before the Sun and our planet existed, and use it to understand the enormous universe around us.

=== Intermediate ===

For more than ten billion years, a burst of radio energy travelled across the universe before arriving at Earth. When it finally reached our telescopes, it lasted just milliseconds. Yet scientists were able to trace the flash to an extraordinarily distant galaxy, setting a new record for the most remote fast radio burst with an identified source. The discovery, reported in October 2026, gives astronomers an unusual opportunity to investigate what the universe was like when it was still young.

Fast radio bursts, or FRBs, are intense flashes of radio waves that arrive from distant parts of space. Their exact causes remain uncertain. Some seem to occur only once, while others repeat. Magnetars, the highly magnetic remains of dead stars, are considered one possible source. The difficulty is that a burst can be over before astronomers have time to examine it closely, and many are detected without a precise location.

The latest record-breaker, named FRB 20240304B, was picked up by the MeerKAT radio telescope array in South Africa. An international team then used additional observations, including data from the James Webb Space Telescope, to identify its host galaxy. The galaxy contained relatively few heavy elements and was forming new stars rapidly. Researchers estimate that the burst was produced when the universe was around three billion years old, roughly a fifth of its present age.

Identifying the galaxy matters because distance changes the scientific value of the signal. Radio waves do not travel through completely empty space. Along the way, they pass through thin gas containing particles that affect how different frequencies arrive. By measuring these effects and comparing bursts from different distances, astronomers can build a clearer picture of matter spread between galaxies. Much of that matter is difficult to observe directly.

The record also raises questions about the early universe. If powerful bursts occurred in distant, young galaxies, they may have been a regular part of cosmic history. A galaxy producing large numbers of new stars may also produce the kinds of stellar remains capable of generating such events. But the evidence does not yet identify the precise object responsible for this particular flash, and researchers are cautious about drawing conclusions from one exceptional detection.

There is something striking about how the discovery was made. A fleeting signal reached a radio telescope on Earth, while a powerful space telescope supplied the information needed to find its distant home. Together, the instruments turned a fraction of a second into a glimpse of the universe billions of years ago. The burst was not a message from another civilisation. It was something just as fascinating: a natural event whose journey allows us to investigate parts of cosmic history we could never visit.

=== Advanced ===

summary: A fleeting radio burst from a distant galaxy gives astronomers a rare view of the early universe. Why does such a tiny signal matter?

It took more than ten billion years for the signal to arrive and only milliseconds for it to disappear. Yet that fleeting burst of radio energy has offered astronomers an unusually detailed glimpse of the early universe. In research published in October 2026, an international team traced a fast radio burst, designated FRB 20240304B, to a galaxy so distant that the event occurred when the cosmos was roughly one-fifth of its present age. The identification set a new distance record for a burst whose host galaxy has been established, and it opens another window onto the matter dispersed across intergalactic space.

Fast radio bursts are intense flashes of electromagnetic radiation that remain mysterious despite nearly two decades of observation. Their brevity complicates the task of locating them, while the diversity of detected signals suggests that the phenomenon may not have one simple explanation. Some sources repeat; others have so far been observed only once. Magnetars, the extraordinarily magnetic remnants of massive stars, are a leading possibility, but assigning a specific physical cause to an individual burst requires evidence that is seldom straightforward to obtain.

The newly reported signal was detected by the MeerKAT array in South Africa using instrumentation designed to identify rapid events as they happen. Locating the burst was only the beginning. Researchers also used the James Webb Space Telescope to examine its likely host, a relatively small, metal-poor galaxy undergoing vigorous star formation. Measurements placed the galaxy at a redshift of about 2.15, indicating that the radiation began its journey when the universe was approximately three billion years old. The finding links a powerful transient signal to an era of cosmic history that is exceptionally difficult to investigate in detail.

There is particular value in the journey itself. Radio waves cross enormous expanses of thin gas, and free electrons along the route delay their different frequencies by different amounts. Astronomers can measure that dispersion and use it, alongside distance estimates, to investigate matter that is otherwise extremely difficult to detect. A sample of well-localised bursts spanning a wide range of distances could eventually help map the distribution of ordinary matter between galaxies and reveal how that distribution has evolved through cosmic time.

The discovery should nevertheless be kept in proportion. Identifying the host galaxy does not demonstrate that a magnetar produced the burst, nor does one record-breaking event provide a complete account of the early universe. Observational selection effects also matter: astronomers are more likely to detect the brightest or most favourably positioned signals at such extraordinary distances. Further observations will be needed to establish how representative this event is and whether similar bursts were widespread when galaxies were rapidly forming stars.

What makes FRB 20240304B compelling is the convergence of instruments and ideas. A ground-based radio array captured a transient event, a space telescope supplied crucial information about its ancient surroundings, and the behaviour of the radio waves carried evidence of material encountered along the way. The signal was not deliberately addressed to anyone, but it has nevertheless delivered information from a period long before the Earth existed. In astronomy, a fraction of a second can become a remarkably extensive historical document.
