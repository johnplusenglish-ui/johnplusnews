title: Are Ties Cool Again?
summary: A British shirt company says it sold many more ties this summer. Some people think young people are bringing ties back.

What do you think of when you see a tie? For many people, it is something old-fashioned, perhaps part of a school uniform or a boring office job. But in October 2026, a British newspaper asked a surprising question: are ties becoming cool again?

Ties almost disappeared in the last few years. The Guardian says two things helped to push them out. One was a style in which men wore suits with open shirts and no tie. The other was the time of Covid, when many people worked from home and did not need to dress up.

Now there are signs of a change. A British company called TM Lewin says that its tie sales went up by 200% this summer, compared with the summer before. That sounds huge, but be careful. It is only one company, and it did not say how many ties it sold. The company also closed its shops in the pandemic and is now opening some again, so the starting point may have been low.

Why might young people like ties? The Guardian gives one idea. It says the open-collar look is now linked with older bosses, and some young people do not want to dress like their boss, so they dress up. This is only one explanation, not proven fact. The head of TM Lewin said that men are dressing smarter for work and for weddings. He joked that the tie has died about ten times and always comes back.

The story is not the same everywhere. Weeks before, the House of Lords, part of the British parliament, changed its rules so that men no longer have to wear a tie in the chamber. In some places, the tie is on its way out. In others, it is a blast from the past that is returning.

Ties have a long history. They began with cravats, a kind of scarf worn by soldiers from Croatia who served in France in the 1600s. A New York tailor patented the modern tie in the early 1920s, and the shapes and knots have changed many times since then. Fashion often seems to come full circle. Do you think ties are cool, or are they only a uniform?

title: Why Is a Dying Fashion Suddenly Selling?
summary: A British menswear retailer reports that tie sales rose by 200% this summer, and a Guardian column links it to young people rejecting their bosses' casual style. One company's figure is intriguing, but it cannot show a national trend.

For years the necktie looked like a fashion on its way out. The Guardian says two forces nearly finished it: the habit of wearing a suit with an open-collar shirt, and the shift to working from home during Covid. In October 2026, however, the paper ran a column asking whether ties are returning, and whether Generation Z is responsible.

The evidence is a single sales figure. The menswear retailer TM Lewin says that year-on-year sales of ties rose by 200% this summer. That deserves attention, but it deserves caution as well. It comes from one company, not from the whole clothing industry, and the coverage gives no absolute numbers, so the rise could be from a small base. The retailer also closed all its shops in the pandemic and has been rebuilding: according to a trade report, it plans seven stores after two openings in November, compared with about 150 at its peak in the mid-2010s. A business that is growing again would be expected to sell more of everything.

The company's managing director, Dan Ferris, told The Times that men are dressing smarter for work and for events such as weddings, and joked that the tie has died about ten times and keeps coming back. He added that ties are often an extra purchase for customers who come in to look at suits. This is a seller describing its own customers, which is useful testimony but not neutral research.

The Guardian's explanation for the youth link is a generational backlash. In its view, the informal look is now the habit of well-off, middle-aged men, and young people, who do not want to resemble the boss, dress up instead. It is an appealing theory, and it fits a familiar pattern in which each generation defines itself against the one before. But it is the columnist's interpretation, and the sales figure does not tell us the age of any buyer. The piece also says, more lightly, that young people may be choosing leather shoes over trainers, without figures to support it.

Meanwhile the evidence points in more than one direction. Only weeks before the column, the House of Lords relaxed its rules so that men no longer have to wear a tie in the chamber. The tie may be a sign of the times in one place and a blast from the past in another, and the two stories do not contradict each other if you allow that fashion moves unevenly.

Its history supports that cautious view. The tie grew out of the cravat, worn by Croatian soldiers who served in France during the Thirty Years' War in the 1600s, and the modern necktie was patented by a New York tailor, Jesse Langsdorf, in the early 1920s. Since then, lengths, widths and knots have been in constant motion: the four-in-hand knot was popular in Victorian times, the windsor arrived between the world wars, and the half-windsor followed in the 1950s. What looks like a revival may simply be fashion coming full circle, as it has before.

=== Activity ===

Work in a pair or a small group. Choose one item that people once thought was out of date, such as a type of shoe, a hairstyle or a hat, and invent a short story about how it became popular again. Decide who started the trend, what changed and why people liked it. Then name one fact you would need before you could say that the trend was real. Nobody needs to wear anything, take photos or share personal details.
