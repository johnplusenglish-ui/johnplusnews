=== B1 ===

For millions of people in Lagos, Nigeria, getting to work can be the hardest part of the day. Traffic is heavy, buses can be crowded and passengers may spend a long time waiting at the side of the road. For Damilola Olokesusi, there was also a more frightening reason to think about transport.

Her sister had a terrible experience involving criminals who were pretending to run a bus service. The incident helped convince Damilola that commuters needed safer and more reliable options. She began working on a business idea that eventually became Shuttlers, a company providing organised shared bus journeys.

Shuttlers started in 2015. Instead of hoping that the next available bus would turn up and go to the right place, customers could book a seat on a planned route. As the service grew, technology helped passengers check their journey and find out more about their transport before they set off. A regular seat on a regular route meant that people could plan their morning properly.

The early days were not easy. Setting up a transport company takes money, vehicles, drivers and customers who trust the service. Damilola and her colleagues had to persuade people that shared transport could be comfortable, dependable and useful for everyday journeys. Many commuters had simply put up with the old system for years.

Over time, the company grew and began serving organisations as well as individual passengers. A bus carrying a group of people can also cut down the number of private cars on the road. Even so, the traffic problems of a city as large as Lagos cannot be solved by one business, and a paid service will always be out of reach for some people.

The story is not about a single person magically making a whole city safe. It is about noticing a serious problem and trying to build a practical answer to it. A frightening experience did not have a happy ending in itself, but it helped inspire a service designed to make other people's journeys better.

=== B2 ===

The daily commute in Lagos can be unpredictable. Passengers put up with crowded roads, unreliable journey times and worries about personal safety. Nigerian entrepreneur Damilola Olokesusi decided that this familiar frustration could become the starting point for a different kind of transport service. If a city makes the same journey difficult every single morning, there is a market in making it ordinary.

Her motivation was sharpened by a frightening incident involving her sister and criminals operating a fake bus service. Rather than writing off dangerous and inconvenient transport as inevitable, Damilola helped set up Shuttlers in 2015. The business set out to offer organised shared journeys with clearer routes and a more dependable booking process. The idea was not a glamorous one. It was a bus that turned up when it said it would.

In its early stages, the company relied on simple methods to keep in touch with customers. As it developed, a dedicated app made it easier to reserve a seat and follow scheduled services. The aim was to combine some of the convenience people associate with private transport with the lower cost and greater efficiency of sharing a vehicle. Passengers who knew when their bus would leave could plan the rest of the day around it.

Creating that system called for far more than a useful idea. Shuttlers needed drivers, vehicles, financing and customers willing to take a chance on an unfamiliar company. It also had to operate in a city where severe congestion can throw out even a carefully planned timetable. Trust, in particular, had to be built up journey by journey. Over time, the business took on corporate customers and attracted investment.

A successful shared transport service can potentially improve safety and cut down the number of separate cars carrying commuters along the same routes. But no single company can deal with every source of congestion, and private services are likely to be too expensive for some residents. Public transport planning remains a far broader responsibility. Roads, policing and planning rules lie outside the control of any operator, however well it is run.

What makes Damilola's story compelling is the link between personal experience and practical problem-solving. People often talk about the need for safer cities in general terms, and the conversation tends to stop there. She and her colleagues set about building one specific alternative instead, showing how frustration with an everyday system can turn into the beginning of a business. That shift, from complaining about a service to running an alternative, is what makes the story worth telling.

=== C1-C2 ===

In Lagos, commuting is not merely a matter of getting from one address to another. It involves negotiating congestion, unpredictable journey times and, for some passengers, well-founded doubts about the reliability and safety of whatever transport happens to be available. For the entrepreneur Damilola Olokesusi, these pressures became the foundation of a business aimed at making shared journeys more predictable. Few urban irritations are as widely shared, or as rarely treated as a commercial opportunity.

Her motivation was shaped partly by a serious incident involving her sister and criminals posing as transport operators. The experience sharpened an existing concern about the city's travel conditions and gave it a personal edge that abstract complaint rarely carries. In 2015, Damilola and fellow founders began developing what became Shuttlers, an organised shared mobility service built around scheduled routes and advance booking. Many businesses begin with a market analysis; this one began with a phone call nobody wants to receive.

The company's development illustrates the unglamorous complexity of turning a social problem into a viable commercial operation. Early communication relied on whatever tools were readily to hand, while later technology allowed passengers to book seats more conveniently. Yet software alone could not lay on vehicles, recruit trustworthy drivers or guarantee that journeys would escape Lagos's notorious traffic. The hardest part of the proposition was never the app but the promise behind it: that a bus would arrive, and that boarding it would be safe. Reliability, unlike an interface, cannot be released in a software update.

Shuttlers gradually took on business customers and attracted investment, positioning itself between conventional public transport and considerably more expensive private rides. By allowing multiple commuters to share a vehicle, the model offers potential gains in convenience and road efficiency, and perhaps in the peace of mind of passengers who had previously taken their chances. Those benefits, however, hinge on route demand, affordability and the availability of reliable infrastructure. Pricing, in a city of enormous income disparities, is itself a design decision with social consequences.

It would be misleading to present the business as a complete answer to insecurity or congestion. Lagos's transport difficulties are structural, bound up with urban planning, public investment and economic inequality, and none of those yields to a booking system. A commercially operated service will always reach some passengers more readily than others, and those it reaches last tend to be the ones with the fewest alternatives. Acknowledging that limit is less a criticism of the venture than a description of its scope.

The venture is nevertheless an instructive example of entrepreneurship driven by lived experience. Rather than treating a frightening episode as a story complete in itself, Damilola helped translate a concern into a service that could be tested, refined and scaled up. Its wider significance lies in the suggestion that familiar urban problems may conceal opportunities for practical innovation, even where solving them turns out to be considerably harder than naming them. The harder question, as ever, is what becomes of the commuters a successful business cannot profitably serve.
