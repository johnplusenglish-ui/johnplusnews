=== Core ===

For millions of people in Lagos, Nigeria, getting to work can be a difficult part of the day. Traffic is heavy, buses can be crowded and passengers may spend a long time waiting. For Damilola Olokesusi, there was also a more frightening reason to think about transport.

Her sister had a terrible experience involving criminals pretending to run a bus service. The incident helped convince Damilola that commuters needed safer and more reliable options. She began working on a business idea that eventually became Shuttlers, a company providing organised shared bus journeys.

Shuttlers started in 2015. Instead of hoping that the next available bus would go to the right place, customers could book seats on planned routes. As the service developed, technology helped passengers check journeys and know more about their transport before they travelled.

The early days were not easy. Building a transport company takes money, vehicles, drivers and customers who trust the service. Damilola and her colleagues had to persuade people that shared transport could be comfortable, dependable and useful for everyday journeys.

Over time, the company grew and began serving organisations as well as individual passengers. A bus carrying a group of people can also reduce the number of private cars on the road, although traffic problems in a city as large as Lagos cannot be solved by one business.

The story is not about a single person magically making a whole city safe. It is about noticing a serious problem and trying to build a practical response. A frightening experience did not have a happy ending in itself, but it helped inspire a service designed to make other people's journeys better.

=== Intermediate ===

The daily commute in Lagos can be unpredictable. Passengers face crowded roads, unreliable journey times and concerns about personal safety. Nigerian entrepreneur Damilola Olokesusi decided that this familiar frustration could become the starting point for a different kind of transport service.

Her motivation was sharpened by a frightening incident involving her sister and criminals operating a fake bus service. Rather than accepting dangerous and inconvenient transport as inevitable, Damilola helped establish Shuttlers in 2015. The business set out to offer organised shared journeys with clearer routes and a more dependable booking process.

In its early stages, the company relied on simple methods to communicate with customers. As it developed, a dedicated app made it easier to reserve seats and follow scheduled services. The aim was to combine some of the convenience associated with private transport with the lower cost and greater efficiency of sharing a vehicle.

Creating that system required more than a useful idea. Shuttlers needed drivers, vehicles, financing and customers willing to trust an unfamiliar company. It also had to operate in a city where severe congestion can affect even a carefully planned timetable. Over time, the business attracted corporate customers and investment.

A successful shared transport service can potentially improve safety and reduce the number of separate cars carrying commuters along the same routes. But no single company can address every source of congestion, and private services may be too expensive for some residents. Public transport planning remains a broader responsibility.

What makes Damilola's story compelling is the connection between personal experience and practical problem-solving. People often talk about the need for safer cities in general terms. She and her colleagues attempted to build one specific alternative, showing how frustration with an everyday system can become the beginning of a business.

=== Advanced ===

In Lagos, commuting is not merely a matter of moving from one address to another. It involves navigating congestion, unpredictable journey times and, for some passengers, concerns about the reliability and safety of available transport. For entrepreneur Damilola Olokesusi, these pressures became the foundation of a business aimed at making shared journeys more predictable.

Her motivation was shaped partly by a serious incident involving her sister and criminals posing as transport operators. The experience sharpened an existing concern about the city's travel conditions. In 2015, Damilola and fellow founders began developing what became Shuttlers, an organised shared mobility service built around scheduled routes and advance booking.

The company's development illustrates the unglamorous complexity of turning a social problem into a viable commercial operation. Early communication relied on readily available tools, while later technology allowed passengers to book seats more conveniently. Yet software alone could not supply vehicles, recruit trustworthy drivers or guarantee that journeys would escape Lagos's notorious traffic.

Shuttlers gradually attracted business customers and investment, positioning itself between conventional public transport and more expensive private rides. By allowing multiple commuters to share vehicles, the model offers potential gains in convenience and road efficiency. These benefits, however, depend on factors such as route demand, affordability and the availability of reliable infrastructure.

It would be misleading to portray the business as a complete solution to insecurity or congestion. Lagos's transport challenges are structural, involving urban planning, public investment and economic inequality. A commercially operated service inevitably reaches some passengers more easily than others.

The venture is nevertheless an instructive example of entrepreneurship driven by lived experience. Instead of treating a frightening episode as a complete narrative in itself, Damilola helped translate a concern into a service that could be tested, improved and expanded. Its wider significance lies in the possibility that familiar urban problems may contain opportunities for practical innovation, even when solving them proves far harder than identifying them.
