title: A Dinosaur Sold for $47 Million
summary: A rare skeleton called Jodi was sold at auction in New York. Scientists are worried about who bought it.

In 2019, a woman called Jodi Pierson was looking at the ground on her own land in Montana, in the United States, when she found some bones. They turned out to belong to a dinosaur that died about 67 million years ago.

Last week, that skeleton was sold at an auction house in New York. It is named “Jodi”, after the person who found it. Six people competed for it, by phone and online, for twelve minutes. The final price was $47.3 million. The auction house had expected somewhere between $6 million and $9 million.

Jodi is a Nanotyrannus, a meat-eating dinosaur related to Tyrannosaurus rex but much smaller. The skeleton is about six metres long and has roughly 191 pieces of fossil bone. For a long time some scientists thought animals like this were young T. rex. The auction house says Nanotyrannus is now accepted as a separate kind of dinosaur, with more than 150 differences in its bones. Only three good Nanotyrannus skeletons are known to science.

The land was Pierson's own, which is why she was allowed to sell what she found. In the United States, fossils discovered on private land belong to the owner of that land. She will not receive the full $47.3 million, because fees and costs are taken out of an auction price before the seller is paid.

So why are scientists unhappy? Nobody knows who bought it. The buyer has not been named.

Thomas Holtz, a dinosaur specialist at the University of Maryland, pointed out that some American museums are closing because they cannot afford to stay open, and that the money they need is a tenth of what Jodi cost. He also said that digging up, cleaning and building a skeleton like this probably costs about a million dollars at most.

His bigger worry is access. If the new owner gives Jodi to a museum, the public can see it and scientists can study it for generations. If it stands in a rich person's house, nobody else can measure the bones or scan them. In that case, he said, it is almost as though the skeleton had been left in the ground.

Linda Ivany, a professor at Syracuse University, made a simpler point. These fossils, she said, should stay somewhere that children can be amazed by them.

The bidding lasted twelve minutes. Six buyers competed by telephone and online, and when it ended, a dinosaur skeleton had sold for $47.3 million, making it what Sotheby's called the second most valuable dinosaur ever sold at auction. The auction house's own estimate had been $6 million to $9 million.

The skeleton is called “Jodi”, after Jodi Pierson, who found the remains in 2019 on privately owned land in the Hell Creek Formation in Garfield County, Montana. According to Sotheby's it measures around 19.6 feet from nose to tail, stands about 9.6 feet on its mount and comprises roughly 191 fossil bone elements. The size and the development of the bones indicate an adult animal.

Jodi is a Nanotyrannus, described by the auction house as a fast, highly specialised carnivore that lived alongside Tyrannosaurus rex at the end of the Cretaceous, about 67 million years ago. Its identity has a complicated history. Some researchers long argued that such specimens were adolescent T. rex rather than a separate animal. Sotheby's states that the genus was confirmed last autumn and that more than 150 differences in bone structure distinguish it from its larger relative. Jodi is one of only three substantial Nanotyrannus skeletons known to science, and the first to reach the market since that confirmation.

There is a legal point worth noting here, because it explains why the sale could happen at all. The remains were on Pierson's own land, and in the United States fossils found on private property belong to the landowner. It is also worth separating the headline from the payment: an auction price is not what the seller receives, since fees and costs are deducted first.

A record price for a rare fossil might sound like good news for palaeontology. Several scientists disagree, and their objections are practical rather than sentimental.

Thomas R Holtz Jr, a tyrannosaur researcher at the University of Maryland, noted that while the sale was taking place, one American natural history museum was closing and another was under threat, over shortfalls amounting to roughly a tenth of Jodi's price. He estimated that excavating, preparing and mounting a skeleton like this costs around a million dollars at most, so a well-funded museum could assemble several good specimens for the price of this one.

His central concern is access. The buyer has not been publicly identified. If the skeleton is donated to an institution, researchers can measure, sample and scan the bones, including with techniques nobody has invented yet. If it ends up decorating a private house, Holtz argued, it is effectively lost to science and the public, no different from being left in the ground.

Linda Ivany, a professor of evolutionary palaeoecology at Syracuse University, put the case for public ownership more plainly to the New York Times: fossils like this should remain where scientists can study them and children can be astonished by them.

title: What a Record Price Buys, and What It Costs
summary: A Nanotyrannus skeleton fetched $47.3m in New York. The interesting question is not what it is worth, but what ownership of a scientific object means.

Sotheby's estimated the skeleton would make between $6 million and $9 million. Twelve minutes and six bidders later it sold for $47.3 million, which the auction house describes as the second highest price ever paid for a dinosaur. Nothing about the specimen changed during those twelve minutes, which is a reasonable summary of what an auction measures.

The object itself is genuinely rare. Found in 2019 by Jodi Pierson on her own land in the Hell Creek Formation of Garfield County, Montana, and named after her, it comprises some 191 fossil bone elements, runs about 19.6 feet from nose to tail and belonged to an adult animal. It is a Nanotyrannus, a smaller and faster tyrannosaur that shared the late Cretaceous with T. rex around 67 million years ago, and one of only three substantial skeletons of its kind known to science.

Its classification deserves a careful sentence. For decades some palaeontologists argued that specimens of this type were juvenile T. rex rather than a distinct genus. Sotheby's position is that the genus was confirmed last autumn and that over 150 morphological differences separate the two. That is the auction house's account of a scientific question, offered while selling the object it describes, which is a reason to attribute it rather than simply repeat it.

The criticism from researchers is not about money as such. Thomas R Holtz Jr of the University of Maryland made two arithmetical points. Excavating, preparing and mounting a skeleton of this kind costs perhaps a million dollars at the outside, so the price represents a trophy premium rather than the cost of the science. Meanwhile American institutions holding and studying such material are closing or under threat over sums roughly a tenth of what Jodi fetched, so a buyer who genuinely cared about palaeontology could have bought vastly more of it.

The sharper argument concerns access, and it turns on a distinction the market cannot represent. A fossil is simultaneously property and evidence. As property it can be owned, displayed and resold. As evidence it is only useful if it can be measured, sampled, scanned and re-examined by people who have not been born yet, using methods not yet invented. Holtz's conclusion is bleak and logical: a specimen in a private lobby, inaccessible to researchers, is for scientific purposes no different from a specimen still in the rock. Linda Ivany of Syracuse University framed the positive version, arguing that such fossils belong where scientists can study them and the public can marvel at them.

One detail keeps the picture honest. The skeleton was found on private land, which under United States law is what made the sale legal, and the proceeds are not the hammer price: fees and costs come out before any seller sees it. The buyer remains unidentified and may yet lend or donate the specimen, which is the outcome every scientist quoted was hoping for. Until then, a rare animal that survived 67 million years in the ground has an owner nobody can name.
