=== Core ===

title: Even Chefs Have Fussy Children
summary: Nine professional cooks were asked what their own children eat at home. The answers are more comforting than you might expect.

If you have ever cooked something carefully and watched a child refuse it, here is some good news. It happens to professional chefs too.

The Guardian asked nine cooks and food writers what they feed their own families. Almost all of them described a child who would not eat something. “I'm fairly certain every child is a picky eater,” said the TV chef Tom Kerridge, whose son Acey is ten. He does not believe the parents who say their children happily eat beetroot and feta salad.

Several of them said the same thing about timing. Kerridge tries to put food in front of his son before he realises he is hungry. If he waits, Acey asks for whatever comes in the brightest packet. If he puts a chopped apple on the table, it gets eaten.

Another common idea was letting the child help. Kerridge gets his son to build his own pizza or thread peppers on to kebabs. “If he's involved, he's more likely to eat it,” he said. Marie Mitchell, who has a five-year-old, puts it a different way. She and her partner decide what goes on the table, but once it is there, it is up to her daughter.

Two of the cooks said they do not hide vegetables in food. Helen Goh grates courgette into pasta sauce, but she always tells her sons it is in there. “I don't want food to become a trick,” she said. Mitchell agreed: if the child never knows she is eating something, she will never learn that she likes it.

The pastry chef Ravneet Gill has a different rule, about sugar. She does not call it a treat. Her son knows he is allowed it, she says, so he does not long for it.

Jackson Boxer has four children, and he had to learn not to take refusals personally. Saying no to food, he realised, is one of the few kinds of power a child has. Rosie Birkett says the same thing in a gentler way: for most children, being fussy is only a phase.

Perhaps the most useful comment came from Meera Sodha, who was called a fussy eater herself as a child. Her advice is not to label children. “If you keep calling them fussy, they start believing that's who they are.” She used to ask her daughter to try one cherry tomato a year. Recently, she ate three.

summary: Nine professional cooks were asked how they feed their own families. Almost all of them described a fussy child, and none of them had a trick.

There is a particular kind of defeat in cooking something properly and watching a four-year-old push it away. It is reassuring, then, that it happens to people who cook for a living. The Guardian's Mina Holland asked nine chefs and food writers what their own children eat at home, and the honesty of the answers is the most striking thing about them.

Tom Kerridge, who runs five restaurants, begins by dismissing the competition. “I'm fairly certain every child is a picky eater,” he says, and he does not believe parents who claim their children happily eat beetroot and feta salads. His ten-year-old son, Acey, trains hard and burns energy constantly, so Kerridge's strategy is to get food in front of him before hunger turns into a request for whatever comes in the brightest wrapper.

A theme runs through several of the accounts: involvement. Kerridge gets Acey to thread peppers on to kebabs or build his own pizza, on the principle that a child who helped make something is more likely to eat it. Marie Mitchell, author of the Caribbean cookbook Kin, describes a version of the same idea as giving her five-year-old agency. The adults decide what goes on the table; what she does with it is her own business.

Two of them push back firmly on a popular tactic. Helen Goh cooks onions, carrots and celery into a base and grates courgette until it melts away, but she tells her sons exactly what is in it. “I don't want food to become a trick,” she says. Mitchell takes the same line, with a practical argument attached: if a child does not know she is eating something, she will never discover that she likes it.

The accounts also differ, which is worth noticing. Ravneet Gill deliberately refuses to frame sugar as a treat, believing that is where things went wrong for her as a child. Anna Jones admits she cannot bring herself to send a child to bed hungry, so she does make alternatives, while Mitchell and Rosie Birkett mostly do not. Jackson Boxer, who has four children, offers the most consoling observation of all: refusing food, he realised, is one of the few forms of power children have, which is why it should not be taken personally.

What almost nobody offers is a method. Birkett says pickiness is nearly always a phase and that what matters is the pattern over weeks and months. Margot Henderson, whose children are now in their late twenties and thirties, took the view that missing one meal would not harm them. And Meera Sodha, labelled fussy herself as a child, gives the one piece of advice that costs nothing: do not call them fussy, because they will believe you.

=== Advanced ===

title: The Problem Is Not Cooking. It Is Negotiation.
summary: Asked how they feed their own children, nine chefs described almost no cooking problems at all. What they described were problems of power, patience and ego.

Ask a professional cook how to make a child eat vegetables and you might expect technique. What nine chefs and food writers actually told the Guardian, in a feature by Mina Holland, is that technique is close to irrelevant. The difficulty they describe is social, and most of their hard-won insight concerns their own behaviour rather than their children's.

Tom Kerridge sets the tone by refusing the premise that some children are simply good eaters. He is fairly certain, he says, that every child is picky, and does not believe the reports of ten-year-olds enjoying beetroot and feta. His own son eats well, but the account of how is mostly logistics: feed him before he knows he is hungry.

The most quoted strategies are about handing over control. Kerridge gets his son building pizzas and threading kebabs, on the reasonable theory that participation changes the relationship to the plate. Marie Mitchell describes the boundary precisely: the adults decide what goes on the table, and the child decides what happens next. Anna Jones talks about ownership too, noting that her eldest only began eating widely after Japanese food entered his imagination through stories about ninjas. Appetite, in other words, has a cultural component long before it has a nutritional one.

Where they disagree is more instructive than where they agree. Hiding vegetables, a tactic many parents rely on, is rejected by two of them on grounds that are essentially ethical. Helen Goh grates courgette into a sauce and then says so, because she does not want food to become a trick; Mitchell's objection is epistemic, since a child who never knows what she ate cannot learn what she likes. Ravneet Gill declines to call sugar a treat, having concluded that the category itself created her own childhood problem. Jones, by contrast, cannot send a child to bed hungry and says so plainly, which is a more useful admission than a rule.

The deepest observation comes from Jackson Boxer, who feeds four children with barely overlapping preferences. He used to take refusal personally, and then understood that refusing food is one of the very few forms of power available to a child. In a household where cooking matters, he adds, they know precisely where the blade goes in. Rosie Birkett arrives at the same place from the other direction: as a food writer she kept making food exciting while her children wanted the opposite, and she had to separate her ego from what they were asking for.

None of this is dietary advice, and it should not be mistaken for it. These are nine individual households, several of them unusually food-literate and none of them a sample. What they offer instead is a correction to the idea that fussy eating is a failure of cooking. Margot Henderson, looking back on three grown children, remembers deciding that a missed meal would not starve anyone. Meera Sodha's version is the one most likely to be useful to a tired parent: stop calling the child fussy, because they are listening, and they will take you at your word.
