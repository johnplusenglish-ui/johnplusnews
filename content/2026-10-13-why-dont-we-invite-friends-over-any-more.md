=== Intermediate ===

When was the last time you invited a friend over for dinner? Not for a special occasion, but simply to spend time together, have something to eat and enjoy a conversation. For many people, it has become surprisingly unusual.

According to research highlighted in a recent Guardian article, the number of Americans who regularly invite friends or family into their homes has fallen dramatically over the past 50 years. In Britain, a survey found that more than four in ten adults avoid hosting because they don't want to deal with the cleaning afterwards. But is cleaning really the problem?

One reason people hesitate to invite others over is the pressure to have a perfect home. Social media is full of beautiful kitchens, stylish furniture and carefully decorated dining tables. Ordinary homes, with washing hanging up and children's toys scattered across the floor, rarely look like that. As a result, some people feel embarrassed about inviting friends into their homes, even when their friends probably wouldn't care about the mess.

Preparing food can also be stressful. Guests may have different dietary requirements, and hosts often worry about whether everyone will enjoy the meal. What should be a relaxing evening can quickly turn into hours of cooking, cleaning and organising. Modern lifestyles have made things more complicated, too. People work long hours, live further apart and have busy schedules. Arranging a simple get-together often requires several messages and weeks of planning. In the past, friends and neighbours were more likely to pop round without making arrangements. Today, turning up unexpectedly at someone's door can almost seem rude.

There is another interesting explanation: people have become more comfortable spending time alone. With streaming services, social media and endless entertainment available at home, we can easily spend an entire evening by ourselves without feeling bored. However, being entertained isn't necessarily the same as feeling connected.

Experts warn that spending less time with friends in person can weaken our relationships and increase feelings of loneliness. Inviting someone into our home also allows them to see a more personal side of our lives. Of course, meeting at a restaurant has advantages. Nobody has to cook, clean or worry about guests staying too late. But eating at home can be cheaper, more relaxed and more personal.

Perhaps the solution is to stop treating every invitation like a special event. A few drinks, some simple food and a comfortable sofa might be enough. After all, most friends aren't interested in judging your furniture or checking whether your kitchen is spotless. They just want to spend time with you. And perhaps that's something worth remembering the next time we consider inviting someone round.

title: The Lost Art of Having People Round
summary: As dinner parties fall out of fashion and our homes become increasingly private spaces, have we forgotten one of the simplest ways to maintain friendships?

There was once something wonderfully unremarkable about dropping round to a friend's house. A cup of tea might turn into dinner, dinner into an evening of conversation, and nobody would think twice about the state of the kitchen. Today, the prospect of inviting someone over can feel like organising a minor diplomatic summit.

Menus must be considered, dietary requirements accommodated and living rooms made presentable. Even the most casual get-together risks becoming a carefully orchestrated performance, complete with elaborate food, stylish table settings and the nagging suspicion that one's home isn't quite good enough. The consequences are increasingly visible. Research cited by The Guardian suggests that regular home entertaining in the United States has declined sharply over the past half-century. A British survey, meanwhile, found that more than four in ten adults avoid hosting because of the cleaning involved.

But the decline reflects something more complicated than an unwillingness to wash up. One explanation is the growing influence of social media, which has quietly transformed our expectations of domestic life. Beautifully arranged dinner tables and immaculate interiors have become everyday images, making ordinary homes seem inadequate by comparison. When hospitality becomes a performance, spontaneity is often the first casualty.

There are practical obstacles, too. Friends increasingly live scattered across sprawling cities, while demanding jobs, childcare responsibilities and lengthy commutes leave little room for informal socialising. Anthropologist Robin Dunbar has suggested that geographical distance plays an important role in determining how regularly friendships are maintained. Even relatively short journeys can discourage spontaneous visits.

Meanwhile, our relationship with home has undergone a fundamental shift. For many people, particularly since the pandemic, home has become a sanctuary from an exhausting outside world. Inviting guests into that space can feel less like relaxation and more like another responsibility. Technology has arguably reinforced the change. Streaming platforms, gaming and social media provide endless entertainment without requiring us to leave the sofa or engage with another person.

Yet convenience comes at a potential social cost. Psychotherapists point out that allowing friends into our homes creates opportunities for a particular kind of intimacy. Unlike meeting in a café, hosting reveals something of our private lives, including their imperfections. There is vulnerability in allowing someone to see the washing-up piled beside the sink or the chaotic reality of family life. But that vulnerability may also help deepen a friendship.

Of course, nostalgia should not blind us to the disadvantages of entertaining. Hosting can be expensive, exhausting and disproportionately demanding for whoever does the cooking and cleaning. Nevertheless, there is something worth preserving in the simple act of welcoming others into our homes.

Perhaps the solution is not to resurrect the elaborate dinner party, but to abandon the expectation that hospitality must involve elaborate preparation. A takeaway, a bottle of wine and an untidy living room might be all that's required. Friendship, after all, rarely depends on perfectly folded napkins. It thrives when people feel comfortable enough to stop pretending that their lives are perfect.
