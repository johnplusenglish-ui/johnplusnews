title: What Does "Mogging" Mean?
summary: Collins has picked its word of the year. Teenagers use it as a joke, but it did not begin as one.

Imagine a family standing together for a photograph. Everyone smiles. At the last second the dog walks into the middle of the shot and looks straight at the camera. Somebody posts the picture and writes that the dog has mogged the entire family. Nobody is upset. The joke is that the dog got all the attention.

On 3 November 2026, Collins chose mogging as its word of the year. It means outshining or outperforming someone or something. Collins says the word is used about 210% more often this year than last, and that young people mostly use it in a light, joking way when somebody is upstaged. A brilliant goal, a very good coat, a dog in a photograph.

The word did not start there. It is short for AMOG, which stands for alpha male of the group. Around 2016 it was used in gyms for the most physically powerful man in the room. From there it moved into corners of the internet where some men ranked each other by looks, confidence and status. Being mogged meant being made to look smaller.

So the same word can do two very different jobs. A friend laughing about a dog is not doing what a website that scores faces is doing. Words change as new people pick them up, and most teenagers using it have no idea where it came from. That is normal. It is still useful to know.

Comparing ourselves with other people is nothing new. Somebody is always faster, funnier or more relaxed in a room full of strangers. A little of that is fun. It stops being fun when every photograph turns into a test of who looks best, because people are more than a face or a score.

There is also a difference between a joke everybody is in on and a joke about somebody who is not. If the person in the photograph is laughing too, it is teasing. If they are not, the word is doing something else, whatever it sounded like.

Collins also chose a shortlist of other new words, including ghost job, which is a job advertised by a company that is not actually hiring. Language keeps moving. Whether or not you use mogging yourself, you can still choose how you talk about the people around you.

Collins announced its word of the year on 3 November, and the winner was mogging. The dictionary defines it as outperforming or outshining someone or something else, and says usage rose by 210% over the year. Alex Beecroft, Collins' managing director, said younger speakers tend to use it playfully and ironically, for the moment when somebody is upstaged. A spectacular goal mogs the rest of the match. A dog mogs the family photograph.

Like most slang, its meaning depends almost entirely on who is speaking and to whom. And this particular word has travelled a long way to arrive at the dog.

Mogging is believed to be a shortening of AMOG, from alpha male of the group. It appears around 2016 as a bodybuilding term for the most physically dominant man in a gym, and from there it became a verb in the manosphere, the loose collection of online spaces where some men discuss status, dominance and attractiveness. In that setting, to mog somebody was to make them look inferior in appearance, confidence or standing. The teasing was not affectionate.

That history has not vanished simply because the word is now funny. Some appearance-focused communities still encourage users to score faces and study individual features in detail. Clinicians at Johns Hopkins All Children's Hospital have warned about the related trend of looksmaxxing, and Dr Jasmine Reese, who runs its adolescent clinic, has described a sharp rise in teenagers and even pre-teens presenting with eating disorders and body dysmorphia, often after following restrictive advice found online. Some apps use artificial intelligence to give a face a rating.

The two ideas are related but they are not the same. Looksmaxxing is about trying to improve how you look. Mogging is about outshining somebody else. One can exist perfectly happily without the other.

None of this means that everyone using the word shares the beliefs of the people who coined it. The great majority do not, and a comment about a dog is just a comment about a dog. The more useful question is what happens when comparison runs constantly in the background. Celebrating somebody who has done something brilliantly is enjoyable. Being sorted into winners and losers by appearance, repeatedly, is a different experience.

Collins reaches its choice by tracking how often words turn up across a very large collection of written and spoken English, which is why a sharp rise matters more than whether a word is new. Mogging has been in circulation for roughly a decade, quietly, until this year.

It is worth saying that Collins and Dictionary.com made separate choices this year, and there is no single official word of the year. Collins also shortlisted firewave, ghost job and carspreading, which is the habit of carmakers building every new model longer, wider and heavier than the last.

=== Advanced ===

title: From the Manosphere to a Joke About Your Dog
summary: Collins' word of the year has changed hands several times. What happens when the vocabulary of ranking people becomes a punchline?

Collins named mogging its word of the year on 3 November 2026, defining it as outperforming or outshining someone or something else, and reporting that its use rose by 210% across the year. The dictionary notes that Generation Z and Generation Alpha tend to deploy it playfully and ironically, for the moment somebody is upstaged. A dog can mog a family photograph. A teenager can mog an elite athlete, which is the sort of claim the word exists to make.

The route it took to get there is the interesting part. Mogging is thought to be a shortening of AMOG, alpha male of the group. It surfaces around 2016 as gym vocabulary for the most physically dominant man present, then hardens into a verb inside the manosphere, where to mog someone was to make them visibly inferior in appearance, confidence or status. It is difficult to imagine a less playful origin, and about a decade separates that usage from the dog.

Comparison itself is not an invention of the internet. People have always registered who runs faster, performs better or walks into a room more easily, and slang gives those observations a convenient shorthand. Teasing between friends is harmless when everybody is enjoying it. A system that sorts people by their faces is a different proposition, and the word has lived in both.

The clinical concern attaches to the neighbouring trend rather than to the joke. Johns Hopkins All Children's Hospital has warned about looksmaxxing, and Dr Jasmine Reese, director of its adolescent clinic, reports a substantial rise in teenagers and pre-teens with eating disorders and body dysmorphia, frequently after following restrictive regimes learned online. Some apps score a jawline or cheekbones with artificial intelligence. The distinction is worth keeping: looksmaxxing concerns altering your own appearance, while mogging concerns outdoing somebody else's.

It does not follow that a teenager saying the word endorses any of this. Words detach from their origins routinely, and most speakers inherit a term without its history. Some newer uses are explicitly mocking the business of ranking people, which is a reasonable fate for a word that began by taking it seriously.

There is also the familiar complaint that young people are wrecking English, which this particular word complicates nicely. Mogging was not coined by teenagers. It was coined by grown men in gyms and forums, and teenagers are the ones who took the teeth out of it. Slang travels upward at least as often as it travels down.

What a word of the year really records is attention rather than approval. Collins' shortlist this year included firewave, for a run of urban wildfires in quick succession, and P(doom), a percentage estimate of how likely someone considers it that artificial intelligence ends civilisation. Dictionary.com, separately, chose maxxing. There is no single official verdict, only several organisations noticing what people said a great deal.

The question mogging leaves behind is not whether the word should exist. It is what a speaker is doing with it: admiring a performance, laughing with a friend, or entering somebody into a competition they never agreed to join.
