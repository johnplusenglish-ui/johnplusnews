=== Intermediate ===

For most of his life, Peter Lloyd had a secret. Although he had a job, a wife and a family, he could barely read or write. Peter grew up in Lancashire, England. He was born partially deaf, which made learning at school extremely difficult. He often struggled to hear his teachers and concentrate in noisy classrooms.

He was moved to a special school when he was six, but things didn't improve. By the time he left school at 16, he had no qualifications and very limited reading skills. However, Peter found ways to manage everyday life. He became a lorry driver in his early twenties, learning to remember roads, numbers and routes without reading maps. Later, satellite navigation made his job easier. His wife also helped whenever he needed to read letters or complete forms.

For years, he managed to get by. But everything changed during the Covid lockdowns in 2021. One day, Peter went to the supermarket to buy milk suitable for his wife and son, who both had food allergies. Standing in front of the shelves, he realised he couldn't read the labels properly. He was frightened of buying the wrong product and making his family ill.

When he returned home, he finally admitted how much he was struggling. His wife encouraged him to contact Read Easy, a charity that helps adults improve their reading skills. Peter was nervous. He had tried learning before and had been disappointed. But a volunteer teacher called Alison helped him feel comfortable and encouraged him to start again.

They met twice a week, working slowly through basic reading exercises. Peter gradually became more confident, and something remarkable happened: he began to enjoy reading. He set himself a special goal. He wanted to finish the first Harry Potter book, a story he had always been interested in. After three years of lessons, he achieved it.

For the first time, reading was not simply a difficult task. It was something that brought him pleasure. Today, Peter can read recipes, enjoy books and take part in activities with his son that were previously difficult. He has also become an ambassador for Read Easy, encouraging others to ask for help.

His story is important because literacy problems are more common than many people realise. According to the National Literacy Trust, around 18% of adults aged 16 to 65 in England have very poor literacy skills. For some, embarrassment prevents them from seeking support. Peter hopes his experience will encourage people to overcome that fear. He now wants to take a GCSE in English and build a career helping others. His experience is a reminder that education doesn't have to end when we leave school. Sometimes, a new chapter can begin much later in life.

=== Advanced ===

title: The Man Who Couldn't Read: Why It's Never Too Late to Start Again
summary: For more than four decades, Peter Lloyd navigated a world of written instructions, road signs and official documents without being able to read. His remarkable journey towards literacy offers a powerful reminder of how easily adults can fall through the cracks of the education system.

For most people, buying a carton of milk is an unremarkable part of everyday life. For Peter Lloyd, however, a trip to the supermarket during the Covid lockdowns became a turning point. Unable to read the labels on products that needed to be safe for his allergic wife and son, the 42-year-old lorry driver suddenly found himself overwhelmed by a problem he had spent his entire adult life trying to hide. Peter could barely read or write.

Born partially deaf in Lancashire, he had struggled throughout childhood to follow lessons in noisy classrooms. His difficulties were compounded by a school system that failed to provide the support he needed. By the age of 16, he had left education without qualifications or the basic literacy skills that most people take for granted. Yet he had somehow managed to build an independent life.

Blessed with a remarkable visual memory, Peter learnt to navigate Britain's roads by remembering routes, landmarks and motorway numbers. He qualified as a lorry driver, supported himself financially and eventually started a family. Later, satellite navigation and his wife's assistance helped him negotiate the increasingly text-dependent demands of modern life. But independence came at a considerable psychological cost.

Even ordinary tasks could require elaborate preparation, while the fear of embarrassment discouraged him from seeking help. Previous attempts at adult education had ended in disappointment, reinforcing his belief that reading might remain permanently beyond his reach. The supermarket incident finally prompted him to reconsider. Encouraged by his wife, Peter contacted Read Easy, a charity providing individual reading support to adults. He was paired with volunteer coach Alison Hollinrake, whose patient, structured approach allowed him to rebuild the foundations he had never properly acquired.

Twice a week, they worked together, gradually replacing years of frustration with confidence. Peter's ambition was surprisingly modest: to read the first Harry Potter novel. The book appealed to him partly because its central character, an outsider struggling to find his place in the world, reflected aspects of his own childhood. After three years of determined effort, Peter finally completed it.

His achievement offers a window into a frequently overlooked social problem. According to the National Literacy Trust, approximately 6.6 million adults aged 16 to 65 in England have very poor literacy skills. Although this does not mean they are all unable to read, many struggle with unfamiliar texts, written instructions and everyday information. The consequences extend far beyond education. Limited literacy can restrict employment opportunities, undermine confidence and complicate everything from healthcare decisions to helping children with homework.

There is also a persistent stigma surrounding adult learning. People who struggle with reading may fear being regarded as unintelligent, despite literacy difficulties arising from circumstances that have little to do with intellectual ability. Peter's experience challenges that assumption. Now an ambassador for Read Easy, he encourages other adults to overcome the embarrassment that once kept him silent. He also hopes to gain a GCSE in English and eventually pursue work supporting people facing similar difficulties.

His story is not simply about learning to recognise words on a page. It is about discovering opportunities, confidence and independence that had previously seemed unattainable. And perhaps, above all, it demonstrates that the most transformative lessons do not always take place in childhood.

=== Before you read ===

- How did you learn to read? What do you remember about it?
- Do you know anyone who finds reading or writing very difficult? How do they manage?
- What would be the hardest part of daily life if you could not read?

=== Talk about it ===

- Why do you think Peter kept his difficulty secret for so long?
- Peter's goal was to read one Harry Potter book. Why can a small, personal goal be so powerful?
- Is it ever too late to learn something new? What would you like to learn?
- Why do some adults feel ashamed to ask for help, and how can society make it easier?
- Who is responsible for making sure everyone can read: schools, families, the government, or the individual?
