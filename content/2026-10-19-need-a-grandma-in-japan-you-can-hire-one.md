=== Core ===

title: A Job for Grandmothers in Japan
summary: A company sends older women to cook, tidy, teach a skill or simply spend time with somebody. The service started in 2011.

What if you could hire a grandmother for an afternoon? In Japan, there is a real service that lets people do something very similar. Customers can ask an older woman to cook a meal, help organise their home, teach them a skill or simply spend time with them.

The service is called OK! Obaachan, which means something like "OK! Grandma". It started in 2011 and is run by a company called Client Partners. Not every worker is actually a grandmother, and the service is not only for people who feel lonely. Some customers just need practical help with a job they cannot manage on their own, and they want someone patient who has done it many times before.

One woman working for the service, Taeko Kaji, was 69 when she spoke to Australian journalists in 2025. She said she enjoyed going to different places and trying new experiences. For older workers, a job like this can bring in money, fill the day with activity and give them a chance to meet new people.

Japan has an ageing population, which means there are a lot of older people compared with younger ones, and many of them live alone. Some families also live far away from one another, so it is not easy to drop in for an hour. In this situation, a service that brings the generations together may be genuinely useful. A younger person can pick up a skill from somebody with years of experience, while the older worker may enjoy feeling needed again.

However, money changes the relationship. A paid visitor is doing a job, even if they are kind and friendly, and the visit ends when the time is up. It is important to be clear about what the service offers. Hiring somebody for company is not the same as having a family member who has known you all your life.

The idea may sound strange at first, but people already pay teachers, carers and guides for their knowledge and their time. Perhaps the most interesting question is not why anyone would hire a grandmother. It is why spending time with older people has become so difficult to arrange.

=== Intermediate ===

title: The Grandmother You Book by the Hour
summary: OK! Obaachan has matched customers with experienced older women since 2011. For the workers it can mean income, variety and a reason to leave the house.

Japan has an unusual answer to the problem of needing a calm, experienced person nearby: hire a grandmother. Through a service called OK! Obaachan, customers can arrange for an older woman to visit them, help with everyday tasks or pass on practical knowledge. The idea sounds like a comedy sketch, but the business has been running since 2011 and shows no sign of winding down.

The company behind the service, Client Partners, provides a wide range of personal assistance. Workers may cook, sort out belongings, offer guidance or accompany somebody on an outing. Some customers are looking for companionship, while others simply need a hand from someone patient and experienced who will not make them feel foolish. The women are paid professionals, and the company does not pretend they are substitutes for a customer's relatives.

In a 2025 Australian news interview, worker Taeko Kaji described enjoying the variety the job brings. For older people, employment of this kind can mean extra income as well as a sense of purpose. Work of this sort keeps people moving, out of the house and in regular contact with others, which matters when the alternative is a long and very quiet day. It also challenges the assumption that retirement should automatically involve stepping away from useful work, or that skills built up over decades stop being worth anything the moment somebody turns sixty-five.

The service has attracted attention partly because of Japan's ageing population and growing concerns about social isolation. Families may be separated by distance, demanding work schedules or personal circumstances, and keeping in touch takes effort that busy people do not always have. Meanwhile, younger adults rarely get the chance to spend meaningful time with anyone several decades older than themselves.

Critics might argue that companionship should never be turned into a product. There is an uncomfortable difference between being listened to by someone who chooses to be there and someone whose time is being paid for by the hour. Yet plenty of valuable relationships already involve professional boundaries, including those with teachers, counsellors and care workers, and few people regard those as fake. The discomfort many of us feel may say rather more about our expectations of friendship than about the service itself.

Perhaps the service is best understood not as friendship for sale but as a practical way of connecting people whose needs happen to fit together. Its success raises an awkward question for every society: if contact between the generations is genuinely valuable, why do we so often leave it to chance? Schools, workplaces and neighbourhoods rarely bring young and old together on purpose, so whatever contact does happen tends to be accidental.

=== Advanced ===

title: Rentable Grandmothers: Honest Terms for an Awkward Modern Need
summary: The arrangement is openly commercial, which may be its most honest feature. The harder question is why contact between the generations now has to be booked at all.

The expression "rent a grandma" sounds almost deliberately provocative. It suggests that something intimate and familiar, the presence of an older relative, has been repackaged as a commercial transaction and put on the market like any other service. The phrase invites a joke, and a good deal of international coverage has duly supplied one. Yet Japan's OK! Obaachan service turns out to be rather more complicated than its attention-grabbing description implies.

Set up by Client Partners in 2011, the service matches customers with women who can provide practical assistance, companionship and accumulated life experience. Their work might involve preparing food, helping with household tasks or accompanying a client on an outing. The relationship is explicitly professional, even when the experience feels warm and informal, and neither side pretends otherwise. What the customer is buying, in effect, is competence and patience, two qualities that are difficult to advertise and impossible to hurry.

Its appeal reflects several overlapping social changes. Japan has a large older population, while smaller households, demanding work schedules and geographical separation can make routine contact between the generations far less common than it once was. Some customers want help with a specific task. Others may value the reassuring presence of somebody older who is willing to listen without judgement, and who has probably encountered the same difficulty several times before. The two motives are not easily separated, and the company makes no attempt to separate them.

The arrangement can be beneficial to the women providing the service as well. In an Australian interview published in 2025, worker Taeko Kaji spoke positively about the activities and variety the role brought into her life. That is worth dwelling on, because it challenges a persistent assumption that older people should be regarded primarily as recipients of care rather than as skilled and independent contributors with something to offer. Framed that way, the business looks less like an admission of social failure than a modest correction to the way older workers are valued.

Nevertheless, paid companionship raises uncomfortable questions about authenticity and inequality. If emotional reassurance becomes another service to be bought, those of limited means may find themselves priced out of something that ought to be freely available. A professional visit cannot replicate the history, the mutual obligations and the sheer unpredictability that characterise an enduring family relationship, however skilled the visitor. There is a further risk that a commercial solution quietly relieves families, employers and public services of obligations they might otherwise be expected to meet.

But writing the business off as evidence of social decline would be too easy. People have always sought wisdom and support beyond their immediate families, falling back on neighbours, mentors and local institutions when relatives were unavailable or unsuitable. Perhaps what is genuinely new here is not the arrangement itself but the honesty of its financial terms. Japan's rentable grandmothers point to both the value of experience and the difficulty, in a busy and dispersed society, of creating ordinary places where the generations can meet without anybody having to make an appointment.
