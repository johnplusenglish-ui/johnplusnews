=== Core ===

What if you could hire a grandmother for an afternoon? In Japan, there is a real service that lets people do something similar. Customers can ask an older woman to cook, help organise their home, share a skill or simply spend time with them.

The service is called OK! Obaachan, which means something like "OK! Grandma". It started in 2011 and is run by a company called Client Partners. Not every worker is a grandmother, and the service is not only for people who feel lonely. Customers may need practical help, too.

One woman working for the service, Taeko Kaji, was 69 when she spoke to Australian journalists in 2025. She said she enjoyed going to different places and trying new experiences. For older workers, these jobs can provide money, activity and a chance to meet other people.

Japan has an ageing population, and many people live alone. Some families also live far away from one another. In this situation, a service that brings generations together may be useful. A younger person can learn something from an older person, while the older worker may enjoy feeling needed.

However, money changes the relationship. A paid visitor is doing a job, even if they are kind and friendly. It is important to be clear about what the service offers. Hiring somebody for company is not exactly the same as having a family member who knows you well.

The idea may sound strange at first, but people already pay teachers, carers and guides for their knowledge and time. Perhaps the most interesting question is not why someone would hire a grandmother. It is why spending time with older people can sometimes be so difficult to arrange.

=== Intermediate ===

Japan has an unusual answer to the problem of needing an experienced, reassuring person nearby: hire a grandmother. Through a service called OK! Obaachan, customers can arrange for an older woman to visit them, help with everyday tasks or share practical knowledge. The idea sounds like a comedy sketch, but the business has existed since 2011.

The company behind the service, Client Partners, provides a wide range of personal assistance. Workers may cook, organise belongings, offer guidance or accompany somebody on an outing. Some customers are looking for companionship, while others simply need help from someone patient and experienced. The women are paid professionals, not substitutes for a customer's relatives.

In a 2025 Australian news interview, worker Taeko Kaji described enjoying the variety the job brings. For older people, employment of this kind can mean extra income as well as a sense of purpose. It also challenges the idea that retirement should automatically mean stepping away from useful work.

The service has attracted attention partly because of Japan's ageing population and concerns about social isolation. Families may be separated by distance, work schedules or personal circumstances. Meanwhile, younger adults do not always have opportunities to spend meaningful time with people several decades older than themselves.

Critics might argue that companionship should never become a product. There is an uncomfortable difference between being listened to by someone who chooses to be there and someone whose time is being paid for. Yet many valuable relationships already involve professional boundaries, including those with teachers, counsellors and care workers.

Perhaps the service is best understood not as friendship for sale but as a practical way to connect people who have different needs. Its success raises a question for every society: if intergenerational contact is valuable, why do we often leave it to chance?

=== Advanced ===

The expression "rent a grandma" sounds almost deliberately provocative. It suggests that something intimate and familiar, the presence of an older relative, has been turned into a commercial transaction. Yet Japan's OK! Obaachan service is more complicated than its attention-grabbing description implies.

Established by Client Partners in 2011, the service matches customers with women who can provide practical assistance, companionship and accumulated life experience. Their work might include preparing food, helping with household tasks or accompanying a client on an outing. The relationship is explicitly professional, even when the experience feels warm and informal.

Its appeal reflects several overlapping social changes. Japan has a large older population, while smaller households, demanding work schedules and geographical separation can make routine contact between generations less common. Some customers want help with a specific task; others may value the reassuring presence of somebody older who is willing to listen without judgement.

The arrangement can be beneficial to the women providing the service as well. In an Australian interview published in 2025, worker Taeko Kaji spoke positively about the activities and variety the role brought into her life. This challenges a persistent assumption that older people should be regarded primarily as recipients of care rather than as skilled, independent contributors.

Nevertheless, paid companionship raises uncomfortable questions about authenticity and inequality. If emotional reassurance becomes another service, those with limited means may be excluded. A professional visit also cannot replicate the history, mutual obligations and unpredictability that characterise an enduring family relationship.

But dismissing the business as evidence of social decline may be too easy. People have always sought wisdom and support beyond their immediate families. Perhaps what is genuinely new is the honesty of the financial arrangement. Japan's rentable grandmothers reveal both the value of experience and the difficulty of creating ordinary spaces where generations can meet without needing an appointment.
