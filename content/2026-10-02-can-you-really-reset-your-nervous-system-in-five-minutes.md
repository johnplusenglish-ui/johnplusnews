title: Do You Need to Reset Your Nervous System?
summary: Videos online promise a quick reset for your nervous system. Experts say that is not how the body works, but some simple habits can help you calm down.

Videos online promise to "reset" your nervous system. They tell you to tap your hand, hum, take cold plunges or stand on a shaking plate. In October 2026, The Guardian asked experts whether any of this is true.

The experts agreed on one thing. There is no such thing as a nervous system reset, and you would not want one. One part of your nervous system controls things you do not think about, such as your heart rate and digestion. It has two main modes. The "fight or flight" mode gets you ready for action. The "rest and digest" mode slows down your heart and helps you recover.

Stress switches on the first mode, whether it is an urgent email or bad news. Your heart beats faster and you feel more alert. That is useful, because you need it to exercise and concentrate. The problem starts when you cannot calm down afterwards. Then you can feel "wired and tired", and experts say this may add to other health problems over time. A healthy system is not always calm. It changes when it needs to, and then recovers.

So the experts talk about flexibility, not resets. Dr Serena Lai, a psychiatrist in London, explains that slow breathing, about six times a minute, can quickly change your heart rate variability, which is the small change in the time between heartbeats. She suggests breathing in for about five seconds and out for about five, for five to ten minutes. In one study, a breathing exercise called cyclic sighing, practised for five minutes a day, improved people's mood over a month.

Other habits have support too. Regular exercise can help your system work better over time, but too much training without rest can have the opposite effect. Regular sleep times and time away from screens also help. Cold water is less clear. One expert says there is not enough good evidence that it gives real benefits for the nervous system. If you enjoy it, that is fine.

So, can you reset in five minutes? Not really, and there is no quick fix. But a few minutes of slow breathing may change some short-term measurements, and simple habits help your body recover. Try to keep your cool, and take big online promises with a pinch of salt. If you feel anxious or tired all the time, talk to a doctor.

=== Intermediate ===

Scroll through social media and you will find an industry built around one promise: that you can "reset" your nervous system. The suggestions range from tapping the side of your hand and humming to cold plunges, vibrating plates and spiky acupressure mats. Stress is out of date, apparently; the fashionable word is "dysregulated". In a feature published on 2 October 2026, The Guardian asked doctors and other experts whether any of this holds up.

Every expert agreed that there is no such thing as resetting the nervous system, and that nobody would want to. The nervous system is the control network linking the brain, spinal cord and nerves. Its autonomic branch handles automatic jobs such as heart rate, blood pressure and digestion, and it has two main parts. The sympathetic system produces the "fight or flight" response, while the parasympathetic system, known as "rest and digest", slows the heart and supports recovery.

Stress activates the first, whether the cause is an urgent email or a family bereavement: the heart speeds up, muscles tense and we become more alert. That response is useful, because we need it to exercise, concentrate and meet deadlines. The trouble begins when we struggle to wind down once the demand has gone, leaving us feeling "wired and tired" and, according to the experts, potentially contributing to inflammation and heart and metabolic problems. A healthy system, then, is not permanently calm. It is flexible.

The most dependable ways of building that flexibility are rather dull. Slow breathing has been shown to raise heart rate variability, the changing gap between heartbeats, which researchers often use as a window on the parasympathetic system. Dr Serena Lai, a London psychiatrist, points to research suggesting that around six slow breaths a minute is among the most effective ways of causing a short-term change, and she recommends breathing in for about five seconds and out for five, for five to ten minutes. In a separate month-long trial, five minutes a day of "cyclic sighing" improved mood.

Other habits come with evidence too. Regular aerobic exercise can improve how the system regulates itself in the long run, although athletes who overtrain show lower heart rate variability, so recovery matters. Consistent sleep and wake times, breaks from screens and time outdoors also help. Cold water is less clear. The body's first reaction to a cold plunge is a stress response, with calm following afterwards, and one neurosurgeon says there is not yet enough credible evidence of meaningful benefits for the nervous system. If you enjoy it and feel calmer, he adds, that is reason enough. Acupressure mats and consumer vagus nerve devices have thinner evidence.

So no, five minutes cannot reset anything, and no single trick is a magic bullet. But a few minutes of slow breathing can shift some short-term measurements, and ordinary habits help the body bounce back from stress. Anyone who feels persistently anxious or exhausted should talk to a doctor, because no breathing exercise replaces that.

title: There Is No Reset Button: What the Evidence Says About Calming the Nervous System
summary: A Guardian feature finds that the viral idea of a nervous system reset has no physiological basis, though slow breathing and ordinary habits have some support. Most of that support concerns short-term measurements, not proven health outcomes.

Social media has found a new villain in the "dysregulated" nervous system and a matching range of remedies: tapping, humming, gargling, cold plunges, vibrating plates and acupressure mats, all promoted as ways to reset the body to calm. In a feature published on 2 October 2026, The Guardian put the premise to doctors and other specialists, and none of them took it at face value.

Their objection was twofold. There is no such thing as resetting the nervous system, and nobody should want to, because the system's value lies in responding to circumstances. Its autonomic branch regulates heart rate, blood pressure and digestion through two main parts: the sympathetic system, which mobilises the body for a challenge, and the parasympathetic system, which conserves energy and supports recovery. Sympathetic activity rises under any stress, from an urgent email to a bereavement, and we depend on it to exercise, concentrate and meet deadlines.

The real difficulty arises when recovery lags behind demand. Failing to wind down can leave people feeling "wired and tired" and may, the specialists say, contribute to inflammation and cardiovascular and metabolic problems. The goal is therefore not permanent calm but flexibility: the ability to rise to a challenge and then return to baseline.

Slow breathing has the clearest support. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 223 studies, published in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, found that voluntary slow breathing was associated with higher vagally mediated heart rate variability during a session, immediately afterwards and after multi-session programmes. Dr Serena Lai, a London psychiatrist, cites research suggesting that about six diaphragmatic breaths a minute is among the most effective ways of producing a short-term change, and she suggests inhaling for about five seconds and exhaling for five, for five to ten minutes. One month-long trial found that five minutes a day of "cyclic sighing" improved mood. The measure involved, however, is a proxy for autonomic activity, and a better reading on it is not the same as a proven health benefit.

The remaining evidence is uneven. Regular aerobic exercise improves autonomic regulation over time, but overtraining is associated with reduced variability, so rest should not take a back seat. Cold immersion triggers a sympathetic response first, with a parasympathetic rebound afterwards, and neurosurgeon Dr Kevin Tracey says the credible evidence is not yet strong enough to show meaningful benefits for regulation, so the jury is still out, though he sees no reason to stop if you enjoy it and feel calmer. Implanted vagus nerve stimulation is an established treatment for epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression, yet independent clinical evidence for consumer devices is limited, which is not the same as proof that they fail. Evidence for acupressure mats is thinner still.

The honest answer to the headline, then, is that five minutes cannot reset anything, and that slow breathing may change some short-term measurements. The advice that survives scrutiny is unglamorous: regular sleep, movement, time outdoors, rest and a plan for stress. Anyone troubled by persistent anxiety or exhaustion should seek medical advice, because a breathing exercise is no substitute for it.
