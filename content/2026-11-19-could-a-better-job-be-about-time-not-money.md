=== Core ===

title: Time or Money at Work?
summary: A new survey says work-life balance is now the top reason people think about leaving a job, ahead of pay.

Imagine two job offers. One pays more, but you must stay in the office until six every evening. The other pays a little less, but you can choose when to start and finish. Which would you take? Many people still need the highest salary they can get. However, a new international survey suggests that time and freedom are becoming just as important when people think about changing jobs.

The employment company Adecco asked 40,500 workers in 36 countries about their working lives. Its report came out on 19 November 2026. For the first time in the seven years of this research, work-life balance was the main reason people said they might leave a job, ahead of pay. The results do not mean that money has stopped mattering. They show that workers are thinking more carefully about what a job takes away from their personal lives.

There are several reasons for this change. Some people travel a long way to work and spend hours on crowded trains. If they can work from home sometimes, or change their hours, they can fit work around their lives more easily. Flexible hours can also leave time for hobbies.

Employers see the problem differently. A restaurant cannot let every employee work from home, and a hospital needs staff at certain times. Even in offices, teams sometimes need to be together to solve problems. Managers may worry that too much freedom will make planning difficult. The challenge is to work out which jobs can offer flexibility and how to treat everyone fairly when some roles cannot.

There is also an important question about money. Someone with a comfortable salary may be happy to give up extra pay for more free time. Someone struggling to pay rent may have no choice. For that person, a higher salary could make life less stressful. It is easy to talk about balance, but not everyone has the same choices. A good job may look different depending on your situation.

The survey is a reminder that success at work is not only about climbing the career ladder. People also want enough energy to enjoy the rest of their day. Perhaps the best job is not the one with the biggest number on the payslip, but the one that lets you live well outside work. If you were offered more money or more time, which would make the biggest difference to you?

=== Intermediate ===

Imagine receiving two attractive job offers. The first comes with a generous salary, but you would have to travel to an office every day and follow a strict timetable. The second pays a little less, yet allows you to choose when and where you work. It might once have seemed obvious which offer was better. Now, a global survey suggests that many employees are starting to measure a good job in something other than money.

The recruitment company Adecco published its latest Global Workforce of the Future research on 19 November 2026. It questioned 40,500 workers across 36 countries and 21 industries during June and July. For the first time in the seven years of its annual survey, work-life balance overtook salary as the leading reason employees said they would consider leaving their employer. That finding does not suggest that wages are suddenly unimportant. Rather, it indicates that people increasingly see control over their time as part of a job's overall value.

There are practical reasons behind this shift. Commuting can eat into several hours a week, while parents and carers often struggle to fit unpredictable responsibilities around fixed working hours. Being able to start early, finish later or work remotely on certain days can take the pressure off. It may also leave people with enough time for exercise, relationships and activities that make life enjoyable. Flexibility, in other words, is not necessarily a luxury; for some workers, it is what makes employment manageable.

Of course, the benefits are not equally available. A software developer may be able to work from home, while a nurse, cleaner or café worker must be physically present. Employers also have legitimate concerns about customer service, teamwork and covering busy periods. If one department gets more freedom than another, tensions can build up. Offering flexibility therefore requires more than simply allowing everyone to choose whatever hours suit them. Managers need sensible arrangements that reflect the demands of different roles.

The survey also raises a question about financial security. Someone on a comfortable income can probably afford to turn down a pay rise in exchange for a shorter commute. A worker struggling with rent, bills and food prices may not have that option. For them, higher wages can provide the peace of mind that flexible hours cannot. A headline about time becoming more valuable than money must be read alongside these very different circumstances.

Still, Adecco's findings suggest that employers should not assume a bigger salary is always enough to keep talented staff. People increasingly ask whether a job gives them room to live, rather than taking over their entire week. The most attractive workplace may be the one that pays fairly and respects personal time. Before accepting your next job, would you look first at the salary, or at the life you could have around it?

=== Advanced ===

title: When Time Counts for More Than Salary
summary: A global survey ranks work-life balance above pay as a reason to leave a job, but stated priorities are not the same as real choices.

Suppose you were offered two positions with similar responsibilities and prospects. One came with a substantial salary increase, but demanded a rigid schedule and a lengthy commute. The other offered less money, yet gave you genuine control over your working hours and location. For years, conventional career advice might have encouraged you to follow the money. Recent research suggests that the calculation is becoming more complicated, as workers weigh up the hidden costs of their working lives.

On 19 November 2026, the recruitment group Adecco released its Global Workforce of the Future report, based on an online survey of 40,500 employees in 36 countries and 21 industries. The fieldwork took place in June and July. For the first time in the seven-year history of the annual research, work-life balance emerged ahead of salary as the leading reason respondents would consider leaving their employer. The distinction matters: the survey records people's stated priorities, not proof that they would necessarily reject higher pay when faced with a real offer.

Nevertheless, the result speaks to a broader reassessment of work. A long commute can quietly consume hours that never appear on a payslip. Fixed schedules can be particularly punishing for parents, carers and anyone juggling education or medical appointments. By contrast, having some say over when and where work gets done can reduce daily friction and create space for relationships, rest and personal interests. For many employees, flexibility is less about enjoying an occasional afternoon off than about making an ordinary week sustainable.

That freedom, however, is distributed unevenly. People who work in hospitals, shops, factories or restaurants cannot simply relocate their duties to a kitchen table. Even among office workers, coordination and face-to-face contact can be valuable. Employers have to strike a balance between autonomy and operational needs, while avoiding a two-tier culture in which some colleagues enjoy considerable freedom and others are left carrying the burden. The real test is whether organisations can redesign work thoughtfully rather than offer fashionable benefits that only a privileged minority can use.

There is a further economic complication. A professional with savings may willingly sacrifice a pay rise for an easier routine, whereas someone living from one payday to the next might find that choice impossible. Flexibility does little to compensate for wages that fail to cover basic expenses. Furthermore, survey findings can disguise differences between countries, industries and stages of life. Treating work-life balance as the new universal priority would therefore oversimplify the picture and risk overlooking those for whom income remains the overriding concern.

Even with those qualifications, the findings challenge employers who rely on salary alone to attract and retain staff. A well-paid job can still be a poor bargain if it leaves little time or energy for anything else. Perhaps career success should be assessed not only in terms of promotion and income, but also by what employment makes possible beyond the workplace. If you had to choose between greater financial reward and greater control over your life, which would you value more, and why?
