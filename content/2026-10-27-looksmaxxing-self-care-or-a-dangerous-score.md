=== Core ===

title: Who Decides If You Look Good?
summary: Online tips can help with clothes, skin or fitness. They can also make people feel that their face is never good enough.

It starts with something small. You want a better haircut, so you watch a video about it. The next one is about skincare, which also seems useful. Then the videos change. Someone is drawing lines across a face and giving it a score out of ten, and explaining which parts are wrong.

This has a name online: looksmaxxing. It means trying to make your appearance as good as possible. Some of the advice is ordinary and safe. Sleep properly. Drink water. Get some exercise. Wear clothes that fit you. A doctor would say the same things.

Other advice is not safe at all. Some videos push extreme diets, products that have never been tested, or ways of changing the shape of your face that can cause real injury. Doctors at Johns Hopkins have warned that this content is reaching boys and teenagers, and that some of it is dangerous.

The bigger problem may be the scoring. When every feature becomes a number, there is no point where you have finished. You can always find something to compare. Researchers who studied one large looksmaxxing forum in 2023 read 8,072 comments and found users examining each other's faces in tiny detail, and telling each other they had failed.

It is worth asking who gains from this. If a video makes you feel bad about your jaw and then sells you a product for your jaw, the bad feeling is the business. The pressure is not an accident.

None of this means you should stop caring how you look. Most people enjoy clothes, hair or sport, and that is normal. The useful question is simple: does this make me feel better about myself, or does it only make me feel behind?

=== Intermediate ===

title: Looksmaxxing: Self-Care or a Dangerous Score?
summary: Looksmaxxing promises confidence through a better face and body. The problem begins when every feature becomes a score.

Nobody searches for an insecurity. They search for a haircut. A few recommendations later, the feed has moved from grooming tips to diagrams of jaw angles, and a stranger is giving a face a number out of ten.

That shift is the subject of growing research. The Centre for Young Lives, which ran focus groups with boys aged 13 to 18 in late 2025, reported in June 2026 that boys were meeting this content without looking for it, and that it arrived mixed in with ordinary gym and self-improvement videos. The report found that 61% of young men follow at least one masculinity influencer.

Part of the appeal is honest enough. Looksmaxxing promises that effort will produce a result, which is a reasonable thing to want at any age. Much of the beginner advice is harmless: sleep, exercise, washing your face, clothes that fit. Doctors at Johns Hopkins make exactly that distinction, separating ordinary routines from the extreme end, where unproven products, severe diets and self-inflicted injury start to appear.

The harder question is what the rating culture does to the people inside it. In a study published in March 2025, researchers analysed 8,072 comments from the two busiest forums on one looksmaxxing site. They found relentless examination of small physical details and language that left users feeling they had failed as men. That is evidence about one community rather than every teenager who watches a skincare video, and it should be read that way.

It is also worth saying plainly that this is not only a boys' problem. Appearance pressure has been aimed at girls for decades. What has changed is the arithmetic, and the habit of turning a face into a number.

There is also a business underneath the insecurity. A video that identifies a flaw and then recommends a purchase has an obvious interest in the flaw existing. Influence is not free, and attention is sold.

Four questions are usually enough to judge any of it. What is the evidence? What is the risk? Who is selling something? And how do I feel after watching? The last one is the most useful, because an account that consistently leaves you worse is not helping you, whatever it claims to be doing.

Caring about your appearance is not the problem. Treating your face as a score that can never quite be high enough is a different thing, and far harder to put down.

=== Advanced ===

title: When Self-Improvement Becomes Appearance Ranking
summary: A trend built around self-improvement can turn ordinary insecurity into a market, a hierarchy and an endless project.

The word sounds almost responsible. Looksmaxxing suggests effort, discipline and a plan, which is why it travels so easily alongside advice about training, sleep and study. The trouble is what happens to a person once appearance is reframed as something to be maximised, because a maximum implies a scale, and a scale implies a rank.

The route in is rarely dramatic. Researchers at the Centre for Young Lives, who ran focus groups with boys aged 13 to 18 in late 2025 and published in June 2026, described content that arrived unrequested, sitting between gym clips and general self-improvement before narrowing towards the extreme. Their report notes that 61% of young men follow at least one masculinity influencer, which makes the first step a matter of ordinary browsing rather than deliberate search.

The academic evidence is narrower but sharper. A study in Sociology of Health and Illness, published in March 2025, analysed 8,072 comments from the two most popular forums on a single looksmaxxing site. The authors describe sustained scrutiny of minute physical features, encouragement towards substantial and sometimes dangerous alteration, and a vocabulary that casts participants as failed men. It is a study of one forum, and it cannot tell us how a casual viewer responds, but it shows clearly what the culture produces at its centre.

Clinicians draw the practical line. Johns Hopkins separates the unremarkable end of the trend, which is grooming, nutrition, sleep and exercise, from practices that risk genuine harm, and notes the arrival of apps that will score a face on jawline or skin quality. The distinction matters because a warning that treats all self-care as a warning sign will simply be ignored.

What makes the ranking durable is how easily it can be monetised. An insecurity that can be measured can be sold to repeatedly, and a hierarchy with no top keeps everybody buying. The content does not need to lie; it only needs to make the gap between a person and the standard feel visible and closable.

Boys are the obvious audience here, and it would be a mistake to conclude that appearance pressure is new or exclusive to them. What is newer is the arithmetic: the conversion of a face into a figure, delivered constantly, by accounts with something to sell.

The mechanics help it persist. A feed learns what holds a viewer, and anxiety holds a viewer well, so the material that unsettles someone is the material they are shown again. An app that returns a score invites a second attempt at a better one. Neither process has to intend any harm to keep a person measuring themselves every evening.

The way out is not indifference, which nobody manages anyway. It is a sense of proportion about what appearance is for. A person can take trouble over how they look and still refuse the premise that the result is a score, and that their self-worth moves up and down with it.
