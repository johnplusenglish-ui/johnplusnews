=== Intermediate ===

Baggy jeans, Adidas trainers, old-fashioned cameras and music from bands such as Oasis and Blur. If you've noticed these things becoming popular again, you're not imagining it. The 1990s are back. Across Britain, younger generations are rediscovering the music, clothes and culture of a decade that ended more than 25 years ago. A new exhibition at London's Tate Britain, called "The 90s: Art and Fashion", explores this period through photographs, clothing and artwork.

But the renewed interest isn't just about fashion. For many people, the 1990s represent a simpler time, before smartphones and social media became part of everyday life. Back then, people made plans by calling each other on landline telephones. If they wanted to listen to a new album, they bought a CD or recorded songs from the radio. Photographs were taken on film, and you couldn't immediately check whether you looked good in them.

Going out with friends also meant being less connected to the outside world. There were no endless notifications, no Instagram stories and no pressure to share every interesting moment online. For younger people who have grown up with smartphones, this sounds surprisingly attractive. Some are even buying old cameras, collecting vinyl records and using basic mobile phones to escape the constant distractions of modern technology.

Music plays a big part in this revival. British bands such as Oasis and Pulp have attracted renewed attention, introducing their songs to audiences who were not even born when they first became famous. Fashion has followed a similar pattern, with oversized clothes, sportswear and other 90s styles appearing in shops and on social media.

However, there is a problem with looking back at the past: we often remember the good things and forget the bad. The 1990s were not perfect. The world experienced serious conflicts, discrimination and economic difficulties. Life before smartphones also came with disadvantages. Staying in touch was harder, information was less accessible and travelling without digital maps could be extremely frustrating. And while today's technology can create stress, it has also made communication, learning and everyday tasks much easier.

Perhaps what people really miss isn't the 1990s themselves, but the feeling of being less distracted. The popularity of old technology suggests that many of us want more control over how we spend our time. There's also something enjoyable about discovering the music and culture of another generation. Parents and children can find common ground through songs, films and fashion.

Ultimately, the 1990s revival may tell us as much about the present as it does about the past. Maybe we don't actually want to return to 1996. We just want to remember what life feels like when we're not constantly looking at a screen.

title: Lost in the 90s: Why a Generation Is Nostalgic for a Decade It Never Knew
summary: As Britpop returns, analogue technology gains new admirers and 1990s fashion dominates the cultural conversation, nostalgia for life before smartphones is proving remarkably difficult to resist.

There is something faintly absurd about a generation raised on TikTok longing for a world in which the internet was slow, mobile phones were enormous and finding your way across an unfamiliar city required a paper map. Yet nostalgia for the 1990s has become one of contemporary culture's more revealing obsessions.

From oversized sportswear and vintage cameras to the renewed popularity of Britpop, the decade is enjoying an enthusiastic revival. At Tate Britain, a major exhibition entitled "The 90s: Art and Fashion" examines a period whose influence continues to shape British popular culture. The renewed fascination extends well beyond those old enough to remember it. For younger generations, the attraction appears to lie partly in what the decade lacked: smartphones, social media and the expectation that virtually every experience should be documented and shared.

The 1990s are increasingly imagined as the final chapter of a more spontaneous, less self-conscious world. Music was experienced through physical objects, nights out unfolded without the possibility of instant online exposure, and friendships developed without the constant interruption of digital communication. In retrospect, these limitations can look remarkably like freedoms. The resurgence of bands such as Oasis and Pulp has reinforced this romanticised vision. Their music evokes an era when youth culture seemed capable of bringing entire communities together, rather than dividing audiences into increasingly narrow algorithmic categories.

Fashion offers a similar escape. The return of casual sportswear and deliberately untidy styles suggests a rejection of the meticulously curated appearance encouraged by social media. But nostalgia has a habit of airbrushing history. The decade remembered for optimism, creative freedom and cultural experimentation was also marked by considerable hardship. The wars in the former Yugoslavia, the Rwandan genocide and the continuing AIDS crisis challenge the idea that the 1990s represented some universally carefree golden age.

Even Britain's celebrated cultural revival was not equally welcoming to everyone. The period's fashionable image often concealed inequalities involving class, gender and race. There is also an obvious contradiction in the way nostalgia is now consumed. Young people discover supposedly authentic 1990s culture through TikTok recommendations, streaming algorithms and Instagram accounts devoted to vintage aesthetics. The very technologies they hope to escape are responsible for promoting the escape.

Nevertheless, dismissing the trend as superficial would overlook a deeper cultural anxiety. Modern life offers unprecedented convenience and connectivity, yet many people feel overwhelmed by information, constant comparison and the pressure to remain permanently available. The appeal of analogue technology may therefore represent a desire for more deliberate experiences. Listening to an album from beginning to end, taking photographs without immediately reviewing them or spending an evening without checking notifications can feel unexpectedly liberating.

Of course, abandoning modern technology altogether is neither practical nor necessarily desirable. Digital tools have transformed accessibility, communication and opportunities for education. What the 1990s revival reveals is not simply a longing for old music or outdated fashion, but an increasingly widespread dissatisfaction with the pace and demands of contemporary life. Perhaps the most interesting question isn't why people want to return to the past. It's why the present makes the past seem so appealing.
