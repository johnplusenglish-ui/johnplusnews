=== Core ===

title: Does Confidence Mean Someone Is Right?
summary: Some people sound sure about everything. A psychiatrist says that is exactly when we should ask more questions.

You are watching a video. The speaker looks straight at the camera. Their voice rises and falls. They never hesitate, and they explain a complicated subject in one clear minute. You find yourself nodding. But do you actually know whether they are right?

Alex Curmi, a psychiatrist writing in the Guardian, thinks this is a problem worth taking seriously. He describes meeting a confident stranger in a bar. The man spoke easily about economies, space travel and politics, and Curmi felt he could learn a lot from him. Then the man said that some people even believe the Earth is round. The confidence had been real. The knowledge had not.

Curmi calls this quality charisma: the ability to make other people trust you, like you or follow you. He argues that charisma is not magic. It is a set of skills. Eye contact, body language, a voice that changes in pitch and speed, a good story at the right moment. Some people are born with these skills. Others learn them, and online there are plenty of guides that teach them.

So why are we so easily persuaded? Curmi offers an explanation from our distant past. For most of human history, people lived in small groups, sometimes only twenty people. You saw the same faces every day, in good times and in danger. If someone was only charming on the surface, you found out quickly.

Today we judge people on very little. We see a few minutes that they have chosen to show us, carefully edited, with the rest of their life left out. Our brains, Curmi says, did not evolve for that.

His advice is not to distrust everybody. It is to separate confidence from knowledge, and to treat them as two different things. When someone speaks smoothly about health, money or world events, he suggests a few simple questions. What experience do they have? What do they gain if you agree? Is there a good argument on the other side?

None of this means that a confident person is lying. Plenty of experts are also excellent speakers. It means that sounding sure is not evidence, and it never was.

=== Intermediate ===

summary: A psychiatrist argues that charisma should make us more careful, not less. Online, we judge people on a handful of confident minutes.

Most of us know the feeling. Somebody is speaking, and they are so fluent, so relaxed and so obviously in command of the subject that we stop evaluating and start agreeing. Alex Curmi, a psychiatrist and podcast host writing in the Guardian, thinks that moment deserves more suspicion than we usually give it.

His own example is small and recognisable. Waiting for a friend in a bar, he fell into conversation with a stranger who talked easily about unstable economies, commercial space travel and the chances of a coup in Russia. Curmi leaned in, asked questions and felt he was learning. Then the stranger remarked that some people even believe the Earth is round, and the whole performance came apart.

Charisma, Curmi argues, is not an undefinable X-factor. The psychology of it describes a set of skills and behaviours: eye contact, certainty in the body, variation in pitch, pace and volume, storytelling, well-timed emotion. Some people have them naturally; many more learn them. He says so from experience, having been told by every content expert that his own voice was too flat for an audience.

Why does it work on us so reliably? Curmi reaches for evolution. Humans spent most of their history in small nomadic groups, sometimes as few as twenty people, where strong leadership mattered. The traits we now call charismatic were useful signals of status. The difference is that you then watched those people every day, often in genuine danger. Superficial charm did not survive that kind of inspection for long, so charisma only won if it came with judgment and character.

That daily inspection has gone. Technology lets anyone present the best few minutes and hide the rest, and the audience has no way to check the remainder. We worry about teenagers being manipulated online, Curmi writes, but algorithmic social media is only about fifteen years old, so in this respect we are all teenagers.

His proposed defence is unglamorous: critical thinking, treated as a collective immune system and taught properly in schools. Learn to evaluate a source. Look for the counter-argument precisely when you feel emotionally pulled towards a view. Treat changing your mind as something to be proud of rather than embarrassed by.

It is an opinion piece rather than a study, and it is worth noting that charisma is not the same as dishonesty: many people who explain things well also know what they are talking about. But the practical test Curmi offers costs nothing. When someone holds forth convincingly on finance, health or geopolitics, ask what experience they have, what they stand to gain, and what need in you their answer is meeting.

=== Advanced ===

title: Confidence Is Not Evidence
summary: Charisma used to be tested by daily contact. Online, it is tested by nothing at all, and that may be the real change.

The uncomfortable thing about being persuaded is how little of it feels like persuasion. We rarely notice ourselves deciding to believe a confident speaker. We simply find that we have stopped weighing what is being said and started enjoying the way it is being said. The psychiatrist Alex Curmi, writing in the Guardian, argues that this reflex deserves to be treated as a warning rather than a pleasure.

He opens with a deflating anecdote. In a bar, a stranger held his attention on unstable economies, commercial space travel and the likelihood of a coup in Russia. Curmi, by his own account, leaned in and asked questions, feeling he had something to learn. The spell broke when the man observed that some people even believe the Earth is round. Nothing about his manner had changed; only Curmi's estimate of what lay behind it.

The argument that follows rests on a deflation of its own. Charisma is not an inexplicable quality some people possess, but a describable set of behaviours: certainty in posture and gaze, variation in pitch and pace, narrative, well-placed moral feeling. Curmi notes that creators are coached in exactly these techniques, his own flat delivery having been diagnosed as a problem by every expert he consulted. If charisma can be taught, it can be adopted by anyone, including people with nothing of substance to offer.

His explanation for our susceptibility is evolutionary, and it is the most interesting part of the case. Human beings spent the overwhelming majority of their history in small nomadic bands, where high status plausibly went to those who displayed the traits we still read as charismatic. Crucially, those displays were continuously audited. You observed the same twenty people daily, often in conditions where incompetence was lethal, so charm unaccompanied by judgment was exposed quickly. The signal was reliable because it was expensive to fake over time.

What has changed is not the signal but the audit. A curated clip offers the display without any of the surrounding evidence, and our instincts were not built to discount it. This is the strongest version of his point, and it does not require anyone to be deceitful: the mechanism misfires even when the speaker is sincere but simply wrong.

Where the piece is weakest is in its headline. Treating charisma itself as a red flag risks a different error, since the ability to explain something well correlates with understanding it, and a rule that distrusts fluent people would discard a great deal of genuine expertise. Curmi's own remedies are more measured than his framing: teach critical thinking as a civic skill, seek the counter-argument when you feel most pulled, and ask what the speaker's experience is and what they gain from your agreement.

Perhaps the usable version is narrower. Confidence is information about the speaker's manner and almost none about the claim. Keeping those two judgments apart is a small discipline, and it is one that the architecture of a feed is designed to collapse.
