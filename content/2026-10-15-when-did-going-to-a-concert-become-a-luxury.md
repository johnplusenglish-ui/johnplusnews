For many music fans, the most stressful part of seeing their favourite artist no longer happens at the concert itself. It happens weeks or months earlier, staring at an online ticket queue, watching prices climb and wondering whether they can justify spending hundreds of pounds on a single evening. Live music has become increasingly expensive, and younger audiences are feeling the pressure. What was once a relatively affordable night out can now require months of saving, careful budgeting and a considerable amount of luck.

The figures illustrate how dramatically things have changed. When Oasis performed at Knebworth in 1996, tickets cost £22.50. Three decades later, tickets for the band's 2027 tour have been advertised at between £71 and £266.60. Other major acts are charging similarly high prices, with tickets for Slipknot's Wembley show reaching £200. Inflation explains some of the increase, but not all of it. One particularly controversial development is dynamic pricing, a system in which ticket prices can rise when demand is exceptionally high.

During sales for Oasis's 2025 reunion tour, some fans reported seeing tickets that initially cost £135 increase to £350. The practice attracted widespread criticism, raising questions about transparency and whether loyal fans were being penalised for an artist's popularity. Then there are resale platforms, where tickets can be offered at substantial mark-ups, and additional booking fees that increase the final cost. For fans already struggling with rent, university fees and everyday expenses, the combined price can be difficult to justify.

The music industry, however, argues that there are legitimate reasons for higher prices. Touring has become more expensive, particularly since the pandemic. Transport, accommodation, equipment, venue hire and staffing costs have all risen. Major stadium productions can involve hundreds of workers and enormous technical operations. The way musicians earn money has also changed. Streaming services have transformed the industry, and many artists now depend more heavily on live performances than they did when physical record sales generated substantial revenue.

There is also a straightforward economic reality: promoters charge high prices because enough people are willing to pay them. Even when fans complain bitterly about the cost, major tours can sell out within hours. Yet this creates an uncomfortable contradiction. Concerts are supposed to bring people together through a shared love of music. Increasingly, access to that experience depends on how much disposable income someone has.

Some musicians have responded by avoiding dynamic pricing or supporting smaller venues through contributions from their tours. Others argue that performers should be free to charge what the market will bear. Meanwhile, smaller independent venues continue to struggle, despite often offering far more affordable opportunities to hear live music. For younger fans, the dilemma is particularly frustrating. An artist might visit their city only once every few years, creating pressure to buy a ticket regardless of the cost. Missing the show can feel like missing a significant cultural moment.

Nobody expects concerts to be free. Musicians and the people who work behind the scenes deserve to earn a living. But there is a growing difference between paying a fair price for live entertainment and being priced out of it altogether. If attending a major concert becomes something only wealthier fans can afford, the music may still bring people together. The audience, however, will look rather different.
