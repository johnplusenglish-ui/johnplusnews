title: Waiting for the Food or the Photo?
summary: People have waited 24 hours for a jacket potato. Why do some foods become so popular online, and what does it mean for small restaurants?

Imagine waiting outside a food stall all night. You're not waiting for a famous chef or a special meal. You're waiting for a baked potato. It sounds ridiculous, but the owners of a business in Preston, in northern England, say it happens. The brothers behind SpudBros say that some people have camped out nearby and waited almost 24 hours to buy one of their loaded jacket potatoes. We only have the brothers' word for the waiting time.

Jacob and Harley Nelson sell baked potatoes from a converted tram. Their food is simple: a jacket potato, which is a large potato baked in its skin, with fillings such as cheese, beans or chilli. However, the brothers did something that changed their business. They began putting out videos on social media. People enjoyed watching them prepare the food, talk to customers and add generous amounts of filling. Their videos reached millions of viewers.

The business soon became an internet attraction. Fans travelled to Preston from other towns and even other countries. Some wanted to taste the food, but they also wanted to take photos and tell their friends they had been there. A normal lunch had become an experience. The queue itself was part of the excitement, because a long line made the food look even more popular.

This is happening at other food businesses too. Small bakeries, ice-cream shops and cafés can suddenly become famous when an influencer shares a video. For the owners, that attention can be extremely helpful. It brings new customers at a time when rent, food and energy costs are high. A small business that used to struggle to attract people may suddenly have more customers than it can serve.

But internet fame has a downside. Staff can feel exhausted, local people may find it difficult to buy food from a shop they used to visit regularly, and customers who have travelled a long way may be disappointed. A video can make a meal seem extraordinary, even when it is only an ordinary, well-made snack. Popularity can also disappear quickly when viewers move on to the next trend.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying a baked potato, and the brothers have worked hard to make their business successful. Yet their story says something interesting about how we choose where to eat. We often trust what we see online, especially when thousands of other people seem excited. The next time you see a huge queue outside a café, it might be worth asking whether everyone is waiting for the food or for the chance to say they joined the queue.

title: Would the Potato Be Worth It Without the Camera?
summary: Jacob and Harley Nelson sell baked potatoes from a converted tram in Preston. Their videos brought the crowds, and the crowds became the attraction.

SpudBros is a business run by brothers Jacob and Harley Nelson from a converted tram in Preston, Lancashire. It sells baked potatoes piled high with fillings, which is hardly the kind of food you would normally travel across the country for. Yet the brothers say that people have queued overnight and waited almost 24 hours to buy one.

The brothers began posting videos when business was difficult during the pandemic. Their clips showed potatoes being prepared, customers reacting and enormous portions being served. The format was simple, entertaining and easy to share. As their following grew, so did the crowds. Visitors arrived from distant towns and overseas, hoping to taste the potatoes they had already watched countless times on their phones. The brothers now operate in the strange space between running a food business and being internet personalities.

SpudBros is part of a much wider trend. A recommendation from the right content creator can transform an ordinary café into the place everybody suddenly wants to visit. In London, popular bakeries, frozen yoghurt shops and takeaway businesses have found themselves dealing with queues that stretch along the pavement. For owners facing rising rents and food costs, this kind of attention can be a lifeline. Free publicity may achieve what an expensive advertising campaign could not.

But going viral creates problems of its own. Staff may struggle to keep up with demand, regular customers can feel pushed aside, and neighbourhoods have to deal with crowds that arrive almost overnight. Restaurants also risk disappointing visitors whose expectations have been raised by carefully filmed videos. A perfectly good meal may seem underwhelming after somebody has travelled three hours and then queued for hours.

There is a psychological element to all this. People are more likely to become interested in something when they can see that others want it. A long queue acts as a public recommendation, while posting a photograph proves that you were part of the moment. Some customers probably care just as much about the story they can tell afterwards as the meal they have bought. Restaurants are not simply selling food; increasingly, they are selling an experience that can be shared online.

The Nelson brothers have shown how much a small business can achieve by understanding that world. But their success also reveals its limits. Online attention is unpredictable, and even the most enthusiastic followers eventually drift away. For customers, the more interesting question is whether the potato would still be worth buying without the camera, the famous visitors or the queue. Perhaps the test of a good lunch is whether you would return for more when nobody is filming.

=== Advanced ===

title: Is the Queue Part of What People Are Buying?
summary: Viral queues can bring fame and pressure to small restaurants. What do 24-hour waits for a baked potato say about social media and food?

A baked potato from a converted tram in Preston has become a destination. The brothers behind SpudBros say some customers have slept outside and waited almost 24 hours for their loaded jacket potatoes. Videos helped draw visitors from far beyond Lancashire. Now lunch comes with a second attraction: being part of the queue everyone has seen online.

Jacob and Harley Nelson built up their following by filming the everyday business of preparing and selling potatoes. Their videos captured generous toppings, lively encounters with customers and the brothers' own on-camera personalities. The approach came about during a difficult period for the business and eventually attracted audiences far beyond Preston. Visitors began arriving from other parts of Britain and overseas, drawn not merely by the promise of lunch but by the opportunity to participate in a story they already knew from their screens.

The phenomenon extends well beyond potatoes. Restaurants and bakeries across Britain have discovered that a single enthusiastic online recommendation can generate queues that traditional advertising would struggle to produce. During a period of rising costs and precarious margins, sudden visibility can rescue a struggling business. It can also transform a small operator's relationship with customers, employees and the surrounding neighbourhood. Virality delivers attention at remarkable speed, without necessarily providing the time or resources required to accommodate it.

That is where the bargain becomes less appealing. Staff face exhausting peaks of demand, loyal customers lose easy access to businesses they helped sustain, and residents must put up with unexpected crowds. Nor does the customer always benefit. A meal that might have been entirely satisfying after a short wait can become a disappointment once inflated expectations, travel expenses and hours of queueing are taken into account. The camera is very good at capturing melted cheese; it is less interested in the practical inconvenience of obtaining it.

Queues also change what people think they are buying. Research into consumer behaviour suggests that visible approval from others can influence food choices, while social platforms reward places that photograph well and generate strong reactions. Waiting becomes a signal of desirability, and posting about the wait turns an ordinary purchase into a small performance of cultural participation. The experience is self-reinforcing: a crowd attracts attention, the attention attracts more people, and the expanding queue provides fresh evidence that the attraction must be worthwhile.

None of this means the food is poor or that customers are foolish. SpudBros has shown entrepreneurial skill, and there is something pleasing about a simple potato enjoying its moment in the spotlight. The concern is a hospitality culture in which survival increasingly depends on being noticed, preferably all at once. Small businesses need loyal customers and sustainable margins, not merely a brief spell of internet celebrity. Once the cameras turn towards the next sensation, a restaurant must still persuade people to return for the less glamorous reason that its food is worth eating.
