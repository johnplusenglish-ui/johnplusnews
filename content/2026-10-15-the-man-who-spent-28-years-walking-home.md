=== Advanced ===

When Karl Bushby arrived in Dover earlier this month, one of his first priorities was a plate of fish and chips. It was an ordinary meal, but for the 57-year-old former British soldier, it marked an extraordinary moment: he was back on British soil after spending almost three decades trying to walk home.

Bushby began his journey in November 1998, setting off from the southern tip of Chile with just $500, a homemade equipment trolley and an ambition that most people considered ridiculous. He intended to travel through the Americas, cross into Russia and continue across Asia and Europe until he reached his hometown of Hull. He had two rules: he could not use transport to advance along his route, and he could not return home until he had completed the journey on foot. He expected it to take 12 years. It has taken more than twice that long.

The expedition, known as the Goliath Expedition, has covered approximately 36,000 miles and 29 countries. Along the way, Bushby has survived extreme temperatures, serious illness and encounters with armed groups. In Colombia, he crossed the notoriously dangerous Darién Gap, where he found himself threatened at gunpoint. In 2006, he and fellow adventurer Dimitri Kieffer spent two weeks crossing the Bering Strait between Alaska and Russia, negotiating unstable sea ice and narrowly avoiding disaster when their equipment fell into freezing water.

Yet some of his greatest obstacles were bureaucratic rather than physical. He was arrested in Russia, struggled repeatedly with visa restrictions and spent long periods unable to continue because of financial problems. The pandemic and international conflicts caused further delays. In 2024, he swam across the Caspian Sea with a small team after political and border restrictions complicated his planned route. Earlier this month, he swam the English Channel after being refused permission to walk through the Channel Tunnel.

The journey has come at a considerable personal cost. Bushby left behind a young son, missed important family occasions and struggled to maintain relationships. His son is now 36. The question of whether any ambition can justify such sacrifices has followed him throughout his travels. Still, Bushby insists the expedition has changed his understanding of human nature. Before leaving Britain, he had a rather cynical view of the world. Twenty-eight years later, he speaks of the remarkable generosity of strangers who offered him food, shelter and medical help, often when they had very little themselves.

Now, with only the final stretch to Hull remaining, Bushby faces a different challenge. For most of his adult life, the journey has given him a clear purpose. Soon, that purpose will disappear. He has spent nearly three decades trying to get home. What he will do when he finally arrives may prove to be the hardest question of all.
