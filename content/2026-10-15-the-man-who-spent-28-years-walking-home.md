=== Core ===

title: The Longest Walk Home
summary: In 1998, Karl Bushby left Chile to walk home to Hull. After almost 28 years, he is nearly there.

In November 1998, Karl Bushby set off from the southern tip of Chile with a small amount of money and a very unusual plan. He wanted to walk home to Hull, in northern England. The journey would take him through the Americas, across the sea ice between Alaska and Russia, and eventually across Europe. He thought it might take 12 years. Almost 28 years later, he finally reached Britain.

Karl had two rules. He would not use a vehicle to move forwards along his route, and he would not return home until he had completed the journey. The former British soldier called his adventure the Goliath Expedition. At first, he walked north through South America. He travelled through hot deserts, mountains and dangerous jungle. In the Darién Gap, between Colombia and Panama, he had to deal with the threat of armed groups.

After reaching North America, Karl carried on towards Alaska. In 2006, he and another explorer crossed the Bering Strait on moving sea ice. It was one of the most dangerous parts of the whole journey. They finally reached Russia, but the authorities arrested them because they had not entered the country at an official border point. Problems with visas and permission to travel would continue for years.

Money, closed borders and the pandemic all held up Karl's progress. In 2024, he made a long swim across the Caspian Sea with support from a team. He then travelled through Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey before continuing into Europe. On 5 October 2026, he reached Dover after swimming across the English Channel. He was finally back in Britain, although he still had around 400 kilometres left to walk to Hull.

The adventure has been exciting, but it has also cost him a great deal. Karl has missed years with his family, including much of his son's childhood. He has also depended on the kindness of strangers for food, shelter and help. These experiences have made him more positive about people. After nearly three decades on the road, one question remains. When he finally arrives home, how will he get used to ordinary life again?

=== Intermediate ===

title: Twenty-Eight Years, One Journey Home
summary: Karl Bushby left the bottom of Chile in 1998 and refused to take a lift. He is now a few hundred kilometres from his own front door.

For most people, getting home is a matter of catching a bus, train or plane. For Karl Bushby, it has taken almost 28 years. The former British soldier left southern Chile in 1998 with a few hundred dollars and an ambition that sounded almost impossible: to reach his hometown of Hull without using transport to move forwards along his route. This October, after crossing continents and swimming the English Channel, he finally set foot on British soil.

Bushby began in Punta Arenas, near the southern tip of South America. He planned to walk north through the Americas, cross the Bering Strait into Russia and continue west towards Europe. He set himself two strict conditions. He could not take transport to advance, and he could not go home until the expedition was complete. He originally expected it to take about 12 years. That estimate turned out to be wildly optimistic.

Physical dangers came early. In South America, he faced extreme weather and crossed the Darién Gap, a difficult jungle region where travellers can encounter armed groups. In 2006, he and fellow explorer Dimitri Kieffer spent roughly two weeks crossing the frozen Bering Strait from Alaska into Russia. Their progress depended on unstable sea ice, and entering Russia led to their arrest because they had not arrived through an official border crossing.

Surprisingly, paperwork became as much of an obstacle as the wilderness. Bushby spent years working around visa restrictions, financial setbacks and international borders that were closed or difficult to cross. The pandemic caused further delays. When his route reached the Caspian Sea, he ran into another seemingly impossible problem. In 2024, he completed a lengthy swim across it with a support team, then continued through Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey.

On 5 October 2026, Bushby landed near Dover after swimming across the English Channel. He was back in Britain for the first time under his own expedition rules, but the walk was not yet over. About 400 kilometres remained between the landing point and Hull. The final stretch is small compared with the thousands of kilometres behind him, yet it carries an enormous emotional weight.

The journey has come at a personal cost. Bushby missed much of his son's childhood, important family occasions and the ordinary routines that shape most people's lives. At the same time, he says that the generosity of strangers has changed his view of humanity. People with very little often offered him food or somewhere to sleep. Soon the mission that has organised almost all his adult life will be complete. Reaching home will be an achievement. Discovering what comes next may be the bigger adjustment.

=== Advanced ===

title: Twenty-Eight Years on the Road: The Man Still Walking Home
summary: After almost three decades on foot, Karl Bushby is finally close to home. But what does a man do when the journey that defined his life comes to an end?

When Karl Bushby emerged from the English Channel at Dover on 5 October, he had already walked, and occasionally swum, a route that had consumed almost three decades of his life. He was back on British soil, but not quite home. Hull, where his extraordinary undertaking began as an idea, still lay roughly 400 kilometres away. It was an almost absurdly small distance after everything that had preceded it.

The Goliath Expedition began on 1 November 1998 in Punta Arenas, southern Chile. Bushby, a former British paratrooper, intended to travel through the Americas, cross into Siberia and return to England across Eurasia. His rules were uncompromising: no mechanised transport to advance along the route and no return home until he had completed it under his own power. He envisaged a 12-year challenge. In practice, geographical hazards proved only one part of the ordeal.

The route carried him through Patagonia, the Andes and the Darién Gap, the dangerous stretch of jungle between Colombia and Panama. In 2006, Bushby and fellow adventurer Dimitri Kieffer negotiated the shifting ice of the Bering Strait to reach Russia. Instead of celebrating a successful crossing, they ran afoul of immigration authorities and were detained. The incident foreshadowed years in which consular complications, restrictive borders and a shortage of funds would set progress back more effectively than bad weather ever could. Walking, it turned out, was the straightforward part; persuading officials to let him carry on walking was not.

The pandemic and subsequent geopolitical tensions compounded those difficulties. Having made his way across parts of Asia, Bushby confronted the formidable barrier of the Caspian Sea. In 2024 he crossed it by swimming, assisted by a support team, before continuing through Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey. Even the final approach to Britain demanded improvisation. Unable to secure permission for a pedestrian passage through the Channel Tunnel, he ended up swimming the Channel instead.

There is a temptation to treat the expedition as a triumph of stubborn determination and leave it at that. Yet its human costs resist such a tidy conclusion. Bushby has been absent for much of his son's life and has sacrificed relationships, stability and countless ordinary family occasions. He has also discovered a generosity that complicates his earlier scepticism about human nature. Strangers in isolated places repeatedly provided shelter, food and assistance, often with little expectation of anything in return. Much of that hospitality came from people with very little to spare, which is precisely why it altered his view of human nature.

The public celebration of extraordinary endurance can gloss over a more difficult question: what happens when the pursuit of a goal becomes a person's identity? Bushby has spent nearly all his adult life moving towards a destination that was always both literal and symbolic. Completing the final miles may require less physical stamina than the previous thousands, but the transition from expedition to everyday life offers no marked trail. For a man who has spent 28 years trying to get home, staying there could prove the most unfamiliar adventure.

=== Core language ===

- set off (phrasal verb): to begin a journey
- deal with (phrasal verb): to take action about a problem
- carry on (phrasal verb): to continue doing something
- route (noun): the way you travel from one place to another
- the threat of (phrase): the danger that something bad may happen
- official (adjective): agreed or allowed by the authorities

=== Intermediate language ===

- set foot on (phrase): to arrive somewhere, especially for the first time
- turn out (phrasal verb): to happen in a particular way in the end
- an obstacle (noun): something that blocks your progress
- setback (noun): a problem that delays your progress
- work around (phrasal verb): to find a way past a problem without removing it
- wildly optimistic (collocation): far more hopeful than the facts allow

- undertaking (noun): a large or difficult task that somebody takes on
- uncompromising (adjective): refusing to change your demands or standards
- foreshadow (verb): to be an early sign of something that happens later
- a shortage of (phrase): not enough of something
- geographical hazards (collocation): dangers created by the land itself
- straightforward (adjective): simple and easy to do
