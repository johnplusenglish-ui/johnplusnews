title: Why Are More People Talking to Therapists?

Twenty years ago, saying "I'm seeing a therapist" could stop a conversation. Today it often receives the same reply as any other appointment: that sounds sensible, I hope it helps. The change is real, and most people would call it an improvement.

A large UK survey suggests how far it has gone. Between February and March 2026, YouGov asked 5,058 adults about therapy for the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy. Thirty-seven per cent said they had seen a therapist at some point, up from 35% the year before. Of those who had, 72% said they found it helpful.

That last number is worth reading carefully. It describes what people reported about their own experience. It does not mean therapy works for everybody, or that it is the right answer to every problem. Some people try it and stop. Some need a different kind of support entirely.

Being willing to go is also not the same as being able to go. The same survey asked people who had considered therapy but never started. Thirty-five per cent said the cost stopped them. Twenty-two per cent said the waiting list was too long. Talking about help has become easier much faster than getting it.

A waiting list is easy to say and hard to live through. Several months is long enough for a problem to grow, or for a person to decide that asking was a mistake.

Something else has changed along the way. Words from the therapy room are now used everywhere, in group chats and in videos online. Having the language to describe a feeling can genuinely help a person explain what is wrong. The risk is that a serious word gets used loosely, as a quick way to end an argument rather than understand one.

None of this makes listening less valuable. A friend who pays attention is not a substitute for a qualified professional, but it is not nothing either. What a person usually wants first is somewhere private to say the thing out loud.

The harder question is for the rest of us. A country can be very comfortable discussing mental health and still leave people waiting months for support. Being open about a problem is the easy part. Paying for the answer is the test.

Mentioning a therapist used to require a certain amount of courage. Now it can pass without comment, somewhere between a dentist and a physiotherapist on the scale of ordinary appointments. The shame has faded faster than almost anyone predicted twenty years ago.

The figures follow the mood. For the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy, YouGov surveyed 5,058 UK adults between 16 February and 6 March 2026. Thirty-seven per cent reported having seen a therapist, compared with 35% in the previous survey, and 72% of those who had therapy said they found it helpful.

Rising numbers can mean several things at once, which is why the survey cannot settle the argument on its own. Less stigma may be bringing people forward who would once have stayed quiet. Greater need may be pushing them. Wider awareness may simply have told more people that the option exists. All three can be true together.

Openness is also cheap, and access is not. Among respondents who had considered therapy without going, 35% pointed to the cost and 22% to long waiting lists. Those are practical barriers rather than cultural ones, and no amount of public conversation removes them. A person can be entirely comfortable asking for help and still be unable to afford it.

The picture across Europe is harder to see clearly. The OECD's modelling suggests that slightly more than one in five people in OECD and EU countries experienced a mental disorder in 2023, most commonly depression or anxiety, and estimates that nearly two thirds of people in the EU who need mental healthcare face inadequate access to it. These are modelled estimates rather than counted appointments, but the direction is consistent with what the UK survey describes.

Meanwhile the vocabulary has escaped the consulting room. Psychologists writing for the American Psychological Association describe the benefit plainly: people now have better words for what they feel, and that makes some conversations possible. They also note the cost. Clinical terms can be stretched until they mean very little, or used to close a disagreement rather than explore it.

Access is not the only thing that matters either. Useful support depends on a qualified practitioner, a reasonable fit between two people, confidentiality, and expectations realistic enough to survive a bad week. The word therapy covers many different methods, and none of them works for everyone.

So the honest summary is that one thing has clearly improved and another has not kept pace. Fewer people are ashamed. Fewer people, proportionally, are still waiting. Those are different achievements, and only one of them has been reached.

=== Advanced ===

title: Therapy Has Gone Mainstream, but Access Has Not
summary: The language of therapy is everywhere. The harder achievement is making qualified, useful support available to those who want it.
audio: audio/therapy-is-more-normal-is-it-easier-to-get-advanced-863d6c.m4a

A sentence that once carried weight now barely registers. Therapy has become mainstream in the plainest sense: it is discussed without explanation, by people who assume their listener already understands. Saying that you see a therapist attracts roughly the response of saying that you see a physio, which counts as genuine social progress and should be recorded as such. The question worth asking is what that acceptance has actually delivered.

The most recent UK evidence suggests a slow upward drift rather than a transformation. YouGov surveyed 5,058 adults between 16 February and 6 March 2026 for the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy. Thirty-seven per cent reported having seen a therapist, up from 35%, and 72% of those who had reported finding it helpful. Self-reported helpfulness is a reasonable signal and a poor measure of clinical outcome, and the distinction matters.

Interpreting the rise is harder than quoting it. Reduced stigma, increased distress, better information and expanded provision would all produce the same upward line, and a single survey cannot separate them. Treating the trend as proof that a society has become kinder, or that it has become sicker, is an argument imported from outside the data.

The treatment gap is where the story turns. Among those who had considered therapy and not pursued it, 35% cited cost and 22% cited waiting lists. The OECD's modelling puts the problem on a continental scale, estimating that slightly over one in five people across OECD and EU countries experienced a mental disorder in 2023, and that nearly two thirds of people in the EU needing mental healthcare encounter inadequate access. Cultural permission arrived first; capacity has not followed.

Language ran ahead of both. Clinical vocabulary now circulates through friendships, workplaces and social media, and psychologists writing in the American Psychological Association's magazine identify the trade-off precisely. The words give people a way to name experiences they previously carried silently. They can also oversimplify, flattening an ordinary conflict into a diagnosis and awarding one party the authority of the clinic.

The direction of travel is contested for a reason. A rise in people seeking help is compatible with a population under greater strain and with a population finally willing to say so, and the two readings carry opposite implications for policy. Choosing between them on the strength of a single trend line is a political preference wearing the clothes of evidence.

There is a quieter requirement underneath all of it. Useful support depends on qualifications, on a reasonable fit between the person and the practitioner, on confidentiality, and on expectations modest enough to survive contact with a difficult month. None of that is conveyed by the word therapy on its own, and none of it is guaranteed by a culture that approves of the idea.

Which leaves the social question the surveys imply but cannot answer. A country that talks easily about getting help has cleared the first obstacle, and the first obstacle is not the expensive one. Whether that openness ever becomes an appointment is decided by funding, workforce and waiting times, not by how comfortable the conversation has become.
