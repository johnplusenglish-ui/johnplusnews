title: Children's Fingerprints in Ancient Clay
summary: Tiny clay figures from Israel are between 11,650 and 15,000 years old. Fingerprints in the clay suggest that children helped to make them.

Imagine finding a tiny clay bird that is thousands of years old. Now imagine that, pressed into the clay, there is a fingerprint left by a child. In November 2026, researchers published a study about objects like this, found at three sites in Israel.

The makers were the Natufians. They lived in some of the first villages, before farming began, and found food by hunting and collecting plants. A team led by Laurent Davin, from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, studied 154 small clay objects from three Natufian sites. The objects are between about 11,650 and 15,000 years old. They include 15 figurines, most of them shaped like birds. Some pieces may show animals such as a gazelle, and some seem to show people. The authors say this is the earliest clay-figure tradition known in this part of the world.

How can anyone know who made them? The team looked at fingerprints. The lines on a child's fingers are closer together than the lines on an adult's. By measuring them and comparing them with prints from modern people who shaped clay, the researchers could estimate the age of the person who left each mark. Some prints came from adults, and some came from children.

One figure shaped like a person is especially interesting. It has the print of a young adult, and a child's print partly covers it. The researchers think that an adult may have passed the figure to a child while the clay was still wet. That is their interpretation. The fingerprints show who touched the clay, but they cannot show why. It could be a way of teaching, or play, or both.

There are limits. This is a preliminary study. Most of the pieces are broken, and only two are complete. The team has not yet found out where the clay came from or how it was hardened, and they have not worked out what the red colour is. We also cannot say that anyone gave an art lesson.

Even so, the children did leave their mark. It may be one piece of the puzzle about how people in the first villages learned the ropes of making things. Where do you pick up your skills, by watching, copying or being told?

title: What Can a Child's Fingerprint Tell Us About the Past?
summary: A study of 154 clay objects from three Natufian sites in Israel uses fingerprints to show that children and adults handled the clay. Why children took part, and whether we should call it art, is a matter of interpretation.

Archaeology usually deals with things that are broken, buried and silent, so it is unusual when it offers something as personal as a fingerprint. In November 2026, a team led by Laurent Davin, from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, published a study in PLOS One of 154 small clay objects from three Natufian sites in Israel. The Natufians lived in some of the earliest villages, before agriculture, and the objects date from roughly 15,000 to 11,650 years ago.

The collection includes 15 figurines, most of them bird-shaped, together with others that appear to show animals and people. One fragment may represent a gazelle's face, and some pieces keep traces of red colouring. The authors argue that this is the earliest clay figurative tradition yet known in Southwest Asia, which challenges the idea that such clay objects became common only with Neolithic farming. Davin says the finds change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in the first villages.

The most original part of the study is its method. The ridges on a child's fingertips are narrower than those on an adult's, so the researchers measured the ridge breadth in prints left in the clay and compared it with prints from modern people who had shaped clay. That allowed them to estimate the age of the person behind each mark. The results suggest that children and adults both took part, and that the children were not simply watching. Their prints appear in the shaping of the objects and, in one case, in a red residue that was smoothed by a child's finger.

One human-shaped figure carries the print of a young adult, partly covered by the print of a child. The authors read this as an adult passing the figure to a child while the clay was wet, which they call a possible rare sign of knowledge passed on through hands-on learning. They also suggest that children who made the bird figures were probably guided by an adult tradition. These are interpretations. A fingerprint records who touched the clay and in what order, but it cannot reveal intention.

The limits are considerable, and the authors acknowledge many of them. The study is described as preliminary. Only two of the objects are complete, so identifications rely partly on comparison with other finds. The origin of the clay, the way it was hardened and the nature of the red pigment have not yet been worked out, and age estimates rest on a comparison with modern fingerprints. Nobody can say whether the children were playing, practising a craft or learning beliefs, and phrases such as art lessons would read too much into the evidence.

None of this makes the finding trivial. It adds one piece of the puzzle to a larger picture in which skills, symbols and knowledge were handed down across generations in the first villages. The children left their mark, literally, and the careful response is neither to dismiss it nor to invent a classroom around it. Which of your own skills did you learn by watching, and how would an archaeologist ever know?

=== Activity ===

Work with a partner. Imagine you are archaeologists and you have found a small clay object with two different fingerprints on it. On paper, write down three things you can say for certain, and three things you can only guess. Then compare your lists with another pair and decide which guess you would most like to test. No clay, real fingerprints or photos are needed, and nobody has to share anything personal.
