title: Fewer Screens, More Pens: Australia's School Plan
summary: Australia's ministers want less screen time and less AI in school. But experts say the evidence is mixed.

Imagine a school day with fewer screens and more pens and paper. In November 2026, Australia's education ministers agreed to move that way. They call the plan a "digital detox".

At a meeting on 2 November, the ministers agreed that technology in schools should have a clear purpose, and that students' use of it should be limited if it does not help them learn or look after their wellbeing. They agreed to bring in, try out or think about limits on screen time. They also agreed to restrict how students use generative AI, which means tools that can write text or answer questions for you, in primary schools and in secondary school assessments.

There is also a change to a national test. NAPLAN is a set of national tests, now taken on a computer. Ministers agreed that the Year 5 writing test will go back to handwriting in 2028. But this is not final. It depends on advice from ACARA, the national agency that looks after the tests, about whether it is possible and how much it would cost.

Why are they doing this? There are growing worries that AI could harm children's concentration and critical thinking. The Education Minister, Jason Clare, said he is not arguing that schools should throw their tablets away, but that there is a good case for more pen and paper and less technology.

Not everyone thinks the answer is simple. In an article shared by ABC, Professor Jason Lodge of the University of Queensland says the evidence is mixed. International test results suggest that 15-year-olds who use devices a moderate amount for learning do better than heavy users and than students who never use them. But very few students now go without technology, so that comparison may not tell us much. Yet technology can help when it is used for a specific job, such as extra practice or quick feedback.

The government is not suggesting a total ban. Professor Lodge says the plan is a step in the right direction, but he says more planning and information are needed. Students will use AI in their future jobs, so schools must still prepare them. Some people will say the plan is simply back to basics. Others will worry that schools will throw the baby out with the bathwater.

=== Intermediate ===

Many students spend part of every school day looking at a screen. In November 2026, Australia's education ministers decided that this may have gone too far. After a meeting on 2 November, they announced what they call a "digital detox" in schools.

The ministers agreed that technology should be used only when it has a clear purpose and supports learning and wellbeing, and that students' use should be restricted when it does not. They agreed to introduce, trial or consider limits on screen use, and to restrict student use of generative AI in primary schools and in secondary school assessments. Each state, territory and school sector will decide the details, which matters in a country where the rules have been patchy. New South Wales and Victoria have already flagged screen limits, and Western Australia will soon pilot them.

The most concrete change concerns NAPLAN, the national tests that Australian students take in several year levels. Year 5 writing is currently completed on a computer. Ministers agreed that it will return to handwriting in 2028, but only subject to advice from the testing agency ACARA on whether this is practical, how much it would cost and what it would mean for teachers' workload. In other words, the 2028 date is a plan, not a promise. A new taskforce will advise on what else could be done.

The motivation is partly worry about AI. According to the report discussed by ABC, there are growing concerns that AI could harm children's critical thinking, concentration and problem-solving skills. Education Minister Jason Clare said he was not arguing for tablets to be thrown away, but that there was a strong case for more pen and paper and less technology.

Professor Jason Lodge of the University of Queensland, whose analysis ABC published, argues that the evidence is more complicated. Recent international test results suggest that 15-year-olds who use devices moderately for learning do better than both heavy users and non-users, but because so few students now go without technology, critics say the comparison is not very useful. A 2015 OECD analysis found that countries that invested heavily in school computers saw no appreciable improvement in reading, maths or science scores. Yet technology can help when it is used for something specific, such as targeted practice or precise feedback.

A complete ban is not what the government is suggesting, and there is still no comprehensive national data on how much time Australian students spend on devices at school. Lodge calls the new report a step in the right direction but says more planning and information are needed. Students will meet AI as soon as they leave the school gates, he points out, so schools still have to prepare them. Whether the detox turns out to be a sensible return to basics or a case of throwing the baby out with the bathwater is, for now, up in the air.

=== Advanced ===

title: Is Australia's Digital Detox Backed by the Evidence?
summary: Ministers want fewer screens and tighter limits on AI in schools. Experts say the research is thinner and more mixed than the policy suggests.

For decades the direction of travel in Australian classrooms was one-way: more laptops, more tablets, more software. On 2 November 2026 the country's education ministers signalled a change of course. They agreed on what they describe as a "digital detox", which Education Minister Jason Clare has summarised as more pen and paper and less technology.

The ministers' communique sets out the principle: technology and AI in schools should be evidence-based, purposeful and good for learning and wellbeing, and student use should be restricted when it fails those tests. They agreed to introduce, trial or consider limits on screen use, and to restrict student use of generative AI in primary schools and in secondary assessments, with states, territories and school sectors deciding how to implement the changes. That flexibility matters, because the rules have been patchy and are often settled school by school.

The most specific commitment concerns NAPLAN, the national literacy and numeracy tests. Ministers agreed that Year 5 writing will return to handwritten completion in 2028, but they made this subject to further advice from ACARA on feasibility, costs and workload. Anyone who reads that the tests are definitely going back to paper in 2028 is therefore reading slightly ahead of the evidence. A new taskforce will advise on further action, and ABC says it is due to report to ministers three times in 2027.

The decision comes amid a mixture of old and new worries. Professor Jason Lodge of the University of Queensland, whose analysis ABC published, writes that purchasing decisions are often based on pilots and word of mouth, and that a 2015 OECD analysis found no appreciable gains in reading, maths or science scores in countries that invested heavily in school computers. More recently, attention has shifted to generative AI. The report behind the announcement raises concerns that AI could harm critical thinking, concentration and problem-solving, and commentators have warned of "cognitive stunting", in which repeated reliance on a tool weakens thinking skills.

Yet the evidence does not point cleanly in one direction. Recent international results suggest that 15-year-olds who use devices moderately for learning do better than both heavy users and non-users, although critics argue that, with so few students now going without technology, the comparison with non-users says little. Lodge adds that technology used for a specific purpose, such as targeted practice or precise feedback, can improve learning, and that there is no comprehensive national data on how much time Australian students spend on devices at school. In that sense, the policy is being built on incomplete information.

Lodge describes the report as a step in the right direction but not a complete answer. A blanket ban is not what is being proposed, and students will meet AI the moment they leave school, so schools must still prepare them. His advice is to invest in teachers and to use tools designed for learning, as Singapore and Estonia have done, rather than simply to churn out answers. Whether Australia manages to strike a balance, or finds that the devil is in the detail, will become clearer once the taskforce has reported.
