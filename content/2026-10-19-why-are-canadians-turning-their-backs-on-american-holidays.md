=== Core ===

For many Canadians, taking a holiday in the United States has always been simple. They can drive across the border to shop, visit friends or spend a few days in another city. Some older Canadians also travel south during winter to escape the cold.

But during 2025 and 2026, fewer Canadians chose to make these journeys. Relations between the two countries became more difficult because of political arguments and trade disputes. Some Canadian travellers said they no longer felt welcome or did not want to spend their money in the United States.

This created problems for American businesses. Hotels, restaurants and shops in areas that usually welcome Canadian visitors began to feel the effects. Tourist organisations tried to attract visitors back with discounts and special offers.

Not every Canadian made the same decision. Some people continued travelling south for work, family or holidays. Others chose destinations such as Mexico or decided to travel within Canada instead. The reasons behind a travel decision can also include the exchange rate, prices and personal safety.

The disagreement raises a question about whether tourists should be influenced by politicians. A hotel worker in an American city may have nothing to do with a government decision, but that worker can still lose income if international visitors stay away.

Travel is usually described as a way to bring people from different cultures together. Yet it can also become a way of expressing disagreement. When someone cancels a holiday for political reasons, is that a useful protest, or does it mainly hurt ordinary people who depend on tourism?

=== Intermediate ===

Canadians have traditionally been among the United States' most important international visitors. A short drive across the border can lead to a shopping trip, a weekend away or an extended winter holiday. Yet political tensions and trade disputes have encouraged some travellers to reconsider a journey that once felt entirely routine.

Reporting in September 2026 described American tourism organisations trying to persuade Canadians to return. Discounts and special promotions were offered in destinations dependent on cross-border spending. The response followed a decline in Canadian visits and concern about the money that hotels, shops and restaurants were losing.

The reasons for staying away are not identical for everyone. Some travellers object to political rhetoric or trade policies. Others worry about how they might be treated, while practical factors such as prices and currency exchange rates also affect their decisions. Some have chosen to spend their holidays within Canada or travel to other countries.

There are competing ways to interpret this behaviour. Avoiding a destination can be a peaceful form of political expression: consumers decide where their money goes. But the people who suffer first may be hotel employees, restaurant owners and small businesses rather than the politicians responsible for the tension.

The situation is especially complicated along a border where personal and economic connections have developed over generations. Many families, workers and businesses depend on regular travel. A disagreement between governments can therefore influence relationships that have little to do with national politics.

The tourism industry often promises escape from everyday problems, but holidays never take place in a political vacuum. Canada's changing travel patterns reveal how quickly ideas about hospitality, national identity and trust can affect a commercial relationship that previously seemed secure.

=== Advanced ===

Cross-border tourism between Canada and the United States has historically been sustained by more than attractive destinations. Geographic proximity, family connections and a sense of familiarity made American holidays a routine choice for millions of Canadians. Political friction has unsettled that relationship, demonstrating how fragile consumer confidence can become when travellers begin to question whether they feel welcome.

In September 2026, reporting described US tourism organisations launching promotions to attract Canadian visitors after a substantial decline in travel. Destinations accustomed to Canadian spending faced pressure on hotels, restaurants and retailers. The economic impact was particularly visible in businesses for which a large northern customer base had long been taken for granted.

The reasons behind the shift were varied. Some Canadians objected to political statements and trade measures; others cited discomfort, uncertainty or practical financial considerations. It would be inaccurate to attribute every cancelled trip to a single political cause. Exchange rates, costs and individual circumstances all influence international travel.

Nevertheless, the trend has the character of a consumer boycott, even where no formal movement is involved. Travellers can express dissatisfaction through their spending decisions without joining a demonstration. The difficulty is that the immediate effects are often felt by workers and businesses with little influence over national policy.

The situation also challenges the assumption that tourism naturally improves relations between neighbouring countries. Personal contact can build trust, but diplomatic hostility may prevent such contact from taking place. Economic interdependence does not guarantee that political relationships will remain stable, particularly when public sentiment becomes part of the dispute.

The Canadian response raises a wider ethical question. Individuals are entitled to choose destinations that match their values, yet travel decisions inevitably affect people who may disagree with their own government. The absence of a straightforward answer is precisely what makes consumer choice a complicated form of political expression.
