title: Why Some Learners Pick Up Pronunciation Faster
summary: A new study says that hearing small differences in sound may help people learn how to pronounce a new language.

Do you ever feel that your pronunciation in English is not as good as your grammar? Some learners seem to pick up new sounds quickly, while others work hard for years. A new study suggests that one reason may be how well a person hears small differences in sound.

Researchers from University College London (UCL) in the UK and Tohoku University in Japan studied 67 Japanese people who were learning English. First, they tested each person's auditory precision. This means how well you notice small changes in sounds, such as changes in pitch, length and tone. The sounds in this test were not speech, so it was not a language test.

Next, the learners had their brains scanned with a machine called an fMRI. While they were scanned, they tried to tell two English vowel sounds apart: the sound in "cat" and the sound in "cup". Neither sound matches a Japanese vowel directly. The study found that learners with better auditory precision learned pronunciation faster and more accurately than the others.

The scans showed a difference. When learners with better auditory precision listened to the new sounds, they used one part of the brain less. This part is called the left sensorimotor system, and it is involved in language. Their brain activity looked more like the activity for sounds that are not speech. The researchers think these learners were less affected by the patterns of their first language. Learners with lower auditory precision used that part of the brain more, and they seemed to hear the new sounds through the patterns of Japanese.

The lead researcher, Kazuya Saito-van Poeteren, thinks schools should do more than ask students to practise more. He believes they should also help learners train their ear. His team hopes to create a new kind of training, but nobody has shown yet that it works. That is easier said than done.

We should be careful. Only 67 people took part, and all of them were Japanese learners of English. The study looked at one pair of vowel sounds. It shows a link between a good ear and good pronunciation, but it does not show that listening is the only reason. What can you do this week to listen to English more carefully? A good ear may be just one piece of the puzzle.

Some language learners seem to pick up pronunciation almost effortlessly, while others practise for years and still struggle with certain sounds. Importantly, this was tested with non-speech sounds, so it was not a language test.

The scans suggest why. The researchers suggest that these learners were less affected by the patterns of their first language, so new sounds were easier to process. Learners with lower auditory precision relied more on that network and used the pronunciation patterns of Japanese to make sense of unfamiliar English sounds.

The lead author, Kazuya Saito-van Poeteren of the UCL Institute of Education, argues that language teaching should go beyond simply asking for more practice and should also develop learners' ears. He told The Guardian that he does not believe British people are biologically bad at languages. Instead, he thinks many people get too little meaningful exposure, and that a good ear matters most when you only meet a language for a few hours each week. The team hopes to create a new form of training that combines language and listening practice.

There are limits, though. The study had only 67 participants, all Japanese learners of English, and it focused on one pair of vowels. It shows a link between a good ear and good pronunciation. It does not show that listening is the only cause, and the researchers have a long way to go before they can say that training the ear works. Padraic Monaghan, a professor of cognition at Lancaster University who commented on the findings for The Guardian, said the study confirms how vital listening is, although learners differ in their sensitivity to sounds, at least in the early stages.

So is listening the secret? Perhaps it is one important piece of the puzzle.

title: Does a Good Ear Make a Good Accent?
summary: Researchers at UCL and Tohoku University found that sharp hearing for small sound differences went with faster pronunciation learning, but the study cannot say that ear training works.

Learning to speak a foreign language well is often described as a matter of effort: memorise the vocabulary, drill the grammar, practise until it sticks. A new study from University College London (UCL) and Tohoku University in Japan suggests that, for pronunciation at least, something more basic may also be involved, namely how finely a person can hear sound itself.

The researchers worked with 67 Japanese learners of English. They first measured each learner's auditory precision by testing how well the learner could detect slight differences in pitch, length and tone in non-speech sounds, so the measure was deliberately separate from language. They then carried out fMRI brain scans while the participants tried to distinguish two English vowels, the sound in "cat" and the sound in "cup", neither of which maps directly onto a Japanese vowel.

Learners with higher auditory precision mastered pronunciation faster and more accurately. Their brain activity also differed. They relied less on the left sensorimotor system, a network involved in language processing, when handling the foreign sounds, and their activity resembled the pattern seen when the brain processes non-speech sounds. The researchers interpret this as less interference from the patterns of the learners' first language, which made new sounds simpler to process accurately. Learners with lower auditory precision leaned more heavily on that network and handled the unfamiliar sounds through the pronunciation patterns of Japanese.

The lead author, Kazuya Saito-van Poeteren of the UCL Institute of Education, draws a practical conclusion: language teaching should move beyond an approach built on more practice and towards one that develops learners' ears. He told The Guardian that he does not think British people are biologically poor at languages. The more likely problem, he said, is that many people receive too little meaningful exposure, together with teaching that does not train the auditory skills needed to make the most of it. If you hear a language for only a few hours a week, a good ear may make a much larger difference. His team aims to develop a combined programme of language and auditory training.

That is a promising idea, but it is worth being precise about what has been shown. The sample was 67 people, all Japanese learners of English, and the brain data concern a single pair of vowels. The findings show that auditory precision is associated with better pronunciation learning. They do not show that it is the only factor, nor that training the ear will improve anyone's pronunciation, which is what the team still has to find out. Other influences, such as how much a learner hears and uses the language, may also matter, as the lead author himself suggests.

Padraic Monaghan, a professor of cognition at Lancaster University who commented on the findings for The Guardian, said the study confirms how vital listening is, but he added that learners vary in their sound sensitivity, at least in the early stages of hearing new sounds. In other words, listening matters for everybody, but not everyone starts on a level playing field.

=== Activity ===

Work in pairs. Write down five pairs of English words that sound similar, such as cat and cut, ship and sheep, or bat and bet. Take turns saying one word from a pair while your partner turns away and listens, then guesses which word it was. Count how many you each get right, and talk about which sounds were hardest to hear. Nobody needs to be recorded, and nobody has to share personal information.
