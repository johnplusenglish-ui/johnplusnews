title: Does Writing Make Language More Complex?
summary: Many people think writing makes language more complicated. A big new study found no sign that this is true for all languages.

Most of us believe that writing makes language more serious and more complicated. When you write, you can stop, think and change your words before anyone reads them. So it seems natural that written sentences are more complex. On the face of it, that makes sense. Many people also think that a language with a long written history is more advanced than a language that is only spoken.

A team of researchers wanted to get to the bottom of this idea. They work at the University of Zurich and the University of Passau, and they published their study in 2026 in the journal PNAS. Earlier studies had looked at only a few languages, and most of those languages were European. So the team carried out a much bigger check.

First, they looked at about 100,000 sentences, both spoken and written, from 30 different languages. They counted how many clauses each sentence had. A clause is a group of words with its own verb. They also looked at how deeply the clauses were placed inside each other. Second, they studied the grammar of 59 languages. They wanted to know whether a language grew more grammar rules after people began to write it down.

The answer was no, at least not as a general rule. Written sentences were not always more complex than spoken ones, and languages did not all become more complex once they got a writing system. In other words, the study found no universal pattern.

Does this mean that writing never changes a language? No. The study looked at one thing only: how sentences are built. It did not look at words or style, and it does not say that writing has no effect on any single language. One of the researchers thinks that culture may be the missing piece of the puzzle. In Western societies, for example, long and complex sentences are often linked with a high social position. But sentences can be very complex in any language.

Many people worry that social media and AI will damage language. The researchers say that new technology does not have to change the way sentences are built. However, this is their own opinion, and the study did not test it.

The study also gives us something fascinating to think about. The researchers say it invites us to think again before we judge a language that has no written tradition.

=== Intermediate ===

Most of us carry around a quiet assumption about writing. When you write, you can plan, revise and edit, so it feels obvious that written sentences should be more elaborate than the ones we speak. On the face of it, that seems like common sense. It has also led many people to believe that a language gains complexity when it gets a writing system, and that languages without one are somehow more basic.

A team from the University of Zurich and the University of Passau set out to test this. Their paper, which appeared in PNAS in 2026, is led by Carlo Meloni, with Chundra Cathcart and Balthasar Bickel as senior authors. Meloni points out that earlier studies supporting the idea looked at only a handful of languages, mostly from Europe. The new study casts a much wider net.

The researchers carried out two kinds of analysis. In the first, they examined around 100,000 spoken and written sentences from 30 languages. They counted the clauses in each sentence and measured how deeply those clauses were nested inside one another. In the second, they looked at the grammar of 59 languages to ask whether grammar had become more complicated after writing was introduced.

Neither test produced a consistent result. Written sentences were not always more complex than spoken ones, and languages did not systematically develop more grammatical structures once they were written down. The authors conclude that there is no universal effect of writing on how complex sentences are, either in everyday use or over the long history of a language.

It is worth being clear about what this does not show. The study measured one specific thing, the structure of sentences, so it says nothing about vocabulary, style or how writing changes the way people think. A finding of no universal effect also leaves room for effects in particular languages. The paper does not claim that writing makes no difference anywhere.

The authors suggest that social attitudes may matter more than the technology. Cathcart notes that Western society tends to link complex sentences with social standing, but across the world, sentences can be highly complex for any speaker in any setting. Meloni adds that many people wrongly assume languages become more complex when they get writing, and the release says this invites us to reconsider how we judge languages with no written tradition.

Bickel draws a parallel with today's worries about social media and artificial intelligence. If a technology as big as writing did not reshape sentence structure in any universal way, he suggests, newer tools may not either. That is the authors' extension of the result rather than something the study tested, so it deserves a grain of salt. Still, the finding is a useful reminder that a dramatic new tool does not always change a language as much as we fear.

title: The Written Word Was Supposed to Make Language Grander. The Evidence Is Not So Sure
summary: A study of around 100,000 sentences in 30 languages, plus grammar from 59, found no universal link between writing and structural complexity. The finding is narrower than the headlines it invites.

There is an old and flattering story about writing. Because a writer can plan, revise and edit, written sentences are supposed to become longer, more layered and more sophisticated than the improvised sentences of speech. By extension, a language that acquires a writing system is thought to acquire complexity too. It is an intuition so familiar that few people stop to question it, and it has quietly shaped how languages with no written tradition have been judged.

Researchers at the University of Zurich and the University of Passau decided to put it to the test. In a paper published in PNAS in 2026, Carlo Meloni and colleagues, with Chundra Cathcart and Balthasar Bickel as senior authors, point out that earlier work supporting the idea drew on only a handful of languages, mostly European ones. The new study widens the lens considerably.

It carries out two analyses. The first compares around 100,000 spoken and written sentences in 30 languages, measuring how many clauses each contains and how deeply they are nested. The second looks at grammatical rules in 59 languages, asking whether grammars grew more complex after writing arrived. In short, one analysis concerns how people use language today and the other concerns how grammars have evolved.

Neither produced a universal pattern. Written sentences were not consistently more complex than spoken ones, and languages did not systematically add grammatical structure after adopting writing. The distinction matters: the authors report no evidence for a universal effect, which is a good deal weaker than claiming that writing never matters. Their measure is also narrow. Clause counts and nesting depth capture one aspect of syntax, not vocabulary, rhetoric or the many other ways in which writing can alter how language is used.

What, then, drives complexity? The researchers point towards social factors, arguing that how people build sentences may depend more on cultural preferences and ideas about how language should be used than on writing itself. Cathcart observes that Western society strongly associates complex sentences with social standing, whereas a global view shows that sentences can be hugely complex in any setting and for any speaker. The release adds that the finding invites us to reconsider how we judge languages without a written tradition.

The authors then reach beyond their data. Bickel suggests that if writing, among the largest revolutions in how information is shared, did not universally change sentence structure, then social media and artificial intelligence may not either. This is an inference, not a result, and it should be taken with a pinch of salt. The study concerns writing, not the internet, and the jury is still out on what new technology does to vocabulary, tone and habits of expression.

Two practical cautions follow. The information here comes through the authors' own institutions, and the paper's print issue is dated 24 November 2026, after online release, so readers should check the final version. More importantly, a null result across 30 and 59 languages deserves respect, but it is the start of an argument and not the end of one.
