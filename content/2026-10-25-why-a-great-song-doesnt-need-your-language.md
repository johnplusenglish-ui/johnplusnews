=== Core ===

title: Can You Love a Song You Don't Understand?
summary: A music service said songs in 16 languages reached its world top 50 in 2025. Do you need the words to enjoy a song?

Think of a chorus you sing in the shower. Now imagine you don't know what the words mean. You only know the beat, the voice and the feeling it gives you. Is it still your song?

For many people, the answer is yes. In March 2026, the music service Spotify published a report about its own data. It said that in 2025, songs in 16 different languages reached its Global Top 50 chart, more than double the number in 2020. That is one company's figure about its own service, but it shows that the songs people choose can come from many different languages.

So why do people enjoy songs in languages they don't speak? There isn't one proven answer. Maybe it's the beat, the steady rhythm that makes you move. Maybe it's a voice that sounds happy, sad or strong. Maybe it's a chorus that is easy to sing, even if you only copy the sounds. Or maybe it's a friend who played it for you.

Meaning can come later. Some people look up a translation and love the song more. Others find the meaning disappointing and prefer the mystery. A good translation can help, but it can also lose jokes, rhyme and rhythm.

Try a test. Play a short part of a song in a language you don't know. Describe it in three words without translating anything. Is it fast or slow? Happy or sad? Then check the meaning and see if you were close.

Now it's your turn. Which song would you play for a friend in another country, and what would you tell them first?

=== Intermediate ===

title: Why a Great Song Doesn't Need Your Language
summary: Songs in 16 languages reached a streaming service's global top 50 in 2025. What makes a song travel, and what gets lost on the way?

Imagine you're at a party and a song comes on. Everybody knows the chorus except you. Nobody has ever explained the lyrics to you, but you're already nodding to the beat. By the end of the song you're humming the melody, and you still don't know what it's about.

That experience may be more common than it was a few years ago. In March 2026, Spotify published a report called Loud & Clear, based on its own streaming data. It said that songs in 16 languages reached its Global Top 50 in 2025, more than double the number in 2020. The report also named Brazilian funk, K-pop and reggaeton among the fastest-growing music styles on its service.

It's important to read that carefully. These are Spotify's figures about Spotify, and the language of a song doesn't tell us the nationality of the artist.

So why might a song work without its words? Nobody has a tested, universal answer, but listeners often point to the beat, the voice and the feeling of a chorus. A voice can sound playful or fragile before you understand a single word. A rhythm can make a whole room move. A simple chorus is easy to join, even if you're only copying the sounds. And a song often arrives attached to a friend, a video or a moment, so the memory travels with it.

Meaning matters too, of course. Many fans enjoy looking up a translation, and it can change how they hear a song. A cheerful tune might turn out to be about something sad. But translations aren't perfect. Rhymes, jokes and wordplay often don't survive the journey, and a version that sounds natural in your language may feel different from the original.

That raises a question for anyone who shares music. When you recommend a song in another language, do you explain the words first, or let your friend listen cold? Both choices change the experience, and neither is wrong.

So imagine you can send one song to a class in another country. Which would you pick, and what would you say before they press play?

=== Advanced ===

title: You Don't Know the Words. You Know the Feeling.
summary: You hear a chorus in a language you don't speak, and you replay it. In 2025, songs in 16 languages reached one service's Global Top 50.

There is a kind of listening that doesn't need a dictionary. You hear a chorus in a language you don't speak, and something in it, the way the singer leans on a word or the way the drums arrive a beat late, makes you replay it. Weeks later you can imitate the sounds perfectly and still have no idea what you are singing.

In March 2026, Spotify published its Loud & Clear report, drawn from its own data. In 2025, it said, songs in 16 languages reached the service's Global Top 50, more than double the number in 2020. The report also listed Brazilian funk, K-pop and reggaeton among the fastest-growing genres on its platform.

Those are Spotify's figures about Spotify, presented by a company with an interest in showing growth, and the report gives no exact count for 2020. The language of a song doesn't establish how well its listeners speak that language, and it certainly doesn't establish the artist's nationality. Nor does the chart say that lyrics have stopped mattering. It shows that a song can become popular far from where it began, not why it did.

A chart also counts plays, not love: a song can be played often because a friend sent it or because it suits the background, not because anyone adores it.

The reasons for a song's journey are probably several, and some of them are only suggestions. Rhythm and voice carry emotion before meaning arrives. Repetition makes a chorus easy to join, and joining in is a way of belonging. Sharing helps too, because a song often reaches you attached to a person, a video or a moment, and the memory travels with it. None of this has been tested here, but each explanation fits the way people describe their favourite songs.

Translation complicates the picture. Learning what the words mean can deepen a song or flatten it. A translated line may explain the story but lose the rhyme, the pun or the rhythm that made it work, and a version that sounds natural might change the register, turning slang into something polite. Some listeners deliberately avoid translations to keep the mystery; others consider that a missed opportunity. Neither group is simply right.

That makes a recommendation more interesting than it looks. If you share a song with someone in another country, do you explain it first, or let them hear it cold? A translation might reveal a joke you missed, or a story you did not expect. It can change a familiar song without changing a note. A song left untranslated keeps its mystery, and keeps its distance.
