title: Schoolies Adds Pickleball and a Ferris Wheel
summary: Australia's end-of-school celebration now has more daytime activities. But fewer young people are registering.

Finishing school is a big deal everywhere, but in Australia it comes with its own tradition. Every November, thousands of young people who have just finished their final exams travel to celebrate together. They are called school leavers, and the tradition is called schoolies. The biggest event is on the Gold Coast in Queensland, a city famous for its long beaches. In 2026, schoolies week runs from 21 to 27 November.

This year, the organisers are adding more daytime and free activities. These include a Ferris wheel, run clubs, line dancing and pickleball. Pickleball is a friendly, fast sport a bit like tennis, played with paddles and a light plastic ball on a small court. Sam O'Connor, the Queensland Youth Minister, said that the event has changed to suit young people's changing tastes.

One reason for the change is numbers. About 12,000 school leavers registered for the official Gold Coast celebrations in 2025, and ABC News reports that this number has gone down every year for five years. At its peak in the early 2000s, up to 50,000 young people came, according to Professor Daniel Gschwind of Griffith University.

So where do school leavers go instead? Some choose other places in Queensland, such as Noosa, Cairns and the Whitsundays. Others travel abroad, and Bali and Fiji are among the most popular choices. The Australian government advises them to check official travel advice, learn the local laws and have a plan in case something goes wrong. A charity called Red Frogs, which supports school leavers during the week, says it has seen an amazing shift in schoolies culture. More young people now come out early in the day and meet in cafes.

We should be careful about big conclusions. The 12,000 figure counts only official registrations on the Gold Coast. It does not count young people who celebrate in other places, at home or with family. And nobody can say yet whether pickleball and line dancing will bring more people back. Professor Gschwind believes schoolies will stay an important event, but pickleball may not be everyone's cup of tea.

With schoolies week now in full swing, it is a good moment to ask a question. How would you like to celebrate the end of school, and how would you let off steam after months of exams?

title: Is Schoolies Changing, or Just Shrinking?
summary: Queensland has added pickleball, line dancing and free daytime activities to Australia's biggest school-leaver celebration. The numbers show a decline, but not necessarily why.

Every November, Australian school leavers, young people who have just completed their final year of secondary school, head to holiday destinations to mark the end of their exams. The tradition is known as schoolies, and its best-known home is the Gold Coast in Queensland, where the 2026 week runs from 21 to 27 November.

This year's programme reflects a change of emphasis. ABC News reports that the Queensland government and organisers are offering more daytime and free activities, among them a state-funded Ferris wheel that is a first for the event, run clubs, line dancing and pickleball, a paddle sport that combines elements of tennis, badminton and table tennis. The Youth Minister, Sam O'Connor, said the event had evolved to suit young people's changing tastes and that the aim was to keep the Gold Coast as the home of schoolies. Meanwhile, one night in the beachfront Schoolies Hub, a fenced area that has long been free of alcohol and drugs, is being used as a trial for a paid, ticketed music event. The Gold Coast's mayor has said he strongly disagrees with charging school leavers.

The commercial and political background is a long decline in attendance. About 12,000 school leavers registered for official Gold Coast celebrations in 2025, and the number has fallen year on year for five years. Organisers estimated almost 20,000 for 2022, and Professor Daniel Gschwind of the Griffith Institute of Tourism says that at its peak in the early 2000s the event drew up to 50,000 young people.

The reports suggest that the celebration has partly spread out rather than disappeared. Professor Gschwind says that many young people now choose other Queensland destinations, such as Noosa, Cairns and the Whitsundays, and thousands more are expected to travel overseas, notably to Bali and Fiji. Australia's foreign affairs department reminds travellers to check official advice, understand local laws and plan for emergencies. Red Frogs, a charity that supports school leavers, describes an amazing shift in schoolies culture, with more young people coming out early in the day and fewer late-night activities.

It would be wise not to read too much into any of this. The registration figure covers only official celebrations on the Gold Coast, so it says nothing about young people who celebrate elsewhere, at home or not at all. The claim that tastes are changing also comes from a minister and a charity describing what they see, not from a survey of school leavers, and the reports do not establish why numbers have fallen or whether pickleball will bring them back. One event, however well documented, cannot tell us what a whole generation prefers.

Professor Gschwind is nevertheless relaxed about the future. He believes that Gold Coast schoolies will remain an iconic event and an important part of the tourism industry, even if it looks very different from the crowds of the early 2000s. The jury is still out on whether a Ferris wheel and a pickleball court are a sign of the times or simply a clever way to draw people back, and next year's registration figures may begin to settle it.
