=== Core ===

A plate can be much more than a plate. It can show a flower, an old pattern or a picture that people in a country have used for hundreds of years. In Ukraine, some designers are using everyday objects to help keep their culture alive.

One example is GUNIA Project, a design company based in Kyiv. It makes clothes, jewellery, candles, plates and other objects. Many of its designs come from traditional Ukrainian art and craft. The company does not simply copy old objects. It works with artists to create new pieces that fit modern homes and lives.

Some skills are very difficult to learn. Glassblowing, weaving, embroidery and making ceramics can take years of practice. GUNIA says it works with more than 150 craftspeople across Ukraine. Buying handmade products can help these workers continue using and teaching their skills.

Since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, protecting Ukrainian culture has become especially important. War can damage museums, destroy buildings and force families to leave their homes. It can also make it harder for small workshops to find materials or customers.

Not everyone can afford expensive handmade products, and a fashion brand cannot protect a whole country's history on its own. But a successful design company can introduce younger buyers to traditions they might otherwise never notice. A pattern that once appeared on a traditional coat might now appear on a modern cushion or cup.

Culture is not only something we find in museums or history books. It can be part of what we wear, use and give as gifts. Sometimes the most ordinary objects can help people remember who they are.

=== Intermediate ===

How can a ceramic plate or a hand-embroidered shirt become part of a country's cultural survival? In Ukraine, designers are answering that question by transforming traditional crafts into modern products. One of the best-known examples is GUNIA Project, a Kyiv-based company working with artisans from across the country.

The brand creates clothing, jewellery, tableware and decorative objects inspired by historical designs. Rather than producing exact copies of museum pieces, its designers research folk traditions and adapt their patterns, materials and techniques for contemporary customers. A familiar symbol might appear on an unusual candle, while traditional embroidery may become part of a modern garment.

The work depends on craftspeople whose knowledge cannot easily be replaced by machines. GUNIA says it collaborates with more than 150 artisans using techniques including ceramics, weaving and blown glass. Each handmade object represents not only a finished product, but also time spent learning a craft and passing it from one generation to the next.

After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the idea of cultural preservation acquired greater urgency. Military attacks and displacement have placed historic places and traditional livelihoods under pressure. For designers, continuing to produce objects rooted in Ukrainian culture can be both a business decision and a statement about national identity.

There are tensions in this approach. Handmade products may be too expensive for many people, and selling cultural traditions as luxury goods can raise questions about who benefits. Designers also have to consider the difference between respectful reinterpretation and treating a community's heritage as decoration.

Even so, the project challenges a familiar assumption about tradition. Preserving culture does not always mean keeping old objects untouched behind glass. It can also mean giving a skill new customers, new uses and a reason to survive.

=== Advanced ===

Cultural preservation often conjures up archives, museums and painstaking restoration work. Yet in Ukraine, some of the most visible attempts to sustain traditional craftsmanship are taking place in the commercial world of design. Kyiv-based GUNIA Project has built a business around reinterpreting inherited artistic practices as contemporary clothing, ceramics and household objects.

The company's approach begins with research into historical motifs and techniques, followed by collaborations with working craftspeople. According to GUNIA, its production network includes more than 150 artisans across Ukraine and draws on disciplines ranging from embroidery and weaving to blown glass and fine ceramics. A traditional image, removed from its original setting, is given a new function without necessarily losing its cultural associations.

Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 has made such work particularly resonant. Cultural heritage can be endangered not only when buildings or artefacts are destroyed, but also when artisans are displaced, local markets collapse or specialist knowledge ceases to be economically viable. In these circumstances, commercial demand can provide a reason for skilled workers to continue their practice.

The relationship between commerce and heritage remains uneasy, however. Luxury positioning may narrow the audience for objects presented as national culture, while reinterpretation risks turning complex traditions into attractive motifs stripped of context. Supporters argue that the alternative, allowing valuable techniques to disappear from ordinary use, would be a greater loss.

What distinguishes this kind of design from simple nostalgia is its refusal to treat tradition as something fixed. Techniques survive partly because they are adapted. A woven textile need not retain its original social function to preserve the knowledge required to make it, and a new buyer may discover a much older history through an object intended for everyday use.

Ukraine's experience offers a broader lesson: a culture is not only a collection of irreplaceable monuments. It is also a network of people who know how to make things, teach others and invest familiar symbols with contemporary meaning. Preserving that network may require creativity as well as conservation.
