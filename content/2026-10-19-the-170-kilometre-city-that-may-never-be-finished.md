=== Core ===

Imagine a city that is 170 kilometres long but only a short distance across. Its buildings have shiny outside walls, and people travel through it without needing ordinary cars. This was the idea behind The Line, one of Saudi Arabia's most famous building projects.

The Line was introduced as part of NEOM, a large plan to develop an area in the north-west of the country. Designers imagined a new way of life, with homes, shops and workplaces close together. Fast transport would take residents from one part of the city to another.

The pictures of the planned city looked like something from a science-fiction film. Supporters believed the project could show the world a different way to build cities. They also hoped it would attract workers, tourists and businesses while helping Saudi Arabia develop industries beyond oil.

But turning a beautiful picture into a real city is extremely difficult. Builders need huge quantities of materials, electricity and water. The desert creates extra challenges, and every part of the project has to be connected to services that work every day.

In May 2026, Semafor reported that major work on The Line had been delayed until after 2030. That did not mean the entire project had officially been cancelled. However, it raised new questions about whether the original 170-kilometre plan would ever be completed as announced.

The story is about more than one country or one expensive project. It asks what should happen when an exciting idea becomes too costly or complicated. Is it better to make a dream smaller, or keep trying to build something nobody has ever built before?

=== Intermediate ===

Saudi Arabia once presented The Line as a radically different answer to urban life. Instead of growing outwards like an ordinary city, it would stretch for 170 kilometres through the desert, with homes and services stacked into a narrow, high-density structure. Promotional images showed two enormous mirrored walls cutting across the landscape.

The Line forms part of NEOM, a development project intended to attract international investment and support Saudi Arabia's effort to diversify its economy. Its promoters imagined neighbourhoods connected by rapid transport, with daily necessities within easy reach and far less dependence on private cars.

The promise was extraordinary, but so were the practical obstacles. A city on this scale would require vast amounts of construction material, energy and water, as well as complicated transport and infrastructure systems. Creating comfortable public spaces inside such an unusual structure presents challenges that cannot be solved by dramatic computer images alone.

In May 2026, Semafor reported that further work on The Line had been put back until after 2030 as Saudi authorities reconsidered spending priorities. The report did not establish that every element of the plan had been permanently abandoned. It did, however, suggest a widening gap between the early promotional vision and what might be achievable in the near future.

Large projects often change as costs rise and governments face competing demands. Supporters argue that ambitious experiments can encourage innovation even if their original scale is reduced. Critics question whether the money would be better invested in housing, public transport and infrastructure that communities can use sooner.

The Line is a particularly dramatic example of a familiar dilemma. An impressive design can persuade people that the future is already visible. Construction forces them to confront less glamorous questions: how much will it cost, who will live there and what happens if the financial plan no longer works?

=== Advanced ===

Few proposed cities have captured the tension between architectural imagination and economic reality as vividly as The Line. Announced as part of Saudi Arabia's NEOM development, it envisaged a linear urban settlement extending 170 kilometres through the desert, enclosed within mirrored structures and designed to function with minimal reliance on conventional roads.

The proposal was inseparable from a broader national ambition: reducing economic dependence on oil by attracting investment, tourism and high-value industries. Its striking visual identity helped turn an infrastructure plan into an international symbol of technological optimism. Yet the same unprecedented scale that made the project compelling also complicated any credible assessment of its feasibility.

A continuous high-density settlement would need integrated transport, water, energy and waste systems capable of functioning reliably in an extreme environment. Construction costs, labour requirements and the environmental consequences of the materials involved create further difficulties. Even if a concept is technically possible, it must eventually answer the more ordinary question of whether people would choose to live there.

In May 2026, Semafor reported that further work on The Line had been delayed until after 2030, amid a shift in Saudi investment priorities. That report should not be confused with an official declaration that the project has been permanently cancelled. It does, however, illustrate the uncertainty surrounding the original timetable and the difference between announced ambitions and confirmed delivery.

There are competing interpretations. Advocates of experimental urban design argue that genuinely new solutions require risks and that scaled-down projects may still generate useful innovations. Sceptics counter that spectacular promises can conceal difficult trade-offs, particularly when public resources might otherwise improve existing cities.

Ultimately, The Line raises a question of political as well as architectural judgement. Should a government be praised for pursuing an extraordinary vision, or criticised if the vision proves financially unsustainable? Perhaps the real test of a futuristic city is not how impressive it looks in a rendering, but how responsibly its promises survive contact with reality.
