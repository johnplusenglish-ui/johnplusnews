title: The Plan for a City in a Straight Line
summary: Saudi Arabia planned a city 170 kilometres long. In May 2026, a report said the main work had been put off until after 2030.

Imagine a city that is 170 kilometres long but only a short distance across. Its buildings have shiny outside walls, and people travel from one end to the other without needing ordinary cars. This was the idea behind The Line, one of Saudi Arabia's most famous building projects, and for a while it was everywhere online.

The Line was introduced as part of NEOM, a large plan to develop an area in the north-west of the country. Designers imagined a completely new way of life, with homes, shops and workplaces close together. Fast transport would carry residents from one part of the city to another in minutes, so nobody would spend an hour stuck in traffic.

The pictures of the planned city looked like something from a science-fiction film. Supporters believed the project could show the world a different way to build cities. They also hoped it would attract workers, tourists and businesses, and help Saudi Arabia build up industries that do not depend on oil.

But turning a beautiful picture into a real city is extremely difficult. Builders need huge quantities of materials, electricity and water. The desert creates extra challenges, and every part of the project has to be connected to services that work every single day, not just on the day the photographs are taken. Nobody has built a city of this size in a straight line before, so there are no older projects to copy and learn from.

In May 2026, Semafor reported that major work on The Line had been put off until after 2030. That did not mean the entire project had officially been cancelled. However, it raised new questions about whether the original 170-kilometre plan would ever be finished in the way it was announced.

The story is about more than one country or one expensive project. It asks what should happen when an exciting idea turns out to be too costly or too complicated. Costs can also go up while the work goes on, so a plan that looked affordable at the start may become impossible later. Is it better to make a dream smaller, or to keep trying to build something nobody has ever built before?

=== Intermediate ===

title: When the Pictures Are Easier Than the Building
summary: The Line promised mirrored walls, rapid transport and homes within easy reach. Semafor reported in May 2026 that further work would wait until after 2030.

Saudi Arabia once presented The Line as a radically different answer to urban life. Instead of spreading outwards like an ordinary city, it would stretch for 170 kilometres through the desert, with homes and services stacked into a narrow, high-density structure. Promotional images showed two enormous mirrored walls cutting across the landscape, and they travelled around the world within days.

The Line forms part of NEOM, a development project intended to attract international investment and support Saudi Arabia's effort to diversify its economy away from oil. Its promoters imagined neighbourhoods linked by rapid transport, with daily necessities within easy reach and far less dependence on private cars. On paper, it solved problems that older cities have struggled with for a century.

The promise was extraordinary, but so were the practical obstacles. A city on this scale would call for vast amounts of construction material, energy and water, as well as complicated transport and infrastructure systems. Creating comfortable public spaces inside such an unusual structure presents challenges that cannot be solved by dramatic computer images alone, and the desert environment makes every one of them harder. There is no existing city of this shape to learn from either, so even routine engineering decisions have to be worked out from scratch.

In May 2026, Semafor reported that further work on The Line had been put back until after 2030 as Saudi authorities reconsidered their spending priorities. The report did not establish that every element of the plan had been permanently abandoned. It did, however, point to a widening gap between the early promotional vision and what might realistically get off the ground in the near future. Governments rarely announce a change of heart loudly, which is why a reported delay can say more than an official statement.

Large projects often change as costs rise and governments face competing demands on their budgets. Supporters argue that ambitious experiments can drive innovation even when their original scale is scaled back, and that a few useful ideas may survive. Critics question whether the money would be better invested in housing, public transport and infrastructure that communities can actually use within a decade. There is also a question of timing, because a project that slips by ten years loses the technology and much of the enthusiasm it was designed around.

The Line is a particularly dramatic example of a familiar dilemma. An impressive design can persuade people that the future is already visible and more or less paid for. Construction forces them to confront far less glamorous questions: how much will it cost, who will live there, and what happens if the financial plan no longer adds up?

=== Advanced ===

title: What Happened to the 170-Kilometre City?
summary: A postponement is not a cancellation. But a plan put off for a decade comes back into a world whose costs, technologies and priorities have all moved on.

A city 170 kilometres long, built in a straight line through the Saudi desert: The Line was designed to make people look twice. The NEOM proposal promised mirrored buildings and little need for conventional roads. Turning that image into a place where people could live would demand something less spectacular to show: transport, water, power and a workable budget.

The proposal was inseparable from a broader national ambition: reducing economic dependence on oil by attracting investment, tourism and high-value industries. Its striking visual identity helped turn an infrastructure plan into an international symbol of technological optimism, which was itself a considerable achievement. Yet the same unprecedented scale that made the project so compelling also complicated any credible assessment of whether it could be delivered.

A city in a line would need transport, water, energy and waste systems that work reliably in an extreme environment, where failure is rarely a minor inconvenience. Construction costs and labour requirements add further difficulties, none of which can be resolved by enthusiasm alone. Even where a concept is technically possible, it must still answer a more ordinary question: would people choose to live there, and on what terms?

In May 2026, Semafor reported that further work on The Line had been put on hold until after 2030, amid a shift in Saudi investment priorities. That report should not be confused with an official declaration that the project has been permanently cancelled. It does, however, illustrate the uncertainty surrounding the original timetable and the persistent gap between announced ambitions and confirmed delivery, a gap that is hardly unique to Saudi Arabia. Megaprojects elsewhere have been quietly pared back in much the same way, long after the launch films stopped circulating.

There are competing interpretations. Advocates of experimental design argue that new solutions need risk, and that even a scaled-back project can produce useful ideas. Sceptics reply that spectacular promises hide difficult trade-offs, especially where public money could improve cities that already exist. Timing matters too: a scheme deferred by a decade re-emerges into a world whose technologies, costs and priorities have all moved on.

Ultimately, The Line raises a question of political as well as architectural judgement. Should a government be praised for pursuing an extraordinary vision, or held to account if that vision proves financially unsustainable? Perhaps the real test of a futuristic city is not how impressive it looks in a rendering, but how responsibly its promises are revised once they come into contact with reality, and how honestly that revision is explained to the people paying for it.
